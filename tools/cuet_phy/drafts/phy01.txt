@@ P1|Conductors and equipotential surfaces — which hold|m|s|B|conductor field zero,equipotential perpendicular
Consider the following statements:
(A) Inside a charged conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field is zero.
(B) Electric field lines are perpendicular to an equipotential surface at every point.
(C) The electric potential inside a charged hollow conducting sphere is zero.
(D) No work is done in moving a charge along an equipotential surface.
Which of the statements given above are correct?
A) (A) and (B) only
B) (A), (B) and (D) only
C) (B), (C) and (D) only
D) (A), (B), (C) and (D)
=
Why: (A) free charges move until the field inside vanishes. (B) a field component along the surface would make the potential change along it. (D) \(W = q\Delta V = 0\) when \(\Delta V = 0\).
Why not the others: (C) is false: the field inside is zero, so the potential inside is constant and equal to its value on the surface, \(\dfrac{kQ}{R}\), not zero. Options (C) and (D) include (C); (A) leaves out (D).
Key point: Zero field means constant potential, not zero potential.

@@ P1|Coulomb force between 2 μC and 3 μC at 30 cm|e|n|A|coulomb force two charges
The force between point charges of \(2\ \mu\text{C}\) and \(3\ \mu\text{C}\) placed 30 cm apart in air is:
A) \(0.6\) N
B) \(0.18\) N
C) \(6\) N
D) \(1.8\) N
=
Given: \(q_1 = 2 \times 10^{-6}\) C, \(q_2 = 3 \times 10^{-6}\) C, \(r = 0.3\) m
Formula: \(F = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r^2}\)
Step 1: \(kq_1q_2 = 9 \times 10^9 \times 6 \times 10^{-12} = 5.4 \times 10^{-2}\)
Step 2: \(F = \dfrac{5.4 \times 10^{-2}}{0.09} = 0.6\) N
Common mistake: \(0.18\) N (B) divides by \(r\) instead of \(r^2\).
Key point: Coulomb's law is an inverse-square law.

@@ P1|Point of zero force between +4q and +q|m|n|C|null point two like charges
Charges \(+4q\) and \(+q\) are fixed 3 m apart. A third charge feels no net force when placed on the line joining them at a distance from \(+4q\) of:
A) 1 m
B) 1.5 m
C) 2 m
D) 2.25 m
=
Given: let the point be \(x\) from \(+4q\), so \(3 - x\) from \(+q\)
Formula: equal forces: \(\dfrac{4q}{x^2} = \dfrac{q}{(3 - x)^2}\)
Step 1: Take square roots: \(\dfrac{2}{x} = \dfrac{1}{3 - x}\)
Step 2: \(6 - 2x = x \Rightarrow x = 2\) m
Common mistake: 1 m (A) puts the point nearer the larger charge; the null point of two like charges lies nearer the smaller one.
Key point: For like charges the null point is between them, closer to the smaller charge.

@@ P1|Charge after filling a connected capacitor with a dielectric|m|n|D|dielectric battery connected charge

D) \(100\ \mu\text{C}\)
=

@@ P1|Energy lost when a charged capacitor shares charge|h|n|A|capacitor sharing energy loss
A \(4\ \mu\text{F}\) capacitor charged to 50 V is disconnected and joined across an uncharged \(6\ \mu\text{F}\) capacitor. The energy lost in the process is:
A) 3 mJ
B) 2 mJ
C) 5 mJ
D) 1.5 mJ
=
Given: \(Q = 4 \times 10^{-6} \times 50 = 2 \times 10^{-4}\) C, shared by \(10\ \mu\text{F}\)
Formula: \(U = \dfrac{1}{2}CV^2\); common \(V = \dfrac{Q}{C_1 + C_2}\)
Step 1: \(U_i = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 10^{-6} \times 2500 = 5\) mJ
Step 2: \(V = \dfrac{2 \times 10^{-4}}{10^{-5}} = 20\) V; \(U_f = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 10^{-5} \times 400 = 2\) mJ

Common mistake: 2 mJ (B) is the final energy, not the loss. 5 mJ (C) is the initial energy.
Key point: Charge is conserved when capacitors share charge; energy is not (it is lost as heat and radiation).

@@ P1|Torque on a dipole at 30° to a uniform field|m|n|B|dipole torque uniform field

@@ P1|Field inside a charged thin spherical shell|e|d|C|shell field inside gauss
A thin spherical shell of radius \(R\) carries charge \(Q\) spread uniformly. The electric field at a point \(\dfrac{R}{2}\) from its centre is:
A) \(\dfrac{kQ}{R^2}\)
B) \(\dfrac{4kQ}{R^2}\)
C) zero
D) \(\dfrac{kQ}{2R^2}\)
=
Why: A Gaussian sphere of radius \(\dfrac{R}{2}\) encloses no charge, so by Gauss's theorem the flux, and by symmetry the field, is zero.
Why not the others: (A) is the field just outside the surface. (B) treats the charge as a point charge at the centre seen from \(\dfrac{R}{2}\). (D) has no basis.
Key point: All the charge of a shell lies outside any Gaussian surface drawn inside it.

@@ P2|Two unequal cells in parallel driving a resistor|h|n|B|cells parallel equivalent emf

@@ P2|Material whose resistivity falls as it is heated|m|d|C|semiconductor resistivity temperature
Which of these materials shows a decrease in resistivity when its temperature rises?
A) copper
B) nichrome
C) silicon
D) manganin
=
Why: In a semiconductor such as silicon, heating frees more charge carriers across the small band gap, and this outweighs the extra collisions, so resistivity falls (negative temperature coefficient).
Why not the others: Copper's resistivity rises with temperature. Nichrome and manganin are alloys whose resistivity rises only slightly, which is why they are used in heaters and standard resistors.
Key point: Metals: resistivity rises with temperature; semiconductors: it falls.
