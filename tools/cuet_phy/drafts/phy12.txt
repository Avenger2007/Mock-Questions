@@ P1|Which square arrangements cancel E, V or both|m|m|B|square charges centre e v match
Charges of equal size \(q\) are placed at the corners of a square as described in List-I. Match each with the field \(E\) and potential \(V\) at the centre in List-II.

List-I
(A) \(+q\) at all four corners
(B) \(+q\) and \(-q\) at alternate corners
(C) \(+q\) at the two top corners, \(-q\) at the two bottom corners
(D) \(+q\) at three corners, the fourth empty

List-II
(I) \(E \ne 0\), \(V = 0\)
(II) \(E = 0\), \(V \ne 0\)
(III) \(E \ne 0\), \(V \ne 0\)
(IV) \(E = 0\), \(V = 0\)
A) (A)-(II), (B)-(I), (C)-(IV), (D)-(III)
B) (A)-(II), (B)-(IV), (C)-(I), (D)-(III)
C) (A)-(IV), (B)-(II), (C)-(I), (D)-(III)
D) (A)-(II), (B)-(IV), (C)-(III), (D)-(I)
=
Step 1: (A) opposite fields cancel in pairs; potentials add to \(\dfrac{4kq}{r}\) (II)
Step 2: (B) opposite corners carry like charges, so fields cancel; potentials sum to zero (IV)
Step 3: (C) potentials cancel, but all four fields have a downward part (I)
Step 4: (D) the uncancelled charge leaves a field, and the potential is \(\dfrac{3kq}{r}\) (III)
Common mistake: Assuming \(E = 0\) wherever \(V = 0\); in (C) the potential vanishes but the field does not.
Key point: Fields add as vectors; potentials add as numbers.

@@ P1|Energy stored on an isolated sphere carrying 1 μC|m|n|D|isolated sphere stored energy
An isolated metal sphere of radius 9 cm carries a charge of \(1\ \mu\text{C}\). The energy stored in its field is:
A) 0.1 J
B) 0.025 J
C) \(5 \times 10^{-8}\) J
D) 0.05 J
=
Given: \(R = 0.09\) m, \(Q = 10^{-6}\) C
Formula: \(C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R\), \(U = \dfrac{Q^2}{2C}\)
Step 1: \(C = \dfrac{0.09}{9 \times 10^9} = 10^{-11}\) F
Step 2: \(U = \dfrac{10^{-12}}{2 \times 10^{-11}} = 0.05\) J
Common mistake: 0.1 J (A) drops the \(\dfrac{1}{2}\).
Key point: Treat an isolated sphere as a capacitor with \(C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R\).

@@ P1|Potential inside a charged shell with an opposite charge at its centre|h|n|A|shell plus centre charge potential
A thin spherical shell of radius 10 cm carries \(+2\) nC, and a point charge of \(-1\) nC is held at its centre. The potential at a point 5 cm from the centre is:
A) zero
B) \(-180\) V
C) \(+180\) V
D) \(+45\) V
=
Given: shell \(Q = 2 \times 10^{-9}\) C, \(R = 0.1\) m; centre charge \(q = -10^{-9}\) C; \(r = 0.05\) m
Formula: inside a shell its potential is constant at \(\dfrac{kQ}{R}\); add the point charge's \(\dfrac{kq}{r}\)
Step 1: Shell: \(\dfrac{9 \times 10^9 \times 2 \times 10^{-9}}{0.1} = +180\) V
Step 2: Centre charge: \(\dfrac{9 \times 10^9 \times (-10^{-9})}{0.05} = -180\) V; total \(= 0\)
Common mistake: \(-180\) V (B) leaves out the shell, assuming its zero field inside means zero potential.
Key point: A shell's field vanishes inside it, but its potential does not.

@@ P1|Capacitance of a balanced bridge of five capacitors|h|n|C|balanced capacitor bridge
Between points A and B, one path has \(2\ \mu\text{F}\) and \(4\ \mu\text{F}\) in series, and a parallel path has \(1\ \mu\text{F}\) and \(2\ \mu\text{F}\) in series. A \(5\ \mu\text{F}\) capacitor joins the two junction points. The capacitance between A and B is:
A) \(14\ \mu\text{F}\)
B) \(3.5\ \mu\text{F}\)
C) \(2\ \mu\text{F}\)
D) \(7\ \mu\text{F}\)
=
Given: upper path \(2, 4\); lower path \(1, 2\); bridge \(5\ \mu\text{F}\)
Formula: in series, voltage divides inversely as capacitance; equal junction potentials mean no charge on the bridge
Step 1: Upper junction: \(V_{2\mu F} = \dfrac{4}{6}V\); lower junction: \(V_{1\mu F} = \dfrac{2}{3}V\). These are equal, so the bridge carries no charge
Step 2: \(C = \dfrac{2 \times 4}{6} + \dfrac{1 \times 2}{3} = \dfrac{4}{3} + \dfrac{2}{3} = 2\ \mu\text{F}\)
Common mistake: \(7\ \mu\text{F}\) (D) adds the bridge capacitor in parallel; at balance it plays no part.
Key point: A balanced bridge lets you remove the middle element.

@@ P1|Work to turn a dipole from 60° to 180° in a field|m|n|B|dipole work 60 to 180
An electric dipole of moment \(2 \times 10^{-8}\) C m is in a uniform field of \(1 \times 10^4\) N C⁻¹. The work needed to turn it from \(60^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\) with the field is:
A) \(1 \times 10^{-4}\) J
B) \(3 \times 10^{-4}\) J
C) \(4 \times 10^{-4}\) J
D) \(1.73 \times 10^{-4}\) J
=
Given: \(pE = 2 \times 10^{-4}\) J
Formula: \(W = pE(\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2)\)
Step 1: \(\cos 60^\circ - \cos 180^\circ = 0.5 - (-1) = 1.5\)
Step 2: \(W = 2 \times 10^{-4} \times 1.5 = 3 \times 10^{-4}\) J
Common mistake: \(4 \times 10^{-4}\) J (C) turns it from \(0^\circ\), not from \(60^\circ\).
Key point: A dipole's energy is \(-pE\cos\theta\); the work is the change in it.

@@ P1|Flux through the curved face of a hemisphere in a uniform field|m|n|D|hemisphere flux uniform field

A) 12.6 N m² C⁻¹
B) zero
C) 3.14 N m² C⁻¹
D) 6.28 N m² C⁻¹
=
Given: \(R = 0.1\) m, \(E = 200\) N C⁻¹
Formula: the curved face and the flat base form a closed surface with no charge inside, so their fluxes are equal and opposite; the base flux is \(\pi R^2E\)
Step 1: \(\pi R^2 = 3.14 \times 0.01 = 0.0314\) m²

@@ P2|Where Ohm's law holds and fails — which hold|m|s|B|ohmic resistance constant current,diode non ohmic
Consider the following statements:
(A) A junction diode does not obey Ohm's law.
(B) For some devices the current depends on the sign of the applied voltage, not just its size.
(C) A metal wire at constant temperature obeys Ohm's law.
(D) An ohmic conductor at constant temperature has a resistance that rises with the current.
Which of the statements given above are correct?
A) (A) and (C) only
B) (A), (B) and (C) only
C) (B), (C) and (D) only
D) (A), (B), (C) and (D)
=
Why: (A) its \(V\)–\(I\) graph is curved. (B) a diode conducts one way only. (C) at fixed temperature \(V \propto I\).

Key point: Ohm's law is a property of some materials, not a universal law.

@@ P2|Power in four parts of one circuit in increasing order|m|q|D|power four parts circuit order

(B) the \(2\ \Omega\) resistor

B) (B) only
C) (C) only
D) (D) only
=
