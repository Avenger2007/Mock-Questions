%P W2
When the Second World War ended, the economies of Western Europe lay in ruins. The United States helped them recover through a large programme of aid known as the Marshall Plan, and in 1948 the Organisation for European Economic Cooperation was set up to channel this aid to the Western European states. In 1949 the Council of Europe was formed to promote political cooperation. Step by step, economic integration deepened, from a common market to a European Union with its own flag, anthem, founding date and currency. Not every member wanted to go so far: some, such as Denmark and Sweden, resisted the move to a single currency and kept their own. Economically the Union is a giant. Its share of world trade is far larger than that of the United States, and its currency, the euro, could one day challenge the dominance of the US dollar. It carries political and diplomatic weight too. France is a nuclear power and a permanent member of the UN Security Council, and the Union's combined armed forces are among the largest in the world. Yet the EU is not a single state. Its members keep their own foreign and defence policies and often disagree. When the United States invaded Iraq in 2003, some members joined the war while Germany and France opposed it.
