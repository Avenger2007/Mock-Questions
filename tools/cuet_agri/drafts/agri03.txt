# CUET Agriculture Test 3 — odd key 13/12/13/12. Passage: H3 (jam and jelly).

%P H3
Jam and jelly are among the oldest ways of keeping fruit for the months when it is out of season. Both depend on three things being present in the right balance: pectin, acid and sugar. Pectin is a natural substance found in the cell walls of fruit. Guava, apple and the peel of citrus fruits are rich in it, while fruits such as strawberry and cherry contain little. Acid helps the pectin to form a gel; most jellies set best when the fruit extract is quite acidic, at a pH of roughly 3.0 to 3.5. Sugar draws water away from the pectin so that its chains can join, and at high concentration it also stops microbes from growing. Jam is made by boiling the fruit pulp with sugar until the product is thick. Jelly is made only from the strained, clear juice, so a good jelly is transparent and holds its shape when turned out of the mould. Marmalade is a jelly in which thin shreds of citrus peel are suspended. The cook must judge the end point carefully. One way is the sheet test: when a little of the boiling mass is lifted on a ladle and allowed to fall, it should drop off as a sheet, not as a thin stream. Another way uses a thermometer: the mass is ready when it boils about 5 to 6 °C above the boiling point of water, which is 105 to 106 °C at sea level. The hot product is filled into clean, sterilised jars and sealed at once.
