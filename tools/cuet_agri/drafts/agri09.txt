%P H2
The mango (*Mangifera indica*), India's national fruit, belongs to the family Anacardiaceae. India grows more mangoes than any other country, and hundreds of varieties are known, from Alphonso on the Konkan coast to Dashehari and Langra in the north. Mango trees raised from seed are not true to type and take many years to bear, so orchards are planted with grafted plants. Veneer, softwood and stone grafting are the common methods. In traditional orchards the trees are spaced about 10 m × 10 m, but in high-density planting, now spreading fast, they are set as close as 5 m × 5 m and kept small by pruning. Many popular varieties, including Alphonso and Dashehari, bear heavily in one year and poorly in the next; this habit is called alternate or biennial bearing. To overcome it, breeders at IARI crossed Dashehari with Neelum and released Amrapali, a dwarf hybrid that bears regularly and suits high-density planting. Mango growers face several problems. Malformation turns the flower panicles into compact, sterile masses. The mango hopper sucks sap from the flowers and causes them to drop, while the fruit fly lays eggs in ripening fruit. In Alphonso, a disorder called spongy tissue leaves patches of sour, unripe pulp inside fruit that looks normal. For marketing, mature fruits are harvested with a small stalk, cleaned and packed; they may be ripened with controlled ethylene gas, while the use of calcium carbide for ripening is banned.
