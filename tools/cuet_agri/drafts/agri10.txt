Millets are small-seeded grasses that have been grown for grain since ancient times. At India's proposal, the United Nations declared 2023 the International Year of Millets, and in India millets were promoted as 'Shree Anna' and as 'nutri-cereals'. India is the world's largest producer of millets. They are grouped as major millets — sorghum (jowar), pearl millet (bajra) and finger millet (ragi) — and minor millets such as foxtail, kodo, little, proso and barnyard millet. Pearl millet is the most widely grown millet in India, and Rajasthan has the largest area under it. Millets are C₄ plants that use water efficiently. Most of them mature in 70–120 days and tolerate high temperature, drought and poor soils, so they suit rainfed drylands and need little fertilizer. A millet crop such as pearl millet needs only about 300 mm of water, while transplanted rice may need about 1,200 mm. Millets are rich in dietary fibre, in minerals such as iron and calcium, and in B vitamins. They are gluten-free and have a lower glycaemic index than polished rice, so they release sugar into the blood slowly. Their tiny seeds, hard seed coat and the short shelf life of their flour after milling have, however, limited their use, and better processing machines are being developed. Millet straw is also valued as fodder for animals.
