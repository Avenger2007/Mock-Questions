# CUET Agriculture Test 7 — odd key 13/12/13/12. Passage: C3 (drip and sprinkler irrigation).

%P C3
As groundwater tables fall, farmers are being urged to get "more crop per drop". Two methods of micro and pressurised irrigation help most. In drip irrigation, water flows through a network of plastic pipes and reaches each plant through small emitters, drop by drop, close to the roots. Very little water is lost to evaporation or deep percolation, so drip commonly saves 30 to 50 percent of water compared with flooding and keeps the space between rows dry, which also reduces weeds. Fertilizers dissolved in the water can be supplied through the same pipes; this practice is called fertigation. Drip suits widely spaced crops such as fruit trees, vegetables and sugarcane. Its main weakness is clogging: the tiny emitters are easily blocked by sand, algae or salts, so the water must pass through filters, and the lines are flushed regularly. A typical emitter delivers 2, 4 or 8 litres per hour. In sprinkler irrigation, water under pressure is sprayed into the air and falls on the crop like rain. Sprinklers work well on sandy soils, which lose water quickly under flooding, and on uneven land that would be costly to level. They suit close-growing crops such as wheat, groundnut and vegetables, and can even protect crops from frost. However, strong winds blow the spray off course, so sprinklers perform poorly in windy areas. Both systems cost more to install than surface methods, which is why governments offer subsidies under programmes such as "Per Drop More Crop".
