# CUET Agriculture Test 5 — odd key 13/12/13/12. Passage: C2 (Soil Health Card and INM).

%P C2
In 2015 the Government of India launched the Soil Health Card scheme, so that farmers would know what their soil actually needs before they buy fertilizer. Soil samples are drawn on a grid, roughly one sample for every 2.5 hectares in irrigated areas and one for every 10 hectares in rainfed areas, and tested in a laboratory. The card reports twelve parameters: the major nutrients nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium; the secondary nutrient sulphur; the micronutrients zinc, iron, copper, manganese and boron; and three general properties, namely pH, electrical conductivity and organic carbon. For each field it then recommends fertilizer doses and suggests organic manures where they are needed. The scheme was a response to a long-standing problem. Because urea has been much cheaper than phosphatic and potassic fertilizers, many farmers apply far more nitrogen than phosphorus and potassium, and the ratio of N to P₂O₅ to K₂O used in several states has drifted far from the ratio of roughly 4:2:1 that is often treated as balanced. Unbalanced fertilization raises costs, wastes nitrogen and slowly drains the soil of other nutrients. The card therefore promotes integrated nutrient management. In this approach, chemical fertilizers that supply nutrients quickly are combined with farmyard manure, compost, green manures and biofertilizers. The organic sources add humus, improve soil structure and feed soil microbes, while the fertilizers meet the crop's peak demand. Used together, they keep yields high without running down the soil.
