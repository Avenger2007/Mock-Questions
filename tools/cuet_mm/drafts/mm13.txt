For a film club screening, Ananya prepares a talk on how cinema began. She starts in Paris on 28 December 1895, when the Lumière brothers showed a set of short films to a paying audience at the Grand Café. Each film was under a minute long and was shot with a fixed camera. They showed everyday scenes just as they happened, such as workers leaving a factory and a train pulling into a station. The Lumières' Cinématographe could record, print and project films. Within a year, Lumière operators had screened films in Bombay.

She then turns to Robert Flaherty, who lived for months with an Inuit family in the Canadian Arctic before completing Nanook of the North (1922). The film follows Nanook and his family through their daily struggle to survive, but some scenes, such as an old-style hunt, were staged for the camera.

In 1926 the Scottish film-maker John Grierson, reviewing Flaherty's next film, Moana, used the word "documentary". He later defined documentary as "the creative treatment of actuality". Grierson went on to lead government film units in Britain that made films for public education.
