On 27 August 1947, B. Pocker Bahadur of Madras asked the Constituent Assembly to keep separate electorates. Minorities, he argued, exist in every country and cannot be wished away. They need a voice in the political life of the nation that is truly their own, and only a system in which they choose their own representatives can give them that voice.

Most nationalist members reacted sharply. They recalled that separate electorates had been brought in by the colonial government, and they blamed the system for the communal bitterness that had ended in Partition. Sardar Patel described it as a poison that had entered the body politic of the country.

Govind Ballabh Pant went further. He argued that the demand was not only harmful to the nation but suicidal for the minorities themselves. Separate electorates, he said, would isolate them permanently, stop them from winning the goodwill of others, and leave them without real influence in government. Citizens had to be built into one nation, not divided into separate compartments.

Separate electorates were rejected. By 1949 most Muslim members of the Assembly had also come to agree that the system worked against the interests of the minorities.
