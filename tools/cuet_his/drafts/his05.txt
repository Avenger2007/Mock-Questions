In 1856 the East India Company took over Awadh, claiming that the kingdom was being misgoverned. Nawab Wajid Ali Shah was dethroned and sent away to Calcutta. He was widely loved, and many people followed him on his journey, singing songs of grief.

The loss of the nawab broke up a whole world. Musicians, dancers, poets, cooks, retainers and officials who had depended on the court lost their livelihood. In the countryside the new rulers turned on the taluqdars, the great landholders who had kept forts and armed retainers. The taluqdars were disarmed and their forts destroyed. Under the Summary Settlement of 1856, the Company tried to remove them as intermediaries and to settle the revenue with those it thought were the actual owners of the land. In some areas the taluqdars lost more than half of their villages.

Peasants gained little. The revenue was often assessed too high and collected harshly, so the burden on the cultivator did not fall. Many sepoys of the Bengal army came from these same villages, and the hardships of their families added to their own grievances. When the revolt broke out in 1857, the anger of the court, the taluqdars, the peasants and the sepoys came together.
