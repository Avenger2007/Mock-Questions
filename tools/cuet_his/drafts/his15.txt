Chanhudaro, a tiny settlement of less than seven hectares, was given over almost entirely to craft production. Bead-making, shell-cutting, metal-working, seal-making and weight-making were all carried on there. Beads alone were made from a striking range of materials: stones such as carnelian, jasper, crystal and quartz; metals such as copper, bronze and gold; and shell, faience and terracotta. Each material called for its own technique. The red colour of carnelian, for instance, was obtained by firing the yellowish raw material at several stages of production.

Some crafts were concentrated close to their raw materials. Nageshwar and Balakot, both near the coast, were specialised centres for making shell objects such as bangles, ladles and inlay, which were then carried to other settlements.

How do archaeologists recognise such centres? They look for raw material such as stone nodules, whole shells and copper ore; for tools; and for unfinished objects, rejects and waste. Waste is one of the best indicators, since it shows that work was actually done at the spot.

Materials that were not available locally were obtained in other ways. Settlements were set up close to the sources, as at Shortughai, near the best source of lapis lazuli. Expeditions were also sent to distant areas, such as the Khetri region of Rajasthan for copper and south India for gold.
