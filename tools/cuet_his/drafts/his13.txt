François Bernier believed that one of the basic differences between Mughal India and Europe was that India lacked private property in land. He was a firm believer in the virtues of private property, and he saw crown ownership of land as harmful to both the state and its people. In his view, the Mughal emperor owned all the land and distributed it among his nobles.

According to Bernier, this had disastrous results. Since landholders could not pass on their land to their children, they were unwilling to make any long-term investment in improving it. The absence of private property had therefore prevented the rise of a class of "improving" landlords, such as he knew in Western Europe. The outcome, he argued, was the ruin of agriculture, the oppression of the peasantry, and a steady fall in the living standards of everyone except the ruling aristocracy.

Bernier described Indian society as a mass of impoverished people ruled by a small minority who were very rich and powerful. Between the two, he claimed, there was no social group or class worth the name. Historians point out, however, that no Mughal official document suggests that the state was the sole owner of land.
