From about 1000 BCE onwards, Brahmanas in particular classified people into gotras. Each gotra was named after a Vedic seer, and everyone who belonged to it was regarded as his descendant. Two rules about gotra were especially important. When a woman married, she was expected to give up her father's gotra and take her husband's. And members of the same gotra could not marry one another.

Inscriptions of the Satavahana rulers, who governed parts of western India and the Deccan, allow historians to test whether these rules were followed. Several Satavahana kings were identified through metronymics, names derived from their mothers. Gotami-puta Siri-Satakani, for instance, means Siri-Satakani, son of Gotami. Gotami is the feminine form of Gotama, the name of a Vedic seer and of a gotra.

The inscriptions suggest that a number of Satavahana queens kept the gotra names of their fathers instead of taking their husbands'. Some of them even belonged to the same gotra as their husbands, which went against the ideal of marrying outside the gotra. Marriages within the kin group were common among several communities in south India. Even so, succession to the Satavahana throne was generally patrilineal.
