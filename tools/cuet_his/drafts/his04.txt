In the nineteenth century Europeans took a keen interest in early Indian monuments, and many sculptures were carried off to museums abroad. Sanchi was luckier. When French officials asked Shahjehan Begum, the ruler of Bhopal, for permission to take the eastern gateway to France, they were finally content with carefully prepared plaster-cast copies. The English, who also wanted a gateway, accepted casts as well. The original stayed where it had been built. Shahjehan Begum and her successor, Sultan Jehan Begum, gave money to preserve the stupa, and Sultan Jehan Begum funded a museum and a guest house where John Marshall lived while he wrote his volumes on Sanchi.

Amaravati met a different fate. It was found before scholars understood how important it was to keep a monument where it stood, and its slabs were taken away to Calcutta, Madras and London. The archaeologist H.H. Cole argued strongly against such removal. He thought that museums should hold plaster casts while the originals remained in place.

The sculptures on Sanchi's gateways are best read alongside Buddhist texts. The Buddha is rarely shown in human form. Instead, an empty seat may stand for his meditation, a stupa for his mahaparinibbana, and a wheel for his first sermon. Other carvings, such as a woman swinging from a tree, come from popular traditions older than Buddhism. This figure, the shalabhanjika, was believed to make trees flower and bear fruit.
