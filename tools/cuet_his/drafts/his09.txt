Shaikh Nizamuddin Auliya's hospice stood at Ghiyaspur, on the banks of the Yamuna on the outskirts of Delhi. It had several small rooms and a large hall, the jama'at khana, where his companions and attendants lived and prayed. The shaikh himself lived in a small room on the roof of the hall, and there he met visitors in the mornings and evenings.

An open kitchen, the langar, ran on futuh, gifts given without being asked for. From early morning till late at night, people from all walks of life came to the hospice: soldiers, slaves, singers, merchants, poets, travellers, rich and poor, and Hindu jogis. Some came for blessings, some for amulets, and some for help in settling disputes. The poet Amir Khusrau was among those who came regularly.

Daily life in the hospice included bowing before the shaikh, offering water to visitors, shaving the heads of new initiates, and yogic exercises. Such practices show how the Chishtis adapted themselves to the traditions of the land in which they lived.
