On the evening of 23 August 2023, millions of people across India watched a screen showing a small dot moving slowly towards the surface of the Moon. At 6.04 p.m., the dot stopped. The Vikram lander of Chandrayaan-3 had touched down safely, and India became the fourth country to make a soft landing on the Moon and the first to land near its south pole.

The success had been a long time coming. Chandrayaan-1, launched by the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) in 2008, orbited the Moon and carried instruments that helped find evidence of water molecules on its surface, a discovery of great interest to scientists everywhere. Chandrayaan-2, in 2019, placed an orbiter around the Moon that continued to send back valuable data, but its lander crashed in the final minutes of its descent. Many people were disappointed. ISRO's engineers studied what had gone wrong, strengthened the lander's legs, improved its software and gave it more room for error.

The south pole of the Moon interests scientists because some of its craters never receive sunlight, and water ice may be trapped inside them. Water could one day be used by astronauts for drinking or broken down to make rocket fuel. After landing, Chandrayaan-3's rover, Pragyan, rolled out and studied the soil, confirming the presence of sulphur in the region.

India's missions are also known for their low cost compared with those of many other nations. Yet the greatest lesson of the Moon programme may not be about money or machines. It is that a failure, honestly studied, can become the first step towards success.

Meghna had never spoken in front of more than four people. In class she knew most of the answers, but she wrote them in the margin of her notebook rather than raise her hand. So when the debate teacher stopped her in the corridor and said that Ritika had fever and Meghna would have to take her place in the inter-school final the next morning, she felt the floor tilt under her feet.

She sat up until midnight, writing her speech on six cards. The topic was "Mobile phones do more harm than good to students", and her team was speaking against the motion.

In the morning, the hall seemed enormous. When her name was called, she walked to the microphone with her cards in her hand, looked out at three hundred faces and found that the words on the first card had stopped making any sense. Her hands were shaking. Somebody at the back laughed.

Meghna put the cards down on the lectern. Then, slowly at first, she began to talk about her cousin in a village in Odisha, who had no science teacher for two years and had learnt chemistry from free videos on a cracked phone, and who was now studying to become a pharmacist. She talked for three minutes without looking at a single card. When she sat down, the hall was silent for a moment, and then it applauded.

Her team lost the debate by two points. But as they were leaving, the judge, a retired professor with white hair, stopped Meghna at the door. "Your notes were not necessary," he said. "You had something better. You had a story you believed."

For three days it had rained in the hills, and on the fourth morning the river came down. We heard it before we saw it: a deep, steady roar, like a train that never finished passing. By the time we reached the bridge, the gentle river of our summer afternoons was gone, and in its place ran something brown and furious, heaving and twisting, carrying whole trees on its back as if they were matchsticks.

The water had swallowed the bathing steps, the washermen's stones and the little temple at the bend, so that only the tip of its flag showed above the current. Everything the river had taken seemed to be travelling with it: a cooking pot, a wooden door, a bale of straw, a plastic chair turning slowly round and round, as though it could not decide where to go.

People stood along the bank in silence. Nobody shouted; the river was louder than any shout. An old boatman beside me watched the water with narrowed eyes. "She is angry," he said, as one might speak of a relative. "She will be calm again. She always is. But she wants us to remember whose house this was first."

Two days later the water fell. The river slid back into its bed, sullen and muddy, leaving a line of wreckage on the banks and a fine grey silt on the fields. In a few months, the farmers said, that silt would give them the best wheat in years.
