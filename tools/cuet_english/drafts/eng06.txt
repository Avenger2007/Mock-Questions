Not long ago, buying vegetables in India meant counting out coins and waiting while the seller hunted for change. Today, in towns and villages alike, the buyer often simply points a phone at a small printed square and the payment is done in seconds. This change has been driven by the Unified Payments Interface, or UPI.

UPI was launched in 2016 by the National Payments Corporation of India, an organisation set up by India's banks under the guidance of the Reserve Bank of India. It links a person's bank account to a mobile app, so that money can be sent straight from one bank account to another at any hour of the day, on any day of the year. A user can pay by entering the other person's UPI ID or mobile number, or by scanning a QR code.

Part of its success lies in its simplicity. A shopkeeper does not need an expensive card machine; a printed QR code stuck on the counter is enough. Person-to-person payments are generally free, and the system works across different banks and apps, so a user of one app can pay a user of another without difficulty. Today UPI handles more than ten billion transactions a month, and some other countries have begun to accept it as well.

Convenience, however, brings new risks. Fraudsters may telephone users pretending to be bank officials, or send "payment requests" disguised as refunds. The rule to remember is simple: a UPI PIN is needed only to send money, never to receive it. Anyone who asks for your PIN so that you can "receive" a payment is trying to cheat you.

The final of the district cricket tournament was played on the maidan behind the sugar mill, and the whole of Kesarpur came to watch. People sat on the boundary wall, on the roofs of tractors and on the branches of the tamarind tree, and the mill whistle had been tied down so that it would blow for every six.

Kesarpur needed nine runs from the last over. At the crease was Salim, the baker's son, fifteen years old and so thin that the senior players called him "Matchstick". He had been put in only because the regular batsman had sprained his wrist.

The first ball beat his bat completely. The second he blocked. The crowd groaned. On the third, he swung with his eyes almost shut, and the ball flew over the bowler's head and bounced into the sugarcane field. The whistle screamed. Three runs were needed from three balls, and the town held its breath.

The fourth ball was a dot. The fifth he pushed into the gap, and they scrambled back for two. One run to win, one ball left, and the captain at the other end walked down the pitch and said only, "Run as if the bakery is on fire."

Salim tapped the last ball towards cover and ran. He ran faster than he had ever run in his life, and dived. The dust rose. The umpire raised his hand, and then the whole town was on the field, and Salim was in the air on a dozen shoulders.

That evening the baker gave away bread to everyone who passed his shop. Nobody called Salim "Matchstick" again.

The Sea at Kovalam Bay

Before the light, the boats go out,
their lanterns rocking, small and brave,
and the sea, half asleep, lets them pass
with a long, slow, silver yawn of wave.

All morning the women mend the nets
on the warm sand, and tell, and tell
the old stories their mothers told,
while the children chase the tide's last shell.

The sea is a mother, the old men say;
she feeds us and she rocks us, and then
some nights she rises, black and wild,
and takes her gifts back again.

At noon the boats come home, low-laden,
with silver heaped in every hold;
the gulls cry out their greedy praise,
and the fish are sold, and sold, and sold.

And when the sun sinks into the water,
the fishermen sit with their pipes and stare
at the sea they love, the sea they fear,
the sea that is always, always there.
