Imagine walking into an examination hall and seeing a question you cannot answer. Your heart pounds, your palms grow damp and your mouth goes dry. These feelings are not signs that something has gone wrong with your body. They are signs that it is working exactly as it was designed to.

When the brain senses a threat, a small region at its base, the hypothalamus, sends signals that cause the adrenal glands, which sit on top of the kidneys, to release hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol. Within seconds the heart beats faster, breathing quickens, muscles tighten and stored sugar pours into the blood to supply extra energy. Processes that are not urgent, such as digestion, slow down. Scientists call this the "fight-or-flight" response, because it prepared our distant ancestors either to fight a predator or to run from it.

In short bursts, this response can be useful. A little stress sharpens attention and can help an athlete run faster or a student think more quickly. The trouble begins when stress does not switch off. If a person feels under pressure for weeks or months, the body stays on alert, and the constant flow of stress hormones can disturb sleep, weaken the body's defences against illness and raise blood pressure.

The good news is that the body also has a way of calming itself, and we can help it. Regular exercise uses up stress hormones; slow, deep breathing signals to the brain that the danger has passed; enough sleep restores balance; and talking to a friend or family member makes problems feel lighter. None of these will remove every worry, but together they can stop ordinary stress from turning into a constant weight.

The smartphone arrived in a cardboard box from Pune, sent by Arvind to his parents in the village, with a note in capital letters: "NOW WE CAN SEE EACH OTHER EVERY SUNDAY."

His father, Shankar Rao, who had driven a bus for thirty years, looked at the shining black slab as if it were a snake. "I have a phone," he said, pointing to the old handset with its rubber buttons. "It rings. I answer. What more is needed?"

His wife, Kamala, said nothing, but she kept the box on the shelf beside the gods.

It was their granddaughter Pinky, aged nine, who took charge. She came every evening after school and sat between them on the cot. "Touch here, Ajja. No, softly. It is not a bus horn." Shankar Rao pressed too hard, then too softly, then held the phone upside down. Twice he called a stranger in Gujarat by mistake. Once he took a photograph of his own ear.

Kamala learnt faster. Within a week she was sending her son pictures of the new calf and voice messages about the price of onions.

On Sunday evening the phone rang with a strange, bubbling tune. Pinky swiped the screen, and there was Arvind's face, a little blurred, in a small rented room with a yellow wall behind him. Kamala began to cry. Shankar Rao leaned so close to the screen that his son could see only his moustache.

"Appa," said Arvind, laughing, "move back. I want to see you."

Shankar Rao moved back. For a long moment he looked at his son's face without speaking. Then he said, quite gruffly, "You have become thin. Are you eating?"

Spring Comes to the Lane

The cold has packed its grey bags and gone,
the neem has put on a coat of new green,
and down in the lane, since early dawn,
the children have mixed the brightest colours ever seen.

A shower of crimson, a splash of gold,
a fistful of green flung high in the air;
the young are laughing, and so are the old,
with pink in their beards and blue in their hair.

The milkman's cycle is purple and red,
the postman runs, but he runs too slow;
Grandmother sits on her cot, and her head
is crowned with saffron, a glowing glow.

Today no one is rich or poor,
no one is stranger, no one is friend;
we are all one colour at every door,
and the lane is a rainbow from end to end.

And when the evening comes, and the water
washes the colours down the drain,
something stays — in a son, a daughter —
that will not wash away again.

@@ V6|Passive voice with many people|e|d|D
Choose the option that best expresses the sentence in the passive voice:
Many people speak English in India.
A) English was spoken by many people in India.
B) English is being spoken by many people in India.
C) English has been spoken by many people in India.
D) English is spoken by many people in India.
=
Why: The simple present "speak" becomes "is spoken".
Why not the others: (A) is in the past. (B) is in the present continuous. (C) is in the present perfect.
Key point: Keep the tense when changing the voice.

@@ V6|Reported thanks|m|d|A
Choose the option that best expresses the sentence in indirect speech:
He said to me, "Thank you for your help."
A) He thanked me for my help.
B) He said me thank you for my help.
C) He told me that thank you for your help.
D) He thanked to me for your help.
=
Why: "Thank you" is reported with the verb "thanked"; "your" becomes "my".
Why not the others: (B) and (C) keep the direct words in a broken structure. (D) adds "to" and keeps "your".
Key point: Greetings and thanks are reported with a verb such as thanked, wished or greeted.

@@ V6|Correct sentences: since morning, one of the best, last year, too good|h|s|C
Which of the following sentences are grammatically correct?
A. The children are playing in the park since morning.
B. He is one of the best students in the class.
C. I have been to Shimla last year.
D. The news was too good to be true.
A) A and B only
B) A, B and D only
C) B and D only
D) C and D only
=
Why: B correctly uses "one of the best" + plural noun. D correctly uses "too ... to".
Why not the others: A should be "have been playing ... since morning". C should be "I went to Shimla last year". Options (A), (B) and (D) include A or C.
Key point: Actions continuing from a past point use the present perfect continuous.
