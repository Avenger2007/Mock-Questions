A honeybee visiting a flower is not thinking about fruit or seeds. It has come for food: sweet nectar, which it turns into honey, and protein-rich pollen, which it carries home to feed the young bees. But as the bee pushes into the flower, fine grains of pollen stick to the dense hairs on its body. When it visits the next flower of the same kind, some of those grains rub off on to the flower's female parts. This transfer of pollen is called pollination, and without it many plants cannot form fruits or seeds.

Bees are especially good pollinators because they are loyal. On a single trip, a bee usually visits flowers of only one kind, so the pollen it carries reaches the right plants. Bees also share information. A worker that has found a rich patch of flowers performs a "waggle dance" on the honeycomb, and the angle and length of the dance tell the other bees the direction and distance of the food.

The value of this work is enormous. Roughly three out of four of the world's leading food crops, including many fruits, vegetables, nuts and oilseeds, depend at least partly on animal pollinators, and bees are the most important of them. In India, farmers growing mustard, sunflower, apples and litchis often keep beehives near their fields, because better pollination can mean a much larger harvest.

Yet bees face growing dangers. Some pesticides harm them, the loss of wild flowering plants leaves them short of food, and diseases and parasites weaken their colonies. Protecting bees is not only about saving honey; it is about protecting the food on our plates.

The train to Darbhanga left Mumbai at midnight, and by eleven the platform was already full. Ramesh had a reserved berth for the first time in his life. For eight years, ever since he had come to the city to work on building sites, he had travelled home in the general compartment, standing for the first day and sleeping on newspaper by the door on the second.

This time he had saved for months. In his bag were a cotton saree for his mother, a school bag for his nephew, a steel tiffin carrier for his sister-in-law and, wrapped in a shirt, a small radio for his father, who had been asking for one for years.

The berth turned out to be the middle one, and directly below him a young couple with a baby argued all night about whose mother they would visit first. Ramesh did not mind. He lay with his arm over his bag and watched the dark country slide past the window: the lights of small stations, a fire in a field, a temple on a hill.

On the second evening, as the train crossed the Ganga, he went to the door and stood looking at the wide brown water. A boy beside him asked whether he was going home for Chhath. Ramesh nodded. "And you?" The boy shook his head. "I am going to Mumbai," he said, "for work." He was perhaps sixteen.

Ramesh looked at him for a long time. Then he took a paper from his pocket, wrote down the name of a contractor and a telephone number, and gave it to him. "Tell him Ramesh from Madhubani sent you," he said. "And always keep your arm over your bag."

The Lighthouse Keeper

He climbs the hundred-and-twenty steps
each evening as the light grows thin,
a lamp in his hand, a key on a string,
and the whole dark ocean waiting to come in.

He trims the wick, he wipes the glass,
he winds the clockwork round and round,
until the great beam wakes and walks
across the water without a sound.

He never sees the ships he saves;
they are only lights that pass, and go,
a cargo boat, a fishing fleet,
names on a list he will never know.

On stormy nights the tower shakes
and the waves come leaping at the door;
he sits and listens to the wind
and keeps the light, as he has before.

And in the morning, grey and spent,
he climbs back down to the empty shore,
puts out his lamp and boils his tea —
one more night, and one night more.
