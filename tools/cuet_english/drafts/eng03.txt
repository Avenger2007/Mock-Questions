Our bodies are protected by an immune system that learns from experience. When a germ such as a virus enters the body for the first time, the immune system takes several days to recognise it and produce antibodies, special proteins that lock on to the germ and help destroy it. During that delay, the germ can multiply and make us seriously ill.

Once the infection is over, however, the body keeps a small army of memory cells that remember the germ. If the same germ returns, these cells recognise it at once, and antibodies are produced so quickly that the illness is usually mild or does not develop at all.

A vaccine makes use of this memory without the danger of the disease. It contains a weakened or killed form of the germ, or only a harmless part of it. The immune system reacts to the vaccine as if it were a real infection and stores the memory. A vaccine is, in a sense, a dress rehearsal: the body practises its defence before the real performance.

The idea is older than the word. In 1796, the English doctor Edward Jenner noticed that milkmaids who had caught cowpox, a mild disease, seemed to be protected from smallpox. He tested the idea, and "vaccine" comes from vacca, the Latin word for cow. Nearly two centuries later, in 1980, the World Health Organization declared smallpox eradicated after a worldwide vaccination drive.

When most people in a community are vaccinated, a germ finds it hard to spread, which also protects babies and others who cannot be vaccinated. This is called herd immunity, and it is why vaccination is both a personal and a social responsibility.

Kavya had travelled by train many times, but never alone. Her parents saw her off at Pune with a tiffin box, a bottle of water and twenty instructions, the most important of which was: "Keep your ticket safe."

For the first few hours she did. She checked her bag at every station, then settled down with a thick novel and forgot the world. Somewhere past Manmad, the ticket examiner entered the coach. Kavya opened her bag. The ticket was not there. She emptied the side pocket, then her purse, then the tiffin bag. Her heart sank.

"No ticket?" said the examiner, a tall man with a grey moustache and a heavy register. He did not raise his voice, but he did not smile either. "You will have to pay the fare and a penalty."

Kavya had barely enough money for an auto at Nagpur. Her eyes stung, and she stared hard at the window so that nobody would see.

The old woman in the opposite seat, who had been knitting silently since morning, leaned forward. "Child," she said, "what is that sticking out of your book?"

Kavya opened the novel. There, marking page 212, was her ticket. She had used it as a bookmark three hours earlier.

The examiner checked it, wrote something in his register and, for the first time, smiled. "Read fewer pages at a time," he said, "and you will lose fewer things."

When the train reached Nagpur, Kavya's father asked whether the journey had been comfortable. "Very," she said, and decided that some stories were better told later.

The banyan at the edge of our village is older than anyone can remember. In summer it is a green fortress. The sun beats on the fields until the air shimmers, but under the banyan the shade is deep and cool, and the old men gather there with their hookahs and their arguments, while the buffaloes doze at a respectful distance.

When the monsoon comes, the tree drinks greedily. Its leaves shine as if they had been polished, and every branch drips long after the rain has stopped. Its hanging roots, which reach down from the branches like the grey beards of old men, swing and sway in the wind, and the children swing on them, shrieking with delight.

In winter the tree is quieter. Mist wraps its trunk in the mornings, and parrots squabble among the red figs that the birds of half the district seem to know about. The banyan feeds them all without complaint, a generous host who never counts the guests.

Each year some of its hanging roots reach the ground and harden into new trunks, so that the tree slowly walks outward, an inch at a time, across the land it has guarded for centuries. It has outlived kings and floods and famines. When I am old, I like to think, it will still be standing, patient and unhurried, sheltering children who have not yet been born.
