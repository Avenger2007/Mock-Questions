Plastic is light, cheap and strong, and that is exactly the problem. A plastic bottle thrown into the sea does not rot away like a fallen leaf. Instead, sunlight, wind and waves slowly break it into smaller and smaller pieces. The plastic does not disappear; it simply becomes harder to see.

Pieces of plastic smaller than five millimetres, about the size of a grain of rice, are called microplastics. Some begin small: tiny beads were once added to face-washes and toothpastes, and every time synthetic clothes are washed, thousands of fibres are rinsed into the drains. Others are the broken fragments of larger objects such as bags, bottles and fishing nets.

Because they are so small and so long-lasting, microplastics have spread almost everywhere. Scientists have found them in Arctic sea ice, on remote beaches, in the deepest ocean trenches and in table salt. Tiny sea creatures called plankton swallow them, small fish eat the plankton, and larger fish eat the small fish, so the plastic moves up the food chain. Some fragments also carry harmful chemicals on their surface.

What this means for human health is still being studied, and researchers are careful not to claim more than the evidence shows. But most agree that the safest course is to stop the flood at its source. Many countries, including India, have banned certain single-use plastic items, and people are being encouraged to carry cloth bags, refuse plastic straws and recycle what they can. Cleaning the oceans of microplastics is almost impossible; keeping plastic out of them in the first place is not.

For six weeks after she sent her scholarship application, Anjali met the postman at the gate every afternoon. For six weeks he shook his head. The results, the newspaper said, had already been posted to every applicant. Her friend Pooja had received a polite letter of refusal. Anjali had received nothing at all.

"Perhaps it is better not to know," her mother said gently, and Anjali began helping at her aunt's tailoring shop, telling herself that college could wait a year.

On a Thursday evening in late July, the postman came running down the lane, waving a creased brown envelope. "It went to Rampur first," he panted. "Someone wrote one wrong number in your PIN code."

Anjali tore it open. She had been selected for an interview. The interview was the next morning, at ten, in the district town eighty kilometres away.

For a moment nobody spoke. Then her aunt was pressing her best kurta, her mother was counting coins out of a steel tin, and her younger brother was running to ask the bus conductor about the night bus.

She reached the district office at half past nine, crumpled and sleepless, holding a file of certificates and a cold paratha wrapped in newspaper. When the panel asked why she wanted to study science, she forgot every sentence she had rehearsed and simply told them about the tailoring shop, and how she had measured cloth to the millimetre while dreaming of measuring stars.

The letter of selection came three weeks later. It was addressed correctly. Anjali keeps the first envelope, with its one wrong number, in the back of her physics textbook.

Autumn comes quietly to the orchard on the hill. One morning the air is suddenly thin and sharp, like a blade drawn from a sheath, and the far peaks, which have hidden behind clouds all summer, step out white and clear against a sky of deep, washed blue.

The apples have been picked by now. For weeks the slopes were full of ladders and voices, wooden crates stacked by the road and trucks groaning down the hairpin bends to the plains. Now the trees stand empty-handed, a little lighter, a little lonely, and their leaves are turning the colour of old copper coins. Every gust of wind spends a few more of them.

Old Bhagat Ram, who has looked after the orchard for forty years, walks slowly between the rows with his hands behind his back. He stops at each tree as if to ask after its health. Smoke from the village chimneys hangs low in the valley, and somewhere below a dog barks once and is silent again.

There is a sadness in all this, but it is not a bitter one. The orchard is not dying; it is resting. Under the frozen earth of winter, the roots will keep their quiet promise, and in spring the bare branches will break, once again, into a white storm of blossom.
