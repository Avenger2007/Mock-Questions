Where the Ganga, the Brahmaputra and the Meghna meet the Bay of Bengal, their waters spread into a maze of channels, mudflats and islands. This is the Sundarbans, the largest mangrove forest in the world, shared by India and Bangladesh. About forty per cent of it lies in the Indian state of West Bengal.

Mangroves are trees and shrubs that can live in salty water that rises and falls with the tides. Twice a day the sea floods the forest floor and then drains away, leaving soft mud that holds very little oxygen. To survive, many mangroves send up breathing roots, called pneumatophores, which poke out of the mud like rows of pencils and take in air at low tide. Others stand on arching stilt roots that keep them steady in the shifting soil.

The forest is home to a remarkable range of life: fish, crabs, crocodiles, spotted deer, hundreds of kinds of birds and the Royal Bengal tiger, which has learnt to swim between the islands. Its shallow waters are also nurseries where young fish grow before they move out to sea, so the fishing families of the coast depend on them.

The mangroves protect people too. Their dense roots slow down storm waves and hold the soil together, so villages behind a healthy belt of forest usually suffer less when a cyclone strikes. Yet the Sundarbans is under pressure. Rising sea levels, saltier water and the clearing of trees for farms and fish ponds are shrinking parts of the forest. Scientists warn that protecting it is not a luxury but a necessity for the millions who live along this coast.

Every morning on his way to school, Arjun passed the cobbler who sat under the railway bridge. The old man had a tin box of tools, a small iron last and a cracked mirror tied to the pillar behind him. Arjun had never looked at him twice.

Then, one wet July morning, the sole of Arjun's left shoe came loose. It flapped with every step like a hungry mouth, and two boys from his class laughed so loudly that Arjun's ears burnt. He had only ten rupees in his pocket, and the examination began in half an hour.

"Sit," said the cobbler, pointing to a low stool. He took the shoe, turned it over in his rough hands and clicked his tongue. "Cheap glue," he said. "They make shoes to break, so that you buy new ones." Then he set to work, stitching the sole with a thick waxed thread, his needle moving in and out as steadily as a clock.

When he had finished, Arjun held out the ten-rupee note, ashamed that it was so little. The old man pushed his hand away. "Pay me when you pass," he said, and turned to the next customer.

Arjun passed. On the day the results came out, he ran to the bridge with a box of sweets and the money he had saved. The cobbler laughed, took one sweet and refused the money once again. "Bring me your shoes when they are old," he said. "That is payment enough."

Arjun still has those shoes. He outgrew them years ago, but he cannot bring himself to throw them away.

Rain on the Tin Roof

At first a single drop, then two,
like someone tapping, shy and slow,
who is not sure that he may enter —
and then the whole sky lets go.

Now the roof is a drum: a thousand fingers
rattle and hammer, rat-a-tat;
the gutters gurgle, the drainpipe chuckles,
and the cat curls tighter on the mat.

My grandmother sets down her needle
and says the fields will drink tonight;
my father counts the leaks in silence
and moves the buckets left and right.

I lie awake and let it sing me
songs that no radio can play —
of mango boughs and swollen rivers,
of muddy schoolbags, and of May

burning behind us like a fever
we have at last got over. Then,
as softly as it came, it leaves,
and the roof is only tin again.
