For thousands of years, millets such as jowar, bajra and ragi were everyday food in much of India. They are small-seeded grains that grow well where other crops struggle. Millets need far less water than rice, tolerate heat and poor soil, and many varieties are ready for harvest in just a few months. A farmer in a dry district can often raise a millet crop on rain alone.

From the 1960s, however, the Green Revolution brought high-yielding varieties of rice and wheat, along with irrigation, fertilisers and government support for buying these two grains. Farmers naturally grew what paid best, and the area under millets fell sharply. In many cities, millets came to be seen as "poor people's food", while polished white rice and wheat flour became symbols of a better life.

Nutrition scientists now see millets very differently. Most millets are rich in fibre and minerals. Ragi is one of the best plant sources of calcium, and bajra is a good source of iron. Millets contain no gluten, and because they are digested slowly, they release sugar into the blood more gradually than polished rice does, which can help people who need to control their blood sugar.

In 2023, which the United Nations declared the International Year of Millets at India's suggestion, these grains returned to the spotlight. Millet dosas, biscuits and noodles appeared on shop shelves, and several states added millets to school meals. With rainfall becoming less predictable, supporters argue that these hardy grains are not a step backwards but a sensible choice for the future, good for the farmer, for the consumer and for the land.

For three months Raju did not touch the wheel. He swept the yard, carried water, kneaded clay with his feet until his legs ached, and watched Ustad Karim's hands. The old potter rarely spoke. When he did, it was usually to say, "Not yet."

Then one morning in March, Ustad Karim stood up from the wheel and pointed at the stool. Raju sat down, his heart thumping. He threw a lump of wet clay on to the centre, set the wheel spinning and pressed with both hands, as he had seen his master do a thousand times.

The clay wobbled, leaned and flew off the wheel into the dust.

He tried again. This time the walls rose a little, then folded in on themselves like a tired flower. The third pot collapsed. The fourth tore. By noon his hands were raw, his shirt was grey with clay, and the boys from the next street had gathered at the gate to laugh.

Ustad Karim said nothing. He only brought another lump of clay and set it on the wheel.

It was late afternoon when Raju lifted his hands from the ninth pot and found that it was still standing. It leaned a little to the left, and its rim was thicker on one side than the other. He looked at it and felt like crying.

The next day, when the kiln was opened, Ustad Karim took out the crooked little pot, filled it with water from the well and set it beside his own sleeping-mat. "Every potter's first pot is ugly," he said. "Mine was uglier." It was the longest speech Raju had ever heard him make.

A Letter Home from the Hills

Dear Amma, the hills are bigger than you said,
and greener, and the clouds walk in the street;
they come into our classroom without knocking
and sit, like sleepy cows, about our feet.

The food is fine. The porridge could be warmer.
I share my room with boys from five states;
one snores like a tractor climbing a mountain,
one cries at night, and nobody says he hates

this place, though every Sunday after letters
the dormitory is very quiet and still.
I am learning to tie my own tie properly,
and to climb, without stopping, Chapel Hill.

Last week I saw a flame-red pheasant
cross the path as if it owned the wood;
I wished that you could see it. Do not worry.
I wear the sweater. I am being good.

Tell Baba that I came third in the maths test.
Tell Meenu she may keep my old cricket bat.
And Amma — if the mango tree is fruiting,
save one for me. Just one. I'll come back for that.
