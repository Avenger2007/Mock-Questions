The ground beneath our feet seems solid, but the Earth's outer shell is broken into huge slabs called tectonic plates, which move very slowly, a few centimetres a year. Where two plates press or slide against each other, the rocks bend and store up strain, like a stick being slowly bent. When the strain becomes too great, the rocks suddenly snap and slip along a crack called a fault. The energy released travels outwards as seismic waves, and the ground shakes.

India is especially exposed. The Indian plate is still pushing northwards into the Eurasian plate, the same collision that raised the Himalaya, and more than half of India's land area is considered prone to moderate or severe earthquakes. The Bureau of Indian Standards divides the country into seismic zones; Zone V, the highest, includes the north-east, parts of the Himalaya and the Kutch region of Gujarat, where an earthquake in January 2001 killed thousands of people.

Engineers often say that earthquakes do not kill people; buildings do. Most deaths are caused not by the shaking itself but by walls and roofs falling on those inside. A building can be made far safer at little extra cost. Steel bars in concrete columns and beams must be properly tied together; a continuous concrete band at the level of the doors and windows helps brick walls move as one unit; and light roofs are less deadly than heavy ones if they fall. India has building codes that set out such rules, but they are not always followed.

Earthquakes cannot yet be predicted, but their damage can be reduced. Safe buildings, practised drills and the simple rule of "drop, cover and hold on" can save thousands of lives.

The school at Ghatgaon had one room, one blackboard and, for the last six years, one teacher. Dolma Negi had been posted there at twenty-four, straight from her training college in Dehradun, and everyone had said she would ask for a transfer within a year.

She did not. Instead she learnt the names of every child's goats, the paths they walked to school, and the months in which they would vanish to help with the harvest. When there was no chalk, she taught counting with pine cones. When the roof leaked, she moved the class under the walnut tree. When snow closed the road for three weeks in January, she sent homework up the hill with the milkman.

One spring, ten-year-old Kundan stopped coming. Dolma waited a week, then walked the six kilometres to his village. She found him on the hillside with forty sheep. His father had broken his leg, and there was no one else to take the flock out.

Dolma did not argue. She sat down on a rock beside the boy and took a book out of her bag. "The sheep can graze while you read," she said. For the next two months, she climbed the hill every Saturday and Sunday with a new lesson.

Kundan came back to school in July, when his father could walk again. That winter he came first in the block-level mathematics test. When the newspaper reporter asked him who his teacher was, he pointed down the valley at the one-room school and said, as if it were obvious, "Dolma Madam. She comes to you if you cannot come to her."

After the last visitor has gone and the great doors have been locked, the museum begins its other life. The guard switches off the main lights, and the long galleries sink into a blue dusk, broken only by the green glow of the exit signs.

In the hall of sculpture, the stone gods stand in their rows, their faces calm, their hands raised in blessing over empty floors. They have watched over temples for a thousand years; one more night is nothing to them. A bronze dancer, caught in the middle of a turn, seems about to finish it the moment no one is looking.

Further on, in the gallery of ancient pots, the glass cases hold what is left of meals and markets and kitchens from cities that no longer exist. A child's clay toy, a small bull with one horn missing, lies beside a cooking pot with the marks of fingers still pressed into its rim. Somewhere, four thousand years ago, someone made these things, used them, and set them down for the last time.

The guard walks his rounds slowly, his torch moving across faces and fragments. He has done this for twenty-two years, and he says that he is never afraid, only sometimes a little lonely, as if he were the youngest person in a very old family. At five in the morning he unlocks his little room, makes himself a cup of tea, and wishes the stone gods good morning before the cleaners arrive.
