In the dry lands of Gujarat and Rajasthan, rain falls for only a few weeks each year, and for the rest of the year the rivers shrink and the wells run low. For centuries, people here solved the problem in a remarkable way: they built stepwells, known as vav in Gujarati and baori in Rajasthan.

A stepwell is a deep well that people could reach by walking down flights of steps. Instead of lowering a bucket on a rope, villagers went down to the water, however far it had fallen. In the rainy season the well filled almost to the top; in the dry months the water level dropped, and people simply went further down. Because the water lay deep underground, it stayed cool, and the shaded galleries around it became places to rest, meet and celebrate festivals.

Many stepwells were also works of art. The Rani ki Vav at Patan in Gujarat, built in the eleventh century in memory of a king, descends through seven levels and is decorated with hundreds of carved figures of gods, goddesses and dancers. For centuries it lay buried under silt left by floods, until archaeologists cleared it in the twentieth century. In 2014 it was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and it now appears on the hundred-rupee note. At Abhaneri in Rajasthan, the Chand Baori has thousands of narrow steps arranged in neat zigzags down thirteen levels.

When piped water and hand pumps arrived, many stepwells fell into neglect and became dumps for rubbish. Today, as cities face water shortages, engineers and citizens are restoring some of them, discovering that these ancient structures were not only beautiful but also a sensible way of saving rain.

Nani had made besan laddoos every Diwali for fifty years, and every Diwali somebody asked her for the recipe. "There is no recipe," she always said, and laughed.

This year Ishaan, who was sixteen and believed in measurements, decided to write one down. He followed her into the kitchen with a notebook and a pen.

"How much gram flour, Nani?"

She tipped some into the heavy pan without looking. "This much."

"How much is 'this much'?"

"Enough for the family."

Ishaan sighed and weighed what was left in the packet, then did a quick subtraction. "And the ghee?"

"Until the flour stops being thirsty," said Nani, pouring it in a thin golden stream.

She roasted the flour on a low flame, stirring slowly, while Ishaan stood ready with his pen. Five minutes passed, then ten, then fifteen. "When is it done?"

"When the whole house knows," she said.

He was about to protest when the smell reached him: warm, nutty and sweet, like every Diwali he could remember. From the next room his little sister shouted, "Nani is making laddoos!"

Nani smiled at him. "There," she said. "Now it is done."

That night Ishaan copied his notes neatly. He had grams and minutes and temperatures. But at the bottom of the page, in brackets, he added a line no cookbook would have printed: "Roast until the house knows."

Night in the Desert

When the last camel bell has faded
and the dunes have given back the heat,
the sky comes down so close above us
it almost touches the sand at our feet.

No city glare, no lamp, no window —
only the dark, so deep and wide
that every star burns like a lantern
somebody forgot to hide.

The Milky Way, a road of silver,
runs from one horizon to the other;
my grandfather points out the Seven Sages,
just as he learnt them from his mother.

A shooting star slides down the darkness,
quick as a match struck and gone;
I make a wish, and keep it secret,
the way that wishes must be done.

And lying there on the cooling sand,
so small beneath that endless sky,
I feel, somehow, not less but larger,
as if the stars were watching, and so was I.
