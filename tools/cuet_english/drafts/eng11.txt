Before the printing press, every book had to be copied by hand. A single copy could take months, so books were rare and costly, and most people never owned one. In the middle of the fifteenth century, Johannes Gutenberg of Mainz in Germany developed a press that used movable metal type: small blocks, each carrying one letter, which could be arranged into pages, inked, printed and then used again. Hundreds of copies of the same page could now be produced in the time it once took to write a few.

Printing reached India in 1556, when Portuguese missionaries set up a press in Goa. For more than two centuries, however, it was used mainly for religious texts. India's first newspaper did not appear until 1780, when an Irishman, James Augustus Hicky, began publishing Hicky's Bengal Gazette in Calcutta. It was full of gossip and sharp attacks on officials of the East India Company. The Company was not amused: by 1782 Hicky's press had been seized and the paper closed.

Indian reformers soon saw that the press could be a voice for their own ideas. In the 1820s, Raja Ram Mohan Roy started journals in Bengali and Persian that discussed social reform and public questions. By the end of the nineteenth century, newspapers in English and in many Indian languages were spreading news and nationalist ideas to readers far beyond the big cities. The British government grew so worried about the Indian-language press that in 1878 it passed the Vernacular Press Act to control it.

The story of printing in India is therefore not only about machines. It is about who has the right to speak, and who gets to listen.

Ananya had not lost a game of chess in two years. She had beaten every child in her school, three teachers and, once, the uncle who had taught her the moves. So when she arrived at her first state-level tournament, eleven years old and wearing her lucky blue hairband, she was quietly sure she would win.

Her first-round opponent was a tall, silent boy of fourteen who wrote down every move in a small notebook. For twenty moves the game was even. Then Ananya saw a chance to win his bishop. She took it at once, with a little flick of her fingers, and looked up to see whether he had noticed.

He had. Three moves later, his knight landed on a square she had not even looked at, and she realised, with a cold feeling in her stomach, that the bishop had been bait. Her queen was trapped. She played on for another ten moves, hoping for a miracle, and then laid her king down on its side.

She did not cry until she reached the corridor. Her coach found her there and sat beside her on the bench without saying anything for a long time.

"Show me," he said at last, and handed her a pocket set. She set up the position. "Here," he said, touching the bishop. "Why did you take it?"

"Because it was free."

"Nothing on a chessboard is free," he said. "Ask what it costs."

That night, in a new notebook, Ananya wrote down every move of the game. At the top of the first page she wrote: "Lesson one." She won her next four games.

For two months the earth had been waiting. The fields had cracked into a pattern of dry tiles, the pond had shrunk to a cake of mud where the buffaloes stood in hopeless patience, and the leaves of the mango trees hung grey with dust. Every afternoon clouds gathered over the hills, looked down at the thirsty land and drifted away again, like rich relatives who promise and never come.

Then, one evening, the wind changed. It came from the south, damp and sudden, and the tops of the trees began to toss. The light turned a strange yellow-green. Somewhere a door banged, and the hens ran for cover with their wings half open.

The first drops were enormous. They fell far apart, each one striking the dust with a small soft explosion, and where they struck, the earth darkened in coins. Then the whole sky opened, and the rain came down in grey ropes, drumming on the tin sheds, hissing on the hot stones, filling the cracks in the fields as if it were trying to stitch the land together again.

And the smell — that is what everyone remembers. It rose from the soaked ground all at once, rich and earthy and green, the smell of every childhood monsoon. Children ran out to dance in the courtyards. Old men stood in their doorways with their palms held out, catching the water as if it were a gift, which of course it was.
