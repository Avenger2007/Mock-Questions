Every summer, India waits for the monsoon. In April and May, the sun beats down on the subcontinent, and the land heats up far faster than the surrounding seas. Warm air over north-western India rises, creating an area of low pressure, while the air over the cooler Indian Ocean stays relatively heavy. Since winds blow from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure, moist air from the ocean is drawn towards the land. These are the south-west monsoon winds.

The monsoon usually reaches the coast of Kerala around the first of June and covers most of the country by early July. It travels in two branches, one from the Arabian Sea and the other from the Bay of Bengal. When the Arabian Sea branch meets the Western Ghats, the air is forced to rise; it cools, and its moisture falls as heavy rain on the windward slopes. By the time the winds cross the mountains, they have lost much of their moisture, so the plateau on the other side receives far less rain. Geographers call such a dry region a rain-shadow area.

Between June and September, India receives roughly three-quarters of its annual rainfall. Kharif crops such as rice depend on it, reservoirs that supply drinking water and electricity are refilled, and wells are recharged. A delayed or weak monsoon can therefore push up food prices and slow the economy, while an unusually strong one can bring floods.

Because so much depends on these few months, the India Meteorological Department issues its first forecast in April and updates it as the season approaches. Farmers, planners and ordinary citizens follow these forecasts closely.

When old Ramdas-ji retired, nobody in Sonapur wanted to look after the village library. It was only one dusty room in the panchayat building, with three shelves of books and a register that no one had signed for months. Meera, who had just finished Class 12 and was waiting for her college to open, offered to keep it open in the afternoons.

On the first day, she swept the floor, wiped every shelf and sat at the table with a book open in front of her. Nobody came. On the second day, two boys peeped in through the window, giggled and ran away. That evening Meera carried a box of picture books to the neem tree outside and began to read a story aloud, doing the voices of the tiger and the clever jackal. Within minutes, the two boys were back with their sisters. The ice was broken.

By Wednesday, the children were coming every afternoon, and some of their mothers waited to borrow cookery and health books. On Friday, an old farmer stood at the door, turning his turban in his hands. "Do you have anything about soil?" he asked. "My wheat has not done well for two years." Meera searched the shelves, found a government booklet on soil testing and copied the address of the nearest testing centre for him.

On Saturday evening, she counted the names in the register: twenty-three. It was not a large number, but as she locked the door, Meera felt that the room behind her was no longer empty.

Before the sun had climbed over the far bank, the river was already awake. It stirred under a thin blanket of mist, stretched its grey arms along the steps of the ghat and murmured to the boats tied at its edge. The first temple bell rang out, clear and cold, and a flock of pigeons rose from the roof of the old rest-house like a handful of thrown ash.

One by one, the lamps of the evening before floated past, their flames long gone, small leaf-boats carrying nothing but the memory of light. An old woman stood waist-deep in the water, her palms joined, so still that she seemed to have grown out of the river itself. Behind her, a boatman dipped his oar — splash, drip, splash — and the silence closed again behind every stroke.

Then the light came. It did not arrive all at once; it leaked into the sky, pale as milk at first, then gold, then a fierce copper that set the water on fire. The mist dissolved as though it had been only a dream the river was having. Bells, boatmen's banter and the bargaining of flower-sellers filled the air, and the city, which had been holding its breath all night, remembered its voice.

=

@@ V6|Passive voice, present continuous|m|d|C
Choose the option that best expresses the sentence in the passive voice:
The workers are repairing the road near the school.
A) The road near the school was being repaired by the workers.
B) The road near the school has been repaired by the workers.
C) The road near the school is being repaired by the workers.
D) The road near the school is repaired by the workers.
=
Why: The present continuous "are repairing" becomes "is being repaired" in the passive, with the object "the road" as the new subject.
Why not the others: (A) changes the tense to the past. (B) changes it to the present perfect. (D) changes it to the simple present.
Key point: Keep the tense when changing the voice.

@@ V6|Reported speech with will and tomorrow|h|d|A
Choose the option that best expresses the sentence in indirect speech:
She said to me, "I will finish the project tomorrow."
A) She told me that she would finish the project the next day.
B) She told me that I will finish the project tomorrow.
C) She said to me that she will finish the project the next day.
D) She told me that she would finish the project tomorrow.
=
Why: With a past reporting verb, "will" becomes "would", "I" becomes "she", and "tomorrow" becomes "the next day"; "said to me" becomes "told me".
Why not the others: (B) changes the pronoun wrongly and keeps "will". (C) keeps "will". (D) keeps "tomorrow".
Key point: Change tense, pronouns and time words together.

@@ V6|Choosing the correct sentences|h|s|D
Which of the following sentences are grammatically correct?
A. Neither the teacher nor the students were present.
B. Each of the boys has a bicycle.
C. One of my friends live in Delhi.
D. The news are good today.
A) A, B and C only
B) B and D only
C) C and D only
D) A and B only
=
Why: In A, the verb agrees with the nearer subject "students" (were). In B, "each" is singular (has).
Why not the others: C should be "lives" (the subject is "one"). D should be "is" ("news" is singular). Options (A), (B) and (C) include C or D.
Key point: Find the true subject before choosing the verb.
