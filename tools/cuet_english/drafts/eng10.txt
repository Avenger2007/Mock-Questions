A coral reef looks like a garden of brightly coloured rocks, but it is built by animals. Each coral is made up of thousands of tiny creatures called polyps, relatives of jellyfish, which build hard skeletons of limestone around themselves. Over hundreds of years these skeletons pile up into reefs large enough to be seen from space.

Most reef-building corals cannot survive alone. Inside their tissues live microscopic algae that use sunlight to make food, and they share much of this food with the coral. In return, the coral gives the algae shelter. The algae also give corals their colours.

This partnership is delicate. When the sea becomes too warm for several weeks, the stressed corals push the algae out of their bodies. Without them, the corals lose both their colour and most of their food, and only the white skeleton shows through the clear tissue. This is called coral bleaching. If the water cools soon enough, the algae can return and the coral may recover; if the heat lasts, the coral starves and dies.

Reefs cover far less than one per cent of the ocean floor, yet they are home to roughly a quarter of all known sea species. They protect coasts by breaking the force of waves, and millions of people depend on them for fish and tourism. In India, reefs are found in the Gulf of Mannar, the Gulf of Kutch, Lakshadweep and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.

As the oceans warm, bleaching has become more frequent and more widespread. Scientists say that cutting the gases that heat the planet, together with reducing pollution and overfishing, offers reefs their best chance of survival.

Sister Leela's night shift at the district hospital began at eight, when the visitors were sent home and the long corridor grew quiet. There were twenty-six patients in the general ward and one other nurse, a nervous girl in her first month, who kept asking whether she was doing things right.

At half past eleven, a farmer carried in his small son, wrapped in a blanket and burning with fever. The boy's lips were dry, and he did not open his eyes when Leela spoke to him. The doctor on duty was in the operation theatre. Leela sponged the child with cool water, checked his temperature every fifteen minutes and sent the young nurse running for the doctor the moment the reading rose again.

At two o'clock, the power failed. For a terrible minute the ward was black and silent; then the generator coughed, choked and started, and the lights flickered back. Leela, who had been standing by the boy's bed with a torch in her teeth and the drip in her hand, let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

By morning the fever had broken. The boy opened his eyes, looked around at the strange room and asked for his mother. The farmer, who had not slept, tried to give Leela a hundred-rupee note. She shook her head and pointed to the young nurse. "Thank her," she said. "She ran faster than anyone."

At eight, Leela handed over the ward, caught the bus home and fell asleep with her shoes on. Nobody would ever write about her night. She did not expect anyone to.

In Praise of an Old Bicycle

It has no gears, no shining paint,
its bell is hoarse, its saddle torn;
it was my grandfather's, then my father's,
and it was rattling before I was born.

It squeaks on every second turn
like an old man who hums off-key;
it does not hurry, does not boast,
and it has never once failed me.

It took my father to his first job,
my mother to her wedding day,
sat patient outside the hospital
the morning I was on my way.

Now it carries me to school,
past honking cars that sweat and fume,
stuck in their own impatient smoke —
while my old bicycle finds room.

I know a new one would be faster;
my friends all laugh and say I should.
But who would sell a friend for speed?
Not I. Not while its wheels are good.
