India is one of the sunniest large countries in the world. Most regions receive bright sunshine on around three hundred days a year, and the sunlight that falls on the country in a single day carries far more energy than India uses in that time. For millions of villagers, this sunshine is beginning to light homes, run pumps and power small businesses.

Solar panels are made of photovoltaic cells, thin layers of material, usually silicon, that produce electricity when sunlight falls on them. They have no moving parts, make no noise and give off no smoke. Once installed, they need little more than regular cleaning to remove dust.

For a farmer, the most important use of solar power is often the irrigation pump. Many farmers have depended on pumps that run on diesel, which is expensive and must be carried to the field, or on grid electricity, which in some villages comes for only a few hours, often at night. A solar pump works in daylight, when the farmer is in the field, and costs almost nothing to run. Government schemes such as PM-KUSUM, launched in 2019, help farmers meet part of the cost of installing one.

Solar power has its difficulties too. The upfront cost of panels and batteries can be high for a poor household, even though the fuel, sunlight, is free. Batteries are needed to store electricity for the night, and they wear out after some years. Panels must be maintained, and a mini-grid needs trained local mechanics. Yet as prices fall and skills spread, more villages are discovering that the sun overhead is a power station that never sends a bill.

Every Sankranti, the sky over Gopal's colony filled with kites: red, yellow, purple, kites with tails and kites with eyes painted on them. Gopal watched them from the roof of the one-room house where he lived with his father, who drove an auto-rickshaw and believed that kites were a waste of money.

"Twenty rupees for a piece of paper that flies away," his father said. "You can eat for two days on twenty rupees."

So that year Gopal made his own. He cut two thin sticks from an old broom, tied them in a cross with his mother's sewing thread and stretched over them a page from the newspaper his father brought home. He glued the edges with boiled rice. For a tail he used a strip of his torn school shirt. When he had finished, the kite looked like a sheet of yesterday's news with a ragged blue ribbon.

The boys on the next roof laughed until they had to hold their stomachs.

But in the afternoon, when the wind came, Gopal's kite rose. It rose past the water tank and the mobile tower, and it went on rising, a small grey square against the blue, long after the boys had lost three bright kites in their battles. It did not cut anyone's string. It simply stayed up, steady and patient, as if it had all the time in the world.

At sunset, Gopal felt a hand on his shoulder. His father had come home early. He watched the kite for a long moment without speaking. Then he held out his hand. "Let me try," he said.

They will pull the house down on Monday. The papers have been signed, the furniture sold, and the men with hammers are already measuring the walls with their eyes. For three days it stands empty, like a man who has said goodbye but not yet left the room.

I walk through it one last time. The rooms are larger without the beds and cupboards, and every footstep comes back to me from the bare walls. On the kitchen wall the soot of forty years of cooking has left a dark ghost where the stove used to be. On the door of the store-room, a ladder of pencil lines climbs upwards, each one marked with a name and a year. My own name is near the bottom, beside a line so low that I can hardly believe I was ever that small.

The courtyard is the hardest. The tulsi plant is gone, but its square stone bed is still there, and the neem tree that shaded our afternoon games still drops its yellow leaves on the floor that nobody will sweep again. A sparrow flies in through the broken skylight, looks around as if it, too, is searching for someone, and flies out again.

I close the front door behind me, though there is nothing left inside to keep safe. Habit, I suppose. Or perhaps one wants to leave a house the way one would leave a sleeping person, quietly, without slamming the door on all those years.
