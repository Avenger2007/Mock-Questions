Many students believe that the best way to prepare for an examination is to stay up all night with their books. Research on sleep suggests the opposite. Sleep is not simply time when the brain is switched off; it is when the brain does some of its most important work on memory.

During the day, new information is held for a while in a small, curved part of the brain called the hippocampus, which acts rather like a notebook for recent events. At night, especially during deep sleep, the brain appears to replay these memories and move them into the outer layer of the brain, the cortex, where they can be stored for the long term. Scientists call this process consolidation. Later in the night, during the stage known as REM sleep, when most vivid dreams occur, the brain seems to link new memories with old ones, which may help us solve problems and see connections.

A night of sleep passes through several cycles of these stages, each lasting about ninety minutes. Cutting sleep short therefore removes some of the stages in which memories are strengthened. Students who sleep well after learning usually remember more the next day than those who stay awake, and a tired brain also finds it harder to pay attention to new material.

Teenagers generally need eight to ten hours of sleep a night, yet many get far less. Bright screens late at night can delay the body's natural signal for sleep. The advice of sleep researchers is simple: study in the evening, put the phone away well before bedtime, and let the night finish the work.

Kalu was a street dog until the winter he followed Neha home from the bus stop and refused to leave. Her father grumbled for a week about the cost of milk, then built him a kennel out of an old cupboard and painted his name on it in red.

On Diwali night, the crackers began at dusk. Kalu, who had never heard such noise, shook under the bed until a rocket burst right outside the window. Then he bolted through the open door and into the dark lanes, and he did not come back.

For nine days the family searched. Neha's brother printed posters with Kalu's photograph and pasted them on every lamp post. Her father, who had once complained about the milk, took his scooter out each evening and rode slowly through the colonies, calling the dog's name. Neha left the gate open at night, against all the rules of the house.

On the tenth morning she found Kalu asleep on the doorstep. He was thinner, his paws were sore and there was a burr caught in his ear, but around his neck was a blue cotton collar that none of them had seen before. Tied to it was a slip of paper. In careful handwriting it said: "He stayed with us at the dairy for a week. He ate well but he would not settle. We think he wanted to go home."

Neha's father read it twice, cleared his throat and went inside. A few minutes later, he came out with a full bowl of milk.

At dusk the old market changes its face. The office-goers have gone, the shutters of the cloth shops are rattling down, and in their place the evening people arrive: the chaat-sellers with their brass pots, the flower women with heaps of marigold and jasmine, the man who sells balloons shaped like fish.

The light goes first. It slides off the rooftops and lingers for a while on the temple spire, turning it the colour of a ripe apricot, and then it is gone, and the market switches on its own small suns. Bulbs swing on wires above the vegetable carts, kerosene lamps hiss beside the fruit-sellers, and the glass bangles in the bangle shop catch every flame and throw it back, a thousand times brighter.

The noise rises with the dark. Sellers sing out their prices in a tune that has not changed in a hundred years; a bicycle bell argues with a scooter horn; somewhere a radio plays an old film song that nobody is listening to and everybody knows. The air is thick with frying, with coriander, with dust and incense and the sweet, heavy breath of the jasmine.

I have walked through this market a thousand evenings. I know where the pavement is broken and which dog sleeps under which cart. And still, every evening, as the lights come on, I feel I am arriving somewhere for the first time.

@@ V6|Passive voice, past continuous|m|d|B
Choose the option that best expresses the sentence in the passive voice:
The children were flying kites on the terrace.
A) Kites were flown by the children on the terrace.
B) Kites were being flown by the children on the terrace.
C) Kites are being flown by the children on the terrace.
D) Kites had been flown by the children on the terrace.
=
Why: The past continuous "were flying" becomes "were being flown".
Why not the others: (A) is in the simple past. (C) is in the present continuous. (D) is in the past perfect.
Key point: Continuous tenses use "being" in the passive.

@@ V6|Reported suggestion with let us|h|d|C
Choose the option that best expresses the sentence in indirect speech:
He said, "Let us go for a walk."
A) He said that let us go for a walk.
B) He told that we go for a walk.
C) He suggested that we should go for a walk.
D) He ordered us to go for a walk.
=
Why: "Let us" expresses a suggestion; it is reported with "suggested/proposed that we should ...".
Why not the others: (A) keeps the direct form. (B) "told" needs an object and the tense is not shifted. (D) changes a suggestion into an order.
Key point: Let us → suggested that we should.

@@ V6|Correct sentences: prefer to, did come, jury, bread and butter|e|s|A
Which of the following sentences are grammatically correct?
A. I prefer reading to watching television.
B. He did not came to school yesterday.
C. The jury has given its verdict.
D. Bread and butter are my usual breakfast.
A) A and C only
B) B and D only
C) A, C and D only
D) A and B only
=
Why: A uses "prefer ... to" correctly. In C, the jury acts as one body, so "has" and "its" are correct.
Why not the others: B should be "did not come". D should be "is": "bread and butter" is one dish. Options (B), (C) and (D) include B or D.
Key point: Two nouns joined by "and" take a singular verb when they name one thing.
