Two very different systems of farming are found in the tropics. Plantation agriculture was introduced by Europeans in their colonies. Its estates are large and need heavy capital investment, managerial and technical support and scientific methods of cultivation. Each estate specialises in a single crop, depends on cheap labour, and is linked by a good transport system to factories and ports, because most of the produce is processed and exported. Tea, coffee, cocoa, rubber, sugarcane, bananas and oil palm are typical plantation crops; some coffee fazendas of Brazil are still managed by Europeans.

Shifting cultivation is practised in the heavily forested areas of the Amazon basin, tropical Africa, parts of South-East Asia and North-East India. Farmers clear a patch of forest, grow crops with simple tools for a few years, and move to a new patch when the soil loses its fertility. As population grows and land becomes scarce, farmers return to the same patch sooner, and the cycle of jhum becomes shorter.
