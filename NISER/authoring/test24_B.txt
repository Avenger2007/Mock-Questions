sub_topic: Explaining how lymph forms from tissue fluid and what it does, and matching hypertension, coronary artery disease, angina and heart failure with their causes and features
Q: A physician explains to a class how lymph forms and describes four common disorders of the circulatory system. Which set of explanations is correct?
A) Lymph is blood without red cells that is pumped by the heart through lymph vessels; angina is the complete stoppage of the heart; heart failure is the same as cardiac arrest
B) Lymph carries red blood cells back to the heart; hypertension is a blood pressure of 120/80 mm Hg; coronary artery disease is caused by excess platelets
C) As blood passes through capillaries, some water and small solutes leave to form interstitial (tissue) fluid, which has the same mineral distribution as plasma; it is collected by lymphatic vessels as lymph, which carries absorbed fats (via lacteals in the intestinal villi) and lymphocytes for immunity and drains into major veins; hypertension is a pressure of 140/90 mm Hg or higher; coronary artery disease (atherosclerosis) is narrowing of the arteries supplying the heart muscle by deposits of calcium, fat, cholesterol and fibrous tissue; angina is acute chest pain when not enough oxygen reaches the heart muscle; heart failure is when the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the needs of the body, often with congestion of the lungs
D) Lymph is formed in the spleen and contains no white blood cells; heart failure means the heart has stopped beating completely; angina occurs only in children
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) Lymph forms from tissue fluid and returns it to veins carrying fats and lymphocytes; the four disorders are correctly described.
Concept: As blood passes through the capillaries in tissues, some water along with many small water-soluble substances move out into the spaces between the cells of tissues, leaving the larger proteins and most of the formed elements in the blood vessels; this fluid released out is called the interstitial fluid or tissue fluid, and it has the same mineral distribution as that in plasma. Exchange of nutrients, gases, etc., between the blood and the cells always occurs through this fluid. An elaborate network of vessels called the lymphatic system collects this fluid and drains it back to the major veins; the fluid present in the lymphatic system is called the lymph. Lymph is a colourless fluid containing specialised lymphocytes which are responsible for the immune responses of the body; it is also an important carrier for nutrients, hormones, etc., and fats are absorbed through lymph in the lacteals present in the intestinal villi. Hypertension is the term for blood pressure that is higher than normal (120/80); if repeated checks show a pressure of 140/90 mm Hg or higher, it shows hypertension, which leads to heart diseases and also affects vital organs like brain and kidney. Coronary artery disease (CAD), often referred to as atherosclerosis, affects the vessels that supply blood to the heart muscle; it is caused by deposits of calcium, fat, cholesterol and fibrous tissues, which make the lumen of arteries narrower. Angina (angina pectoris) is a symptom of acute chest pain that appears when not enough oxygen is reaching the heart muscle; it can occur in men and women of any age but is more common among the middle-aged and elderly. Heart failure means the state of heart when it is not pumping blood effectively enough to meet the needs of the body; it is sometimes called congestive heart failure because congestion of the lungs is one of its main symptoms. Heart failure is not the same as cardiac arrest (when the heart stops beating) or a heart attack (when the heart muscle is suddenly damaged by an inadequate blood supply).
Why: Tissue fluid that is not reabsorbed must be returned to the blood, and each disorder corresponds to a specific failure of supply or pumping.
Why not the others: (A) Lymph is not pumped by the heart; angina is chest pain, and heart failure differs from cardiac arrest. (B) Lymph has no RBCs; 120/80 is normal; CAD is due to deposits. (D) Lymph forms from tissue fluid and contains lymphocytes; heart failure is not arrest.
Key point: Tissue fluid → lymph (fats, lymphocytes) → veins; hypertension ≥ 140/90; CAD narrows coronary arteries; heart failure ≠ cardiac arrest.

Answer: (B) Uraemia, calculi and glomerulonephritis are correctly defined; dialysis removes urea by diffusion into a waste-free dialysing fluid; relatives' kidneys reduce rejection.
Concept: Malfunctioning of kidneys can lead to accumulation of urea in blood, a condition called uraemia, which is highly harmful and may lead to kidney failure. Renal calculi are stones or insoluble masses of crystallised salts (oxalates, etc.) formed within the kidney; glomerulonephritis is inflammation of the glomeruli of the kidney.
Why: Diffusion carries urea down its concentration gradient from blood into a fluid that lacks it, while plasma-like salts stay balanced.
Why not the others: (A) Uraemia is excess urea; dialysis removes urea; unrelated donors raise rejection risk. (C) Calculi are crystallised salts; dialysing fluid lacks urea. (D) Glomerulonephritis is inflammation of glomeruli; the fluid lacks nitrogenous wastes.
Key point: Uraemia = excess urea; dialysis = diffusion through cellophane into waste-free fluid with heparin/anti-heparin.

B) Any pollen can fertilise any ovule; the zygote has 12 chromosomes and the endosperm 24
C) The pollen tube enters through the chalaza and releases one male gamete, which fuses with three polar nuclei
D) Incompatible pollen fails because the stigma is dry; the endosperm is diploid with 24 chromosomes
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) Pollen–pistil interaction accepts compatible pollen; double fertilisation gives a 2n zygote (24) and a 3n PEN (36).
Concept: Pollination does not guarantee the transfer of the right type of pollen (compatible pollen of the same species as the stigma); often, pollen of the wrong type, either from other species or from the same plant (if it is self-incompatible), also land on the stigma. The pistil has the ability to recognise the pollen, whether it is of the right type (compatible) or of the wrong type (incompatible); if it is of the right type, the pistil accepts the pollen and promotes post-pollination events that lead to fertilisation, and if the pollen is of the wrong type, the pistil rejects the pollen by preventing pollen germination on the stigma or the pollen tube growth in the style. The ability of the pistil to recognise the pollen followed by its acceptance or rejection is the result of a continuous dialogue between pollen grain and the pistil, mediated by chemical components of the pollen interacting with those of the pistil. Following compatible pollination, the pollen grain germinates on the stigma to produce a pollen tube through one of the germ pores; the pollen tube grows through the tissues of the stigma and style and reaches the ovary, enters the ovule through the micropyle and then enters one of the synergids through the filiform apparatus, which guides the entry of the pollen tube. After entering a synergid, the pollen tube releases the two male gametes into the cytoplasm of the synergid; one of the male gametes moves towards the egg cell and fuses with its nucleus, thus completing syngamy, resulting in a diploid cell, the zygote; the other male gamete moves towards the two polar nuclei located in the central cell and fuses with them to produce a triploid primary endosperm nucleus (PEN); as this involves the fusion of three haploid nuclei it is termed triple fusion. Since two types of fusions, syngamy and triple fusion, take place in an embryo sac, the phenomenon is termed double fertilisation, an event unique to flowering plants.
Given: Egg nucleus n = 12.
Formula/Principle: Zygote = n (egg) + n (male gamete); PEN = n + n (polar nuclei) + n (male gamete).
Step 1: Zygote = 12 + 12 = 24 (2n).
Step 2: PEN = 12 + 12 + 12 = 36 (3n).
Step 3: Incompatible pollen is rejected at the stigma or style.
Why: Recognition chemistry decides acceptance, and two male gametes undergo two separate fusions in the same embryo sac.
Why not the others: (B) Pistils reject incompatible pollen; zygote is 2n and endosperm 3n. (C) Entry is usually through the micropyle and two gametes are released; there are two polar nuclei. (D) Rejection is chemical; endosperm is triploid.
Key point: Pollen–pistil dialogue decides compatibility; syngamy (2n) + triple fusion (3n) = double fertilisation.

Concept: Mendel's studies mainly described traits that have distinct alternate forms such as flower colour, which are either purple or white; but if you look around you will find that there are many traits which are not so distinct in their occurrence and are spread across a gradient — for example, in humans, height is not just tall or short but a whole range of possible heights. Such traits are generally controlled by three or more genes and are thus called polygenic traits. Human skin colour is another classic example; in a polygenic trait the phenotype reflects the contribution of each allele, i.e., the effect of each allele is additive. Assume three genes A, B, C control skin colour in humans, with the dominant forms A, B and C responsible for dark skin colour and the recessive forms a, b and c for light skin colour; the genotype with all the dominant alleles (AABBCC) will have the darkest skin colour and that with all the recessive alleles (aabbcc) will have the lightest skin colour; as expected, the genotype with three dominant alleles and three recessive alleles will have an intermediate skin colour. Step 3: k = 3: C(6,3)/64 = 20/64.
Why: With additive alleles only the count of dominant alleles matters, so classes follow binomial coefficients and form a bell-shaped distribution.
Why not the others: (A) and (B) are for one or two genes with complete dominance. (D) 27/64 is the fraction showing all three dominant traits in a Mendelian trihybrid, not additive classes.

Q: E. coli grown for many generations in ¹⁵NH₄Cl is transferred to ¹⁴NH₄Cl. DNA is extracted after each generation (about 20 minutes) and centrifuged in a CsCl density gradient. Which result and conclusion are correct?
A) After one generation two bands, heavy and light, appear in equal amounts, proving conservative replication
B) After one generation (20 min) a single hybrid (intermediate) band appears; after two generations (40 min) equal amounts of hybrid and light DNA; after three generations hybrid : light = 1 : 3; this shows that DNA replicates semi-conservatively, each daughter molecule having one parental and one new strand; replication is carried out by DNA-dependent DNA polymerase in the 5'→3' direction, continuously on the leading strand and discontinuously (Okazaki fragments joined by DNA ligase) on the lagging strand, beginning at a defined origin of replication
C) The heavy band persists unchanged in every generation, showing DNA does not replicate
D) After two generations all DNA is light, proving dispersive replication
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) 1st generation: all hybrid; 2nd: 1 hybrid : 1 light; 3rd: 1 hybrid : 3 light → semi-conservative replication.
Concept: Watson and Crick proposed that the two strands would separate and act as a template for the synthesis of new complementary strands; after the completion of replication, each DNA molecule would have one parental and one newly synthesised strand, a scheme termed semi-conservative replication. Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl performed the following experiment in 1958: they grew E. coli in a medium containing ¹⁵NH₄Cl (¹⁵N being the heavy isotope of nitrogen) as the only nitrogen source for many generations, so ¹⁵N was incorporated into newly synthesised DNA; this heavy DNA molecule could be distinguished from normal DNA by centrifugation in a caesium chloride (CsCl) density gradient. Then they transferred the cells into a medium with normal ¹⁴NH₄Cl and took samples at various definite time intervals as the cells multiplied, extracting the DNA that remained as double-stranded helices; the various samples were separated independently on CsCl gradients to measure the densities of DNA. The DNA extracted from the culture one generation after the transfer from ¹⁵N to ¹⁴N medium (that is after 20 minutes; E. coli divides in 20 minutes) had a hybrid or intermediate density; DNA extracted from the culture after another generation (after 40 minutes, II generation) was composed of equal amounts of this hybrid DNA and of 'light' DNA. In living cells such as E. coli, the process of replication requires a set of catalysts (enzymes); the main enzyme is referred to as DNA-dependent DNA polymerase, since it uses a DNA template to catalyse the polymerisation of deoxynucleotides. DNA polymerases polymerise only in one direction, that is 5'→3'; consequently on one strand (the template with polarity 3'→5') replication is continuous, while on the other (template with polarity 5'→3') it is discontinuous, and the discontinuously synthesised fragments are later joined by the enzyme DNA ligase. Replication does not initiate randomly at any place in DNA; there is a definite region in E. coli DNA where replication originates, called the origin of replication.
Given: 2 heavy parental strands; generations n = 1, 2, 3.
Formula/Principle: After n generations there are 2ⁿ molecules, of which always 2 are hybrid.
Step 1: n = 1: 2 molecules, both hybrid.
Step 2: n = 2: 4 molecules, 2 hybrid + 2 light ⇒ 1 : 1.
Step 3: n = 3: 8 molecules, 2 hybrid + 6 light ⇒ 1 : 3.
Why: The two original heavy strands are never broken or lost; each ends up paired with a new light strand.
Why not the others: (A) Conservative replication would give heavy + light bands; observed was a single hybrid band. (C) DNA does replicate. (D) Hybrid DNA persists; dispersive replication would not give a distinct light band.
Key point: Meselson–Stahl: hybrid after 1 generation, 1 hybrid : 1 light after 2 ⇒ semi-conservative.

In transcription only a segment of DNA and only one of the strands is copied into RNA. A transcription unit in DNA is defined primarily by three regions: a promoter, the structural gene and a terminator. Q: In a randomly mating population, 16% of individuals show a recessive phenotype (aa). Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, find the frequency of allele A, the percentage of heterozygotes, and identify the correct statement about the principle.
A) p = 0.84; heterozygotes 16%; equilibrium is maintained even with strong natural selection
B) p = 0.4; heterozygotes 24%; genetic drift increases equilibrium
C) p = 0.16; heterozygotes 84%; mutation never affects allele frequencies
D) q = 0.4, p = 0.6; heterozygotes 2pq = 48%; allele frequencies stay constant from generation to generation unless disturbed by gene migration (gene flow), genetic drift, mutation, genetic recombination or natural selection, and a deviation from p² + 2pq + q² = 1 measures the extent of evolutionary change
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) p = 0.6, 2pq = 48%; five factors disturb equilibrium.
Concept: In a given population one can find out the frequency of occurrence of alleles of a gene or a locus; this frequency is supposed to remain fixed and even remain the same through generations. Hardy–Weinberg principle stated it using algebraic equations: allele frequencies in a population are stable and constant from generation to generation; the gene pool (total genes and their alleles in a population) remains a constant, which is called genetic equilibrium, and the sum total of all the allelic frequencies is 1. Individual frequencies, for example, can be named p, q, etc.; in a diploid, p and q represent the frequency of allele A and allele a; the frequency of AA individuals in a population is simply p², that of aa is q² and that of Aa is 2pq; hence p² + 2pq + q² = 1, which is a binomial expansion of (p + q)². When frequency measured differs from expected values, the difference (direction) indicates the extent of evolutionary change; disturbance in genetic equilibrium, or Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, i.e., change of frequency of alleles in a population, would then be interpreted as resulting in evolution. Five factors are known to affect Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium: gene migration or gene flow, genetic drift, mutation, genetic recombination and natural selection.
Given: q² = 0.16.
Formula/Principle: p + q = 1; p² + 2pq + q² = 1.

C) By Blackman's law of limiting factors, if a chemical process is affected by more than one factor its rate is determined by the factor nearest its minimal value; on the rising part of each curve light is limiting, and on the plateau CO₂ becomes limiting, so raising CO₂ raises the plateau; light saturation occurs at about 10% of full sunlight, so except for shaded plants light is rarely limiting in nature; CO₂ is the major limiting factor because it is only about 0.03–0.04% of the atmosphere, and C3 plants respond to higher CO₂ concentration (up to about 0.05%) with increased photosynthesis, which is why tomatoes and bell peppers in greenhouses are grown in a CO₂-enriched atmosphere for higher yields; C4 plants show saturation at about 360 µL L⁻¹
D) The plateau shows the leaf has died from excess light
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) Light limits the rising part; CO₂ limits the plateau (Blackman's law); CO₂ enrichment raises yields of C3 crops.
Concept: The rate of photosynthesis is very important in determining the yield of plants, including crop plants; photosynthesis is under the influence of several factors, both internal (plant) and external. The plant factors include the number, size, age and orientation of leaves, mesophyll cells and chloroplasts, internal CO₂ concentration and the amount of chlorophyll; the external factors include the availability of sunlight, temperature, CO₂ concentration and water. As a plant photosynthesises, all these factors will simultaneously affect its rate; hence, though several factors interact and simultaneously affect photosynthesis or CO₂ fixation, usually one factor is the major cause or is the one that limits the rate. Hence, at any point the rate will be determined by the factor available at sub-optimal levels. When several factors affect any [bio]chemical process, Blackman's (1905) Law of Limiting Factors comes into effect: if a chemical process is affected by more than one factor, then its rate will be determined by the factor which is nearest to its minimal value; it is the factor which directly affects the process if its quantity is changed. There is a linear relationship between incident light and CO₂ fixation rates at low light intensities; at higher light intensities, gradually the rate does not show further increase as other factors become limiting. Interestingly, light saturation occurs at 10 per cent of the full sunlight; hence, except for plants in shade or in dense forests, light is rarely a limiting factor in nature; increase in incident light beyond a point causes the breakdown of chlorophyll and a decrease in photosynthesis. Carbon dioxide is the major limiting factor for photosynthesis; the concentration of CO₂ is very low in the atmosphere (between 0.03 and 0.04 per cent), and increase in concentration up to 0.05 per cent can cause an increase in CO₂ fixation rates; beyond this the levels can become damaging over longer periods. C3 and C4 plants respond differently to CO₂ concentrations: at low light conditions neither group responds to high CO₂ conditions; at high light intensities both C3 and C4 plants show increase in the rates of photosynthesis. What is important to note is that C4 plants show saturation at about 360 µL L⁻¹ while C3 respond to increased CO₂ concentration and saturation is seen only beyond 450 µL L⁻¹; thus, current availability of CO₂ levels is limiting to C3 plants. The fact that C3 plants respond to higher CO₂ concentration by showing increased rates of photosynthesis leading to higher productivity has been used for some greenhouse crops such as tomatoes and bell pepper; they are allowed to grow in carbon dioxide enriched atmosphere that leads to higher yields.
Why: The rate is set by whichever factor is in shortest supply, so changing the non-limiting factor has no effect while changing the limiting one shifts the curve.
Why not the others: (A) Raising CO₂ raises the plateau. (B) The rate saturates. (D) The plateau reflects a limiting factor, not death.
Key point: Blackman: the factor nearest its minimum limits the rate; CO₂ is the main limiting factor; greenhouse CO₂ enrichment for C3 crops.

Q: Which statement about differentiation in plants is correct?
A) Once a plant cell has differentiated, it can never divide again under any condition
B) Differentiation is the maturation of meristem-derived cells for specific functions, as when tracheary elements lose their protoplasm and develop strong lignocellulosic secondary walls to carry water; living differentiated cells that have lost the capacity to divide can regain it (dedifferentiation), as in the formation of interfascicular cambium and cork cambium from fully differentiated parenchyma cells; the new meristematic tissues then produce cells that again lose the capacity to divide and mature for specific functions (redifferentiation), such as secondary xylem, secondary phloem and cork; plant growth is open, as cells from the same meristem mature into different structures depending on their location within the plant
C) Dedifferentiation produces dead cells; tracheary elements retain their protoplasm
D) Redifferentiation means cells go back to being meristematic permanently
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) Differentiation, dedifferentiation (interfascicular and cork cambium) and redifferentiation (secondary tissues) correctly described; growth is open.
Concept: The cells derived from root apical and shoot-apical meristems and cambium differentiate and mature to perform specific functions; this act leading to maturation is termed differentiation. During differentiation, cells undergo a few to major structural changes both in their cell walls and protoplasm; for example, to form a tracheary element, the cells would lose their protoplasm, and they also develop a very strong, elastic, lignocellulosic secondary cell wall to carry water to long distances even under extreme tension. Plants show another interesting phenomenon: the living differentiated cells, that by now have lost the capacity to divide, can regain the capacity of division under certain conditions; this phenomenon is termed dedifferentiation — for example, formation of meristems, the interfascicular cambium and cork cambium, from fully differentiated parenchyma cells. While doing so, such meristems or tissues are able to divide and produce cells that once again lose the capacity to divide but mature to perform specific functions, i.e., get redifferentiated. Plant growth is open, i.e., it can be indeterminate or determinate; differentiation in plants is open because cells or tissues arising out of the same meristem have different structures at maturity; the final structure at maturity of a cell or tissue is also determined by the location of the cell within it — for example, cells positioned away from root apical meristems differentiate as root-cap cells, while those pushed to the periphery mature as epidermis.
Why: Plant cells retain the ability to switch between dividing and specialised states, allowing new meristems to arise from mature tissue.
Why not the others: (A) Living differentiated cells can dedifferentiate. (C) Dedifferentiated cells divide actively; tracheary elements lose protoplasm. (D) Redifferentiation means maturing again for a specific function.
Key point: Differentiation → dedifferentiation (cambium from parenchyma) → redifferentiation (secondary tissues).

Q: A healthy adult has tidal volume 500 mL, inspiratory reserve volume 3000 mL, expiratory reserve volume 1100 mL and residual volume 1200 mL, and breathes 12 times a minute at rest. Find the vital capacity, inspiratory capacity, functional residual capacity, total lung capacity and the volume of air breathed per minute.
A) 4600 mL; 3500 mL; 2300 mL; 5800 mL; 6000 mL min⁻¹ — vital capacity being the maximum air breathed out after a forcible inspiration, and residual volume the air that remains even after forcible expiration and so cannot be measured by a simple spirometer
B) 3500 mL; 4600 mL; 1200 mL; 4600 mL; 500 mL min⁻¹
C) 5800 mL; 3000 mL; 1100 mL; 6000 mL; 12 000 mL min⁻¹
D) 4100 mL; 3500 mL; 2300 mL; 4600 mL; 6000 mL min⁻¹
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) VC 4600 mL; IC 3500 mL; FRC 2300 mL; TLC 5800 mL; 6000 mL min⁻¹.
Concept: Volume of air involved in breathing movements can be estimated by using a spirometer, which helps in the clinical assessment of pulmonary functions. Tidal Volume (TV) is the volume of air inspired or expired during a normal respiration, approximately 500 mL, i.e., a healthy man can inspire or expire approximately 6000 to 8000 mL of air per minute. Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV) is the additional volume of air a person can inspire by a forcible inspiration, averaging 2500 mL to 3000 mL; Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV) is the additional volume of air a person can expire by a forcible expiration, averaging 1000 mL to 1100 mL; Residual Volume (RV) is the volume of air remaining in the lungs even after a forcible expiration, averaging 1100 mL to 1200 mL. By adding up a few respiratory volumes, one can derive various pulmonary capacities which can be used in clinical diagnosis: Inspiratory Capacity (IC) is the total volume of air a person can inspire after a normal expiration, which includes tidal volume and inspiratory reserve volume (TV + IRV); Expiratory Capacity (EC) is the total volume of air a person can expire after a normal inspiration (TV + ERV); Functional Residual Capacity (FRC) is the volume of air that will remain in the lungs after a normal expiration (ERV + RV); Vital Capacity (VC) is the maximum volume of air a person can breathe in after a forced expiration, or the maximum volume of air a person can breathe out after a forced inspiration (ERV + TV + IRV); Total Lung Capacity (TLC) is the total volume of air accommodated in the lungs at the end of a forced inspiration (RV + ERV + TV + IRV or VC + RV).
Given: TV 500, IRV 3000, ERV 1100, RV 1200 mL; 12 breaths min⁻¹.
Formula/Principle: VC = IRV + TV + ERV; IC = TV + IRV; FRC = ERV + RV; TLC = VC + RV; minute volume = TV × rate.
Step 1: VC = 3000 + 500 + 1100 = 4600 mL; IC = 500 + 3000 = 3500 mL.
Step 2: FRC = 1100 + 1200 = 2300 mL; TLC = 4600 + 1200 = 5800 mL.
Step 3: Minute volume = 500 × 12 = 6000 mL min⁻¹.
Why: Capacities are sums of the non-overlapping volumes, and residual volume is included only in FRC and TLC.
Why not the others: (B) IC and VC swapped; FRC and TLC wrong; minute volume ignores rate. (C) VC excludes RV; IC includes TV. (D) VC omits TV; TLC omits RV.
Key point: VC = IRV + TV + ERV; FRC = ERV + RV; TLC = VC + RV.
