D) A species is aromatic if it is planar, cyclic, has complete delocalisation of π electrons and contains (4n + 2) π electrons; benzene (6π, n = 1), the cyclopentadienyl anion (6π) and the cycloheptatrienyl (tropylium) cation (6π) are aromatic, cyclopentadiene is not (an sp³ carbon breaks the delocalisation), and cyclooctatetraene (8π) is not aromatic since it adopts a non-planar tub shape; aromaticity explains benzene's unusual stability and preference for substitution over addition
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) Planar, cyclic, fully conjugated, (4n + 2) π electrons; benzene, C₅H₅⁻, C₇H₇⁺ aromatic; C₅H₆ and C₈H₈ not.
Concept: Benzene was considered the parent 'aromatic' compound; now the name is applied to all the ring systems, whether or not having a benzene ring, possessing the following characteristics: planarity; complete delocalisation of the π electrons in the ring; presence of (4n + 2) π electrons in the ring, where n is an integer (n = 0, 1, 2, …); this is often referred to as Hückel's rule. Benzene has 6 π electrons delocalised over a planar ring of six sp² carbons, so it is aromatic; the resulting stability explains why benzene, despite its unsaturation, undergoes substitution rather than addition reactions. In cyclopentadiene one ring carbon is sp³, so the π system is not continuous; removing a proton gives the planar cyclopentadienyl anion with 6 π electrons, which is aromatic. The cycloheptatrienyl cation has 6 π electrons over seven sp² carbons and is aromatic. Cyclooctatetraene has 8 π electrons (4n, not 4n + 2) and is not planar (tub shaped), so it is not aromatic and behaves like an ordinary alkene.
Why: Only a closed, planar loop of p orbitals holding 2, 6, 10 … π electrons fills all bonding molecular orbitals, giving special stability.
Why not the others: (A) 8 π electrons and non-planar. (B) The sp³ carbon interrupts conjugation. (C) Aromaticity is defined by structure, not smell.
Key point: Hückel: planar + cyclic + conjugated + (4n + 2) π electrons.

sub_topic: Interpreting laboratory observations on nitration of benzene, toluene and nitrobenzene in terms of the electrophile, the arenium-ion mechanism and activating or deactivating substituents
Q: A student nitrates benzene with a mixture of concentrated HNO₃ and H₂SO₄ at about 330 K. Toluene reacts faster under milder conditions and gives mainly o- and p-nitrotoluene; nitrobenzene needs a higher temperature and gives mainly m-dinitrobenzene. Which interpretation is correct?
A) HNO₃ alone is the electrophile, and the methyl group deactivates the ring
B) H₂SO₄ protonates HNO₃, which loses water to give the nitronium ion NO₂⁺ (electrophile); NO₂⁺ attacks the ring to form a carbocation, the arenium ion or σ-complex, in which one carbon is sp³ and aromaticity is lost (slow step); loss of H⁺ to HSO₄⁻ restores aromaticity; –CH₃ is electron-donating (+I and hyperconjugation), activating the ring and directing to ortho and para positions, while –NO₂ withdraws electrons (−I, −R), deactivating the ring and directing to the meta position
C) The arenium ion is aromatic, and its formation is fast
D) The nitro group activates the ring and directs to ortho and para positions
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) NO₂⁺ electrophile; arenium ion (slow) then loss of H⁺; –CH₃ activating o/p; –NO₂ deactivating m.
Concept: Benzene shows electrophilic substitution reactions; the mechanism involves three steps: (a) generation of the electrophile, (b) formation of a carbocation intermediate, (c) removal of a proton from the carbocation intermediate. In nitration, a mixture of concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulphuric acid (nitrating mixture) is used; sulphuric acid protonates nitric acid, which then loses water to produce the nitronium ion: HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ ⇌ NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻. The electrophile attacks the aromatic ring to form a σ-complex or arenium ion, in which one of the carbons is sp³ hybridised; the arenium ion gets stabilised by resonance, but it loses its aromatic character because delocalisation of electrons stops at the sp³ carbon; to restore the aromatic character, the σ-complex releases a proton from the sp³ carbon on attack by HSO₄⁻. Substituents already present on the ring influence both the reactivity and the position of further substitution: groups such as –CH₃, –OH, –NH₂ increase the electron density of the ring, especially at ortho and para positions (activating, o/p-directing); groups such as –NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COOH withdraw electrons, decrease the electron density mainly at ortho and para positions and make the meta position relatively richer (deactivating, m-directing).
Step 1: Generate NO₂⁺ from the nitrating mixture.
Step 2: NO₂⁺ + C₆H₆ → arenium ion (slow, aromaticity lost).
Step 3: Loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity; substituents decide rate and position.
Why: The slow step forms a positively charged intermediate, so electron-donating groups speed it up and stabilise attack at o/p, while withdrawing groups slow it and favour meta.
Why not the others: (A) NO₂⁺ is the electrophile; –CH₃ activates. (C) The arenium ion is not aromatic and forms slowly. (D) –NO₂ deactivates and directs meta.
Key point: Nitronium ion electrophile; σ-complex intermediate; activators o/p, deactivators (–NO₂) meta.
