sub_topic: Counting the hormones made by the anterior pituitary, the pars intermedia and the hypothalamic neurons whose hormones are stored in the posterior pituitary
Q: Consider this list: GH, prolactin, TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH, MSH, oxytocin, vasopressin (ADH), GnRH. How many are synthesised and secreted by the pars distalis (anterior pituitary), and how many are made by hypothalamic neurons but released from the neurohypophysis?
A) 8 and 2
B) 7 and 3
C) 4 and 2
D) 6 and 2
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) 6 from the pars distalis; 2 (oxytocin, vasopressin) released from the neurohypophysis.
Concept: The pituitary gland is located in a bony cavity called sella turcica and is attached to the hypothalamus by a stalk. It is divided anatomically into an adenohypophysis and a neurohypophysis. The adenohypophysis consists of two portions, pars distalis and pars intermedia; the pars distalis region, commonly called the anterior pituitary, produces growth hormone (GH), prolactin (PRL), thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH), luteinising hormone (LH) and follicle stimulating hormone (FSH). The pars intermedia secretes only one hormone, melanocyte stimulating hormone (MSH); in humans it is almost merged with the pars distalis. The neurohypophysis (pars nervosa), also known as the posterior pituitary, stores and releases two hormones, oxytocin and vasopressin, which are actually synthesised by the hypothalamus and transported axonally to the neurohypophysis. The hypothalamus contains groups of neurosecretory cells that produce releasing hormones (e.g. gonadotrophin releasing hormone, GnRH) and inhibiting hormones (e.g. somatostatin), which reach the pituitary through a portal circulatory system and regulate the functions of the anterior pituitary.
Given: Ten hormones listed.
Formula/Principle: Classify each hormone by its site of synthesis and site of release.
Step 1: Pars distalis: GH, PRL, TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH ⇒ 6.
Step 2: Made in hypothalamus, released from neurohypophysis: oxytocin, vasopressin ⇒ 2.
Step 3: Remaining: MSH (pars intermedia) and GnRH (hypothalamic releasing hormone) ⇒ 6 + 2 + 2 = 10 ✓.
Why: The neurohypophysis is nervous tissue that only stores and releases hormones made by hypothalamic neurons, while the adenohypophysis is glandular tissue that makes its own hormones under hypothalamic control.
Why not the others: (A) Counts MSH and GnRH as anterior pituitary products. (B) Miscounts the posterior lobe. (C) Omits several tropic hormones.
Key point: Anterior pituitary: GH, PRL, TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH; posterior pituitary releases oxytocin and ADH made in the hypothalamus.

Answer: (A) Sporogenous tissue → MMC → meiosis → tetrad → pollen; exine of sporopollenin (with germ pores), intine of cellulose and pectin; vegetative and generative cells.
Concept: When the anther is young, a group of compactly arranged homogenous cells called the sporogenous tissue occupies the centre of each microsporangium. As the anther develops, the cells of the sporogenous tissue undergo meiotic divisions to form microspore tetrads; each cell of the sporogenous tissue is capable of giving rise to a microspore tetrad and is a potential pollen or microspore mother cell. The process of formation of microspores from a pollen mother cell through meiosis is called microsporogenesis. As the anthers mature and dehydrate, the microspores dissociate from each other and develop into pollen grains. The hard outer layer, the exine, is made up of sporopollenin, one of the most resistant organic materials known; pollen grain exine has prominent apertures called germ pores where sporopollenin is absent. The inner wall, the intine, is a thin and continuous layer made up of cellulose and pectin. When the pollen grain is mature it contains two cells, the vegetative cell and the generative cell; the vegetative cell is bigger, has abundant food reserve and a large irregularly shaped nucleus, while the generative cell is small, floats in the cytoplasm of the vegetative cell, and is spindle shaped with dense cytoplasm and a nucleus. In over 60 per cent of angiosperms, pollen grains are shed at this 2-celled stage; in the remaining species, the generative cell divides mitotically to give rise to the two male gametes before pollen grains are shed (3-celled stage).
Step 1: Diploid MMC undergoes meiosis ⇒ four haploid microspores (tetrad).
Step 2: Microspores separate and develop walls ⇒ pollen grains.
Step 3: Pollen nucleus divides unequally ⇒ large vegetative cell + small generative cell; generative cell later forms two male gametes.
Why: Meiosis halves the chromosome number so that the male gametes are haploid; sporopollenin protects pollen, while germ pores allow the pollen tube to emerge.
Why not the others: (B) Tetrads arise by meiosis, so pollen is haploid. (C) Wall materials reversed. (D) Roles of the two cells reversed.
Key point: MMC (2n) → meiosis → microspores (n) → pollen with vegetative + generative cells; exine = sporopollenin.

Q: Microscope sections of a human ovary show many primary follicles with a large central cell arrested in prophase of meiosis I, and a mature Graafian follicle. A cell released at ovulation is examined later in the oviduct. Which interpretation is correct?
A) The cell released at ovulation is a mature ovum that has completed both meiotic divisions
B) New primary oocytes are formed throughout a woman's life by mitosis of oogonia
C) The primary oocytes were formed during fetal life and stay arrested in prophase I; in the Graafian follicle the primary oocyte completes meiosis I, forming a large haploid secondary oocyte and a tiny first polar body; the secondary oocyte is released at ovulation and completes meiosis II only after a sperm enters it, producing the ovum and a second polar body; so one primary oocyte yields only one ovum
D) Meiosis II is completed before ovulation, so the released cell has 46 chromosomes
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) Primary oocytes arrested in prophase I since fetal life; secondary oocyte ovulated; meiosis II completed after sperm entry; one ovum per primary oocyte.
Concept: The process of formation of a mature female gamete is called oogenesis. Oogenesis is initiated during the embryonic development stage when a couple of million gamete mother cells (oogonia) are formed within each fetal ovary; no more oogonia are formed and added after birth. These cells start division and enter into prophase-I of the meiotic division and get temporarily arrested at that stage, called primary oocytes. Each primary oocyte then gets surrounded by a layer of granulosa cells and is called the primary follicle. A large number of these follicles degenerate during the phase from birth to puberty; therefore, at puberty only 60,000–80,000 primary follicles are left in each ovary. The primary oocyte within the tertiary follicle grows in size and completes its first meiotic division; it is an unequal division resulting in the formation of a large haploid secondary oocyte and a tiny first polar body. The tertiary follicle further changes into the mature follicle or Graafian follicle; the Graafian follicle now ruptures to release the secondary oocyte (ovum) from the ovary by the process called ovulation. The entry of sperm into the ovum induces the completion of the meiotic division of the secondary oocyte; the second meiotic division is also unequal and results in the formation of a second polar body and a haploid ovum (ootid).
Step 1: Arrest in prophase I seen in primary follicles ⇒ primary oocytes formed before birth.
Step 2: Meiosis I completed in the maturing follicle ⇒ secondary oocyte + first polar body.
Step 3: Meiosis II completed only on sperm entry ⇒ ovum + second polar body.
Why: Unequal divisions concentrate cytoplasm in one cell, so the single ovum has enough reserves for early development.
Why not the others: (A) The ovulated cell is a secondary oocyte arrested in meiosis II. (B) No new oogonia form after birth. (D) Meiosis II follows sperm entry, and the cell is haploid.
Key point: Primary oocyte (prophase I arrest, fetal) → secondary oocyte (ovulated) → ovum after sperm entry.

B) Such polygenic traits are controlled by several genes whose alleles have additive effects, each dominant allele adding a small equal amount; with n genes, a cross between two individuals heterozygous for all of them gives 2n + 1 phenotypic classes and the two extreme classes each form (1/4)ⁿ of the progeny, so with many genes and environmental influence the classes merge into a continuous bell-shaped distribution

SOL:
Answer: (B) Several genes with additive effects; 2n + 1 classes; extremes (1/4)ⁿ each; environment blurs classes into a continuous curve.
Concept: Generally traits controlled by three or more genes are called polygenic traits; besides the involvement of multiple genes, polygenic inheritance also takes into account the influence of environment. Human skin colour is another classic example: assume that three genes A, B, C control skin colour in humans, with the dominant forms A, B and C responsible for dark skin colour and the recessive forms a, b and c for light skin colour; the genotype with all the dominant alleles (AABBCC) will have the darkest skin colour and that with all the recessive alleles (aabbcc) the lightest; a genotype with three dominant and three recessive alleles will have an intermediate skin colour. In this manner, the number of each type of allele in the genotype would determine the darkness or lightness of the skin of an individual. In polygenic inheritance the phenotype reflects the contribution of each allele, i.e. the effect of each allele is additive.

Step 1: One gene (Aa × Aa): 3 classes; extremes 1/4 each.
Step 2: Two genes: 5 classes (1 : 4 : 6 : 4 : 1); extremes 1/16 each.
Step 3: Three genes: 7 classes (1 : 6 : 15 : 20 : 15 : 6 : 1); extremes 1/64 each.
Why: Many small additive contributions, plus environmental effects such as nutrition and sunlight, produce a near-normal distribution in which neighbouring classes overlap.
Why not the others: (A) Multiple alleles of one gene give discrete classes, as in ABO groups. (C) Incomplete dominance at one locus gives only three classes. (D) Environment influences polygenic traits.
Key point: Polygenic traits: additive genes + environment ⇒ continuous variation; 2n + 1 classes.

Q: A mature eukaryotic mRNA is allowed to pair with the single-stranded DNA of its own gene and viewed under the electron microscope; several loops of unpaired DNA appear. The same experiment with a bacterial gene shows no loops. Which interpretation is correct?
A) The loops are regions of DNA that code for the 5′ cap and poly(A) tail
B) Bacterial mRNA is processed more extensively than eukaryotic mRNA
C) The loops are introns: eukaryotic genes are split, and the primary transcript (hnRNA) has its introns removed by splicing, is capped with methyl guanosine triphosphate at the 5′ end and given a poly(A) tail at the 3′ end; in eukaryotes RNA polymerase II transcribes mRNA precursors, polymerase I rRNAs (28S, 18S, 5.8S) and polymerase III tRNA, 5S rRNA and snRNAs; bacteria have one RNA polymerase (with σ for initiation and ρ for termination), their genes lack introns, and transcription and translation can be coupled in the cytoplasm
D) Eukaryotes have only one RNA polymerase, so their genes must contain loops
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) Loops are introns removed by splicing; capping and tailing; three eukaryotic RNA polymerases; bacteria have one polymerase, no introns, coupled transcription–translation.
Concept: In bacteria there is a single DNA-dependent RNA polymerase that catalyses transcription of all types of RNA; RNA polymerase associates transiently with the initiation factor (σ) and termination factor (ρ) to initiate and terminate the transcription respectively. In bacteria, since the mRNA does not require any processing to become active, and since transcription and translation take place in the same compartment (there is no separation of cytosol and nucleus in bacteria), many times translation can begin much before the mRNA is fully transcribed. In eukaryotes there are at least three RNA polymerases in the nucleus (in addition to the RNA polymerase found in the organelles): RNA polymerase I transcribes rRNAs (28S, 18S and 5.8S), RNA polymerase III is responsible for transcription of tRNA, 5S rRNA and snRNAs (small nuclear RNAs), and RNA polymerase II transcribes the precursor of mRNA, the heterogeneous nuclear RNA (hnRNA). The primary transcripts contain both the exons and the introns and are non-functional; hence they are subjected to a process called splicing where the introns are removed and exons are joined in a defined order. hnRNA undergoes two additional processes called capping and tailing: in capping an unusual nucleotide (methyl guanosine triphosphate) is added to the 5′-end of hnRNA, and in tailing adenylate residues (200–300) are added at the 3′-end in a template-independent manner.
Step 1: Loops = DNA segments with no complementary sequence in mature mRNA.
Step 2: Such segments are introns, removed from the primary transcript by splicing.
Step 3: No loops for the bacterial gene ⇒ bacterial genes are not split.
Why: The hybridisation directly reveals which parts of the gene survive in the mature message, showing that eukaryotic genes are interrupted.
Why not the others: (A) The cap and tail are added without a DNA template. (B) Bacterial mRNA is not processed in this way. (D) Eukaryotes have three nuclear RNA polymerases.
Key point: Eukaryotic genes are split (exons + introns); hnRNA → splicing, capping, tailing → mRNA.

Q: Which explanation of evidences of evolution is correct?
A) Thorns of Bougainvillea and tendrils of Cucurbita are homologous, showing divergent evolution from a common ancestral structure; the eyes of the octopus and of mammals, and the flippers of penguins and dolphins, are analogous, performing similar functions but differing in anatomy because similar habitats selected similar adaptations (convergent evolution); sweet potato (root) and potato (stem) are likewise analogous; similarities in proteins and genes across organisms give biochemical evidence of common ancestry; Haeckel's proposal that embryos of higher animals pass through adult stages of other animals was disproved by Karl Ernst von Baer
B) The wings of a butterfly and of a bird are homologous, because both are used for flying
C) Analogous structures prove that the organisms share a recent common ancestor
D) The forelimbs of whales and bats are analogous, because they perform different functions

Answer: (A) Thorns/tendrils homologous (divergent); octopus/mammal eyes, penguin/dolphin flippers, sweet potato/potato analogous (convergent); biochemical similarities; Haeckel's idea disproved by von Baer.
Concept: Comparative anatomy and morphology shows similarities and differences among organisms of today and those that existed years ago. Whales, bats, cheetah and human share similarities in the pattern of bones of forelimbs; though these forelimbs perform different functions, they have similar anatomical structure — they are homologous organs, and homology indicates common ancestry; this is divergent evolution. In plants also, the thorns and tendrils of Bougainvillea and Cucurbita represent homology. Wings of butterfly and of birds look alike; they are not anatomically similar structures though they perform similar functions — analogous structures, the result of convergent evolution, different structures evolving for the same function and hence having similarity. Other examples of analogy are the eye of the octopus and of mammals, or the flippers of penguins and dolphins; sweet potato (root modification) and potato (stem modification) is another example. In the same line of argument, similarities in proteins and genes performing a given function among diverse organisms give clues to common ancestry; these biochemical similarities point to the same shared ancestry as structural similarities among diverse organisms. Embryological support for evolution was also proposed by Ernst Haeckel based upon the observation of certain features during the embryonic stage common to all vertebrates that are absent in adults; this proposal was disapproved on careful study performed by Karl Ernst von Baer, who noted that embryos never pass through the adult stages of other animals.
Why: Shared structure with different function reflects inheritance from a common ancestor; shared function with different structure reflects similar selection pressures on unrelated lineages.
Why not the others: (B) Butterfly and bird wings are analogous. (C) Analogy reflects convergence, not common ancestry. (D) Whale and bat forelimbs are homologous.
Key point: Homology → divergent evolution, common ancestry; analogy → convergent evolution.
