sub_topic: Comparing taxonomy with systematics and the biological species concept with identification by morphology alone
Q: A student says: "Taxonomy and systematics mean exactly the same thing, and two populations that look alike must belong to one species." Which comparison corrects the student?
A) Taxonomy deals only with naming, while systematics deals only with collecting specimens; a species is any group living in one place
B) Systematics is the study of fossils alone; species are recognised only by the shape of their leaves or bones
C) Taxonomy covers characterisation, identification, nomenclature and classification of organisms, whereas systematics is broader and also takes account of evolutionary relationships between organisms; a species is a group of individuals with fundamental similarities, and populations that interbreed freely in nature to produce fertile offspring belong to one species even if they differ in appearance, while look-alike populations that are reproductively isolated are different species
D) Taxonomy and systematics are identical; two populations that cannot interbreed must belong to the same genus and the same species
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) Systematics adds evolutionary relationships to taxonomy, and a species is defined by fundamental similarity and natural interbreeding, not by looks alone.
Concept: Characterisation, identification, nomenclature and classification are the processes basic to taxonomy. The word systematics comes from the Latin "systema", meaning systematic arrangement; Linnaeus used Systema Naturae as the title of his publication. The scope of systematics was later enlarged to include identification, nomenclature and classification, and systematics takes into account evolutionary relationships between organisms. Taxonomic studies treat a group of individual organisms with fundamental similarities as a species. One can distinguish one species from a closely related species on the basis of distinct morphological differences, but under the biological species concept the decisive test is whether populations interbreed in nature and produce fertile offspring.
Why: Appearance can mislead in both directions. Breeds of dogs look very different yet interbreed, so they are one species; some sibling species look almost identical but do not interbreed, so they are separate species.
Why not the others: (A) Taxonomy is more than naming, and systematics is not specimen collecting; species are not defined by locality. (B) Systematics is not limited to fossils, and morphology is not the only criterion. (D) The two terms differ in scope, and reproductive isolation marks different species.
Key point: Systematics = taxonomy + evolutionary relationships; species = interbreeding, fertile populations with fundamental similarities.

sub_topic: Using base pairing to find the composition of the complementary strand and of the whole duplex from the composition of one DNA strand, and recognising single-stranded genomes
Q: One strand of a DNA duplex has the composition A = 20 %, T = 30 %, G = 35 %, C = 15 %. A virus genome is found to contain A = 25 %, T = 33 %, G = 24 %, C = 18 %. Which statement is correct?
A) The complementary strand has A 20 %, T 30 %, G 35 %, C 15 %; the virus genome is double-stranded
B) The complementary strand has A 30 %, T 20 %, G 15 %, C 35 %; the whole duplex has A = T = 25 % and G = C = 25 %; the virus genome, with A ≠ T and G ≠ C, is single-stranded
C) The whole duplex has A 20 %, T 30 %, G 35 %, C 15 %, because both strands are identical
D) The complementary strand has A 35 %, T 15 %, G 20 %, C 30 %; the virus genome is double-stranded RNA
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) Complementary strand A 30, T 20, G 15, C 35; duplex A = T = G = C = 25 %; the virus genome is single-stranded.
Given: Strand 1: A 20 %, T 30 %, G 35 %, C 15 %. Virus: A 25, T 33, G 24, C 18 (%).
Formula/Principle: In double-stranded DNA, A pairs with T and G pairs with C (Chargaff's rule: A = T, G = C, and the purine total equals the pyrimidine total).
Step 1: Every A in strand 1 faces a T in strand 2, and so on: strand 2 has A = 30 % (opposite T), T = 20 %, G = 15 % (opposite C), C = 35 %.
Step 2: Whole duplex: A = (20 + 30)/2 = 25 %, T = (30 + 20)/2 = 25 %, G = (35 + 15)/2 = 25 %, C = (15 + 35)/2 = 25 %.
Step 3: Virus: A (25) ≠ T (33) and G (24) ≠ C (18); it contains T, so it is DNA, and the lack of Chargaff equality shows the strands are not paired: single-stranded DNA.
Concept: The two strands of DNA are complementary and antiparallel; the base sequence of one strand fixes the other. Chargaff's equalities hold for double-stranded DNA but not for the individual strands or for single-stranded genomes such as that of bacteriophage φX174.
Why: Base pairing makes the duplex composition the average of the two strands, so A = T and G = C in the duplex even when a single strand is very uneven.
Why not the others: (A) The complementary strand is not identical to the first. (C) The two strands are complementary, not identical. (D) The bases have been paired wrongly, and the presence of T rules out RNA.
Key point: Complementary strand: swap A↔T and G↔C; Chargaff's rule applies only to double-stranded nucleic acid.

Q: In a bacterium, end product D is made by the pathway A → B → C → D, catalysed by enzymes E1, E2 and E3 respectively. D binds to E1 at a site other than its active site and switches it off. What happens in a mutant that lacks E2 when the cells are grown without D?
A) B accumulates and D is not made; E1 stays active
B) B accumulates and D is not made; since D is absent, E1 is not inhibited and keeps converting A to B, so B builds up; adding D to the medium switches E1 off and stops the build-up of B, while the cells can now grow using the supplied D
C) C accumulates; E1 is switched off by B
D) A accumulates, because E1 is competitively inhibited by B at its active site
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) B accumulates; E1 remains active without D; added D switches E1 off and supports growth.
Concept: Enzymes are proteins with an active site where the substrate binds. When a molecule closely resembling the substrate binds the active site, it is a competitive inhibitor (for example malonate inhibiting succinic dehydrogenase). In feedback inhibition, the end product of a metabolic pathway binds the first enzyme of the pathway at a regulatory (allosteric) site, reducing its activity so that the product is not overproduced. Enzymes act in sequence in metabolic pathways, each product being the substrate of the next enzyme.
Why: Without E2, the pathway is blocked after B; the intermediate before the block piles up, and nothing beyond it is formed. Because the regulator D is never made, the first enzyme is never switched off. Supplying D both satisfies the cell's need and restores the feedback control.
Why not the others: (A) Incomplete: it misses what happens when D is supplied and why E1 stays active. (C) The block is before C, and B is not the regulator. (D) A is converted by an active E1; the inhibitor is D, acting allosterically.
Key point: A block in a pathway causes the substrate of the missing enzyme to accumulate; end-product feedback acts on the first enzyme.

Q: In an experiment on an alga, the rate of O₂ evolution (arbitrary units) is 12 with red light of 680 nm alone, 4 with far-red light beyond 690 nm alone, and 24 when both beams are given together. What is the enhancement ratio and what does it show?
A) Ratio 0.5; the two wavelengths compete for the same pigment
B) Ratio 2.0; the two beams act on the same photosystem
C) Ratio 1.0; the effects are simply additive
D) Ratio 1.5 (24 ÷ 16); the combined rate is greater than the sum of the separate rates because two photosystems are involved — PS I, with reaction centre P700, absorbs the longer wavelength, and PS II, with reaction centre P680, the shorter — and they work in series in non-cyclic electron flow (the Z scheme) from water to NADP⁺
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) Enhancement ratio = 24/(12 + 4) = 1.5; evidence for two photosystems in series.
Given: Rate (680 nm alone) = 12; rate (> 690 nm alone) = 4; rate (both together) = 24.
Formula/Principle: Enhancement ratio = rate with both beams ÷ (sum of rates with each beam alone).
Step 1: Sum of separate rates = 12 + 4 = 16.
Step 2: Enhancement ratio = 24/16 = 1.5.
Step 3: A ratio above 1 means the two beams help each other: each drives a different light reaction that the other needs.
Concept: The light reactions use two photosystems, PS I and PS II, named in the order of their discovery, not in the order in which they function. The reaction centre of PS I has chlorophyll a with an absorption peak at 700 nm (P700), and that of PS II has an absorption peak at 680 nm (P680). In non-cyclic photophosphorylation, electrons from PS II pass through an electron transport chain to PS I and then to NADP⁺; water splitting associated with PS II supplies electrons and releases O₂. The drop in O₂ evolution in light beyond 680 nm is the "red drop", and its recovery when shorter-wavelength light is added is the Emerson enhancement effect.
Why: Light beyond 690 nm excites mainly PS I, so electrons are drained from the chain but not replaced by PS II; combined with 680 nm light, both photosystems run and the chain from water to NADP⁺ works at full speed.
Why not the others: (A) and (C) Wrong arithmetic and interpretation. (B) The ratio is 1.5, and synergy indicates two different photosystems.
Key point: Enhancement ratio > 1 → two photosystems (P680 and P700) working in series.

sub_topic: Why alveolar ventilation, not total ventilation, measures useful breathing, using dead space to compare shallow rapid and deep slow breathing
Q: Two people each breathe 6000 mL of air per minute. P breathes 300 mL per breath at 20 breaths per minute; Q breathes 600 mL per breath at 10 breaths per minute. The anatomical dead space (air in trachea, bronchi and bronchioles not reaching the alveoli) is 150 mL in both. Which statement is correct?
A) Both ventilate their alveoli equally, since both move 6000 mL per minute
B) P has better alveolar ventilation because of its higher rate
C) Alveolar ventilation is 3000 mL/min for P and 4500 mL/min for Q, because the first 150 mL of each breath only refills the dead space and does not take part in gas exchange; deep slow breathing is therefore more effective
D) Alveolar ventilation is zero in P, because its tidal volume is less than the residual volume
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) P: (300 − 150) × 20 = 3000 mL/min; Q: (600 − 150) × 10 = 4500 mL/min.
Given: Minute volume 6000 mL/min for both; P: TV 300 mL, rate 20; Q: TV 600 mL, rate 10; dead space 150 mL.
Formula/Principle: Alveolar ventilation = (tidal volume − dead space) × breathing rate.
Step 1: P: 300 − 150 = 150 mL of fresh air reaches the alveoli per breath; 150 × 20 = 3000 mL/min.
Step 2: Q: 600 − 150 = 450 mL per breath; 450 × 10 = 4500 mL/min.
Step 3: Same total ventilation, but Q delivers 50 % more fresh air to the alveoli.
Concept: Tidal volume is the volume of air inspired or expired during a normal respiration (about 500 mL in a healthy adult). The conducting part of the respiratory system (nose to terminal bronchioles) transports atmospheric air to the alveoli, clears it of foreign particles, humidifies it and brings it to body temperature, but no exchange of gases occurs there. The exchange part is the alveoli and their ducts. Residual volume is the air remaining in the lungs even after a forcible expiration.
Why: Each breath first pushes the stale air of the conducting zone into the alveoli; only the volume beyond the dead space is fresh. With small breaths a larger fraction of each breath is wasted.
Why not the others: (A) Ignores dead space. (B) Faster shallow breaths waste more air. (D) Tidal volume is compared with dead space, not residual volume.
Key point: Useful ventilation = (TV − dead space) × rate; deep breaths are more efficient.

sub_topic: Proportions of normal and aneuploid gametes produced by non-disjunction in meiosis I versus meiosis II, and the chromosome numbers of the resulting zygotes
Q: In a human oocyte-forming cell, the chromosome 21 pair fails to separate. Considering all four products of meiosis, what fraction of gametes is abnormal if the failure is in meiosis I, and if it is in meiosis II (in one of the two secondary cells)? If an n + 1 gamete with an extra chromosome 21 is fertilised by a normal sperm, what is the chromosome number of the zygote?
A) Meiosis I: half abnormal; meiosis II: all abnormal; zygote 45
B) Meiosis I: none abnormal; meiosis II: half abnormal; zygote 46
C) Meiosis I: one quarter abnormal; meiosis II: one quarter abnormal; zygote 48
D) Meiosis I: all four abnormal (two n + 1 and two n − 1); meiosis II: half abnormal (one n + 1, one n − 1 and two normal n); zygote 47 with trisomy 21 (Down's syndrome)
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) Meiosis I non-disjunction: 4/4 abnormal; meiosis II: 2/4 abnormal; zygote 2n + 1 = 47 (Down's syndrome).
Given: Normal human gamete n = 23; non-disjunction of chromosome 21.
Formula/Principle: Meiosis I separates homologues; meiosis II separates sister chromatids. Non-disjunction gives gametes with n + 1 and n − 1 chromosomes.
Step 1: Meiosis I failure: both homologues go to one secondary cell (n + 1) and none to the other (n − 1); each then divides normally → two n + 1 and two n − 1 gametes: all four abnormal.
Step 2: Meiosis II failure in one secondary cell: that cell gives one n + 1 and one n − 1; the other secondary cell divides normally → two n. So 2 of 4 abnormal.
Step 3: n + 1 egg (24) + normal sperm (23) = 47 chromosomes, with three copies of chromosome 21.
Concept: Failure of segregation of chromatids during the cell division cycle results in the gain or loss of a chromosome(s), called aneuploidy. Down's syndrome is caused by the presence of an additional copy of chromosome number 21 (trisomy of 21); affected individuals are short statured with a small round head, furrowed tongue and partially open mouth, and physical, psychomotor and mental development is retarded. Turner's syndrome (45, X0) and Klinefelter's syndrome (47, XXY) arise similarly from non-disjunction of sex chromosomes.
Why: The earlier the error, the more products inherit it: a meiosis I error affects both daughter lines, a meiosis II error only one.
Why not the others: (A) Fractions reversed and wrong zygote number. (B) A meiosis I error makes all gametes abnormal. (C) Wrong fractions; 48 would need two extra chromosomes.
Key point: Non-disjunction in meiosis I → all 4 gametes aneuploid; in meiosis II → 2 of 4; n + 1 gamete × n → 2n + 1.

Q: At one VNTR locus the repeat unit is 16 bp and the flanking DNA included in each fragment is 100 bp. A suspect's fragments are 420 bp and 580 bp. Across three independent loci the suspect's genotype frequencies in the population are 0.01, 0.05 and 0.02. Which statement is correct?

B) The alleles carry 20 and 30 repeats; the chance-match probability is 1 × 10⁻³
C) The alleles carry 20 and 30 repeats; the chance-match probability is 0.01 × 0.05 × 0.02 = 1 × 10⁻⁵, so in a city of 2 million people about 20 unrelated individuals would be expected to match by chance — which is why many more loci are used in practice
D) The alleles carry 26 and 36 repeats; a match at three loci proves identity with certainty
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) 20 and 30 repeats; match probability 1 × 10⁻⁵; about 20 chance matches in 2 million.
Given: Repeat = 16 bp; flanking = 100 bp; fragments 420 and 580 bp; genotype frequencies 0.01, 0.05, 0.02.
Formula/Principle: Number of repeats = (fragment − flanking)/repeat length. For independent loci, probabilities multiply (product rule).
Step 1: (420 − 100)/16 = 320/16 = 20 repeats; (580 − 100)/16 = 480/16 = 30 repeats.
Step 2: P(chance match) = 0.01 × 0.05 × 0.02 = 1 × 10⁻⁵.
Step 3: Expected chance matches in 2 × 10⁶ people = 2 × 10⁶ × 10⁻⁵ = 20.
Concept: DNA fingerprinting involves identifying differences in some specific regions of the DNA sequence called repetitive DNA, because in these sequences a small stretch of DNA is repeated many times. Satellite DNA is classified on the basis of base composition, length of segment and number of repetitive units into categories such as micro-satellites and mini-satellites. These sequences show a high degree of polymorphism, and VNTR (variable number of tandem repeats) belongs to the mini-satellite class; the copy number varies from chromosome to chromosome in an individual, so the size of a VNTR varies. After hybridisation with a VNTR probe, bands of different sizes give the characteristic pattern of an individual DNA.
Why: Each additional locus multiplies the rarity of the combined profile, so a few loci can leave several chance matches in a large population, while many loci make chance matches negligible.
Why not the others: (A) Forgets to subtract the flanking DNA, and independent probabilities multiply, not add. (B) The product is 10⁻⁵, not 10⁻³. (D) Wrong repeat numbers; a three-locus match is strong but not certain evidence.
Key point: Repeats = (size − flank)/unit; match probability = product of genotype frequencies at independent loci.

sub_topic: Interpreting an observed change in allele frequency on an island as gene flow (migration) and predicting genotype frequencies after random mating
Q: An island population has allele frequency q(a) = 0.20. Migrants from the mainland, where q = 0.60, arrive and come to make up 25 % of the island population, after which mating is random. What is the new q, and what are the expected genotype frequencies in the next generation?
A) q stays 0.20, because migration does not change allele frequencies; aa = 0.04
B) q = 0.30, because 0.75 × 0.20 + 0.25 × 0.60 = 0.30; after one generation of random mating AA = 0.49, Aa = 0.42, aa = 0.09; the change is due to gene flow, one of the factors that disturb Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium
C) q = 0.40, the simple average of 0.20 and 0.60; aa = 0.16
D) q = 0.60, because migrants replace the residents; aa = 0.36
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) q = 0.30; AA 0.49, Aa 0.42, aa 0.09; gene flow.
Given: Residents: q = 0.20, fraction 0.75; migrants: q = 0.60, fraction 0.25.
Formula/Principle: Mixed q = weighted average of the allele frequencies; Hardy–Weinberg after random mating: p² + 2pq + q² = 1.
Step 1: q = 0.75 × 0.20 + 0.25 × 0.60 = 0.15 + 0.15 = 0.30.
Step 2: p = 1 − 0.30 = 0.70.
Step 3: AA = 0.70² = 0.49; Aa = 2 × 0.70 × 0.30 = 0.42; aa = 0.30² = 0.09.
Concept: Hardy–Weinberg principle says that allele frequencies in a population are stable and constant from generation to generation; the gene pool remains constant, which is called genetic equilibrium. Five factors are known to affect Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium: gene migration or gene flow, genetic drift, mutation, genetic recombination and natural selection. When migration of a section of population to another place and population occurs, gene frequencies change in the original as well as in the new population; new genes or alleles are added to the new population and lost from the old population. There would be a gene flow if this gene migration happens multiple times.
Why: Allele frequency is a property of the whole gene pool, so mixing two pools gives a frequency weighted by their sizes; one round of random mating then restores Hardy–Weinberg genotype proportions at the new frequency.
Why not the others: (A) Migration does change allele frequencies. (C) The average must be weighted by the 75 : 25 proportion. (D) The migrants are only a quarter of the population.
Key point: Gene flow: new q = weighted average; random mating then gives p², 2pq, q².

Q: Two species of seed-eating birds with similar beak sizes come to live on the same island and compete for seeds. Which sequence of events, leading to their coexistence, is most consistent with competition theory?
A) Overlap in diet causes competition → individuals of each species that use the seeds less used by the other species leave more offspring → over generations the beak sizes of the two species diverge (character displacement) and they partition the resource → reduced competition allows coexistence; without such divergence, Gause's competitive exclusion principle predicts that the inferior competitor would eventually be eliminated
B) Competition causes both species to converge in beak size → they then coexist using identical seeds
C) One species immediately becomes a predator of the other → coexistence follows
D) Competition has no effect because each bird eats only a few seeds
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) Competition → selection for divergent beaks → resource partitioning → coexistence; otherwise exclusion.
Concept: Competition is best defined as a process in which the fitness of one species (measured in terms of its 'r', the intrinsic rate of increase) is significantly lower in the presence of another species. Gause's 'Competitive Exclusion Principle' states that two closely related species competing for the same resources cannot co-exist indefinitely and the competitively inferior one will be eliminated eventually. More recent studies show that species facing competition might evolve mechanisms that promote co-existence rather than exclusion; one such mechanism is resource partitioning. If two species compete for the same resource, they could avoid competition by choosing, for instance, different times for feeding or different foraging patterns. MacArthur showed that five closely related species of warblers living on the same tree were able to avoid competition and co-exist due to behavioural differences in their foraging activities.
Why: Selection favours individuals that compete least, so the two species shift apart in the trait that determines resource use; this lowers niche overlap enough for both to persist.
Why not the others: (B) Convergence would increase competition. (C) Competition does not turn into predation. (D) Shared limiting resources make competition significant.
Key point: Competition → exclusion, or → divergence/resource partitioning → coexistence.
