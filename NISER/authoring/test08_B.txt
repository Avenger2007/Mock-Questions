sub_topic: Pine seedlings failing in new soil until mycorrhizal fungi are supplied, contrasted with commensalism
Q: Pine seedlings planted in a newly cleared region with no pine trees grew poorly and showed signs of phosphorus deficiency, although the soil contained enough phosphate. When a little soil from an old pine forest was mixed in, the seedlings grew well. Which explanation is correct?
A) The forest soil supplied mycorrhizal fungi; the fungal hyphae absorb phosphorus and water from the soil for the pine, and the pine supplies the fungus with carbohydrates, so both partners benefit (mutualism)
B) The forest soil supplied a parasitic fungus that weakened competing weeds
C) The forest soil supplied a commensal fungus that gains food from the pine roots while the pine is neither helped nor harmed
D) The forest soil simply added extra phosphate fertiliser
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) Mycorrhizal fungi from the forest soil formed a mutualistic association with the pine roots.
Concept: Mycorrhiza is a close association between a fungus and the roots of a higher plant. The fungal hyphae greatly increase the absorbing surface and take up phosphorus and water for the plant; the plant passes sugars to the fungus. Both partners gain, so it is mutualism (+/+). In commensalism (+/0), one species gains and the other is unaffected, as with an orchid growing on a mango branch or barnacles on a whale.
Why: The soil already had phosphate, so the problem was uptake, not supply. Old pine-forest soil contains the fungal partner; once roots were colonised, phosphorus uptake and growth improved. Many pines cannot grow well at all without mycorrhizae.
Why not the others: (B) Weeds were not the cause of phosphorus deficiency in soil already rich in phosphate, and a parasite harms its host. (C) The pine clearly benefits, so the relationship is not commensal. (D) The soil already had enough phosphate, and a small amount of forest soil adds very little phosphate.
Key point: Mycorrhiza: fungus gives P and water, plant gives sugar, so mutualism (+/+); commensalism is +/0.

sub_topic: Upright and inverted pyramids of number, biomass and energy, and the ten per cent pattern of energy transfer
Q: In an ecosystem, producers fix 10,000 J of energy. Which statement about ecological pyramids is correct?
A) All three pyramids (number, biomass and energy) are always upright in every ecosystem
B) The pyramid of energy is inverted in the sea because phytoplankton have a small standing crop
C) About 1000 J reaches primary consumers, 100 J secondary consumers and 10 J tertiary consumers, so the pyramid of energy is always upright; the pyramid of numbers can be inverted (one tree supporting many insects), and the pyramid of biomass can be inverted in the sea
D) The pyramid of numbers for a tree ecosystem is always upright, because producers are always the most numerous
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) Energy: 10,000 → 1000 → 100 → 10 J, always upright; numbers and biomass can be inverted.
Concept: About 10 per cent of the energy at one trophic level passes to the next (the rest is lost mainly as heat in respiration). The pyramid of energy therefore always narrows upwards. Numbers and biomass are standing measures at one moment, so they can be inverted in special cases.
Why: One large tree (producer) feeds thousands of insects, so the number pyramid is inverted at its base. In the sea, the small standing biomass of fast-reproducing phytoplankton supports a larger biomass of zooplankton and fish, so the biomass pyramid is inverted. Energy flow is measured per unit time and cannot increase up the chain.
Why not the others: (A) Number and biomass pyramids can be inverted. (B) The biomass pyramid, not the energy pyramid, is inverted in the sea. (D) A single tree supports many herbivores, so the pyramid of numbers is inverted there.
Key point: Energy pyramid is always upright (about 10 % transfer); number and biomass pyramids can be inverted.

sub_topic: Using the number of xylem and phloem strands in a transverse section to tell a monocot root from a dicot root
Q: A transverse section of a young root shows a total of 18 separate strands of xylem and phloem, arranged on alternate radii, with a large, well-developed pith. A second root shows 8 such strands in total, with little or no pith. How many xylem bundles does each root have, and which is the monocot root?
A) 18 and 8; the second root is monocot
B) 9 and 4; the first root is monocot (polyarch) and the second is dicot (tetrarch)
C) 9 and 4; the first root is dicot and the second is monocot
D) 6 and 2; both are dicot roots
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) 9 and 4; the first root is monocot (polyarch), the second dicot (tetrarch).
Concept: In roots the vascular bundles are radial: xylem and phloem strands lie on alternate radii, in equal numbers. Dicot roots usually have 2 to 4 xylem bundles (diarch to tetrarch) and little or no pith; monocot roots usually have more than six (polyarch) and a large pith.
Given: root 1: 18 strands in total, large pith; root 2: 8 strands in total, little pith.
Formula/Principle: xylem strands = phloem strands = half the total in a radial arrangement.
Step 1: Root 1: 18 ÷ 2 = 9 xylem bundles: polyarch, monocot.
Step 2: Root 2: 8 ÷ 2 = 4 xylem bundles: tetrarch, dicot.
Why: The count of xylem bundles and the size of the pith together distinguish the two roots; secondary growth (seen later in dicot roots) is absent in monocot roots.
Why not the others: (A) Counts all strands as xylem; half are phloem. (C) Reverses the two roots; many xylem bundles and a large pith mean monocot. (D) Divides by three; the strands alternate one to one.
Key point: Radial bundles: xylem = phloem; polyarch with large pith means monocot root, 2–4 bundles means dicot root.

B) 32; 2C; 32
C) 16; 4C; 8
D) 32; 4C; 16
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) 32; 4C; 16.
Concept: DNA doubles in S phase (2C → 4C) while the chromosome number stays 2n, each chromosome now having two sister chromatids. In mitotic anaphase the centromeres split, so each chromatid counts as a chromosome. In anaphase I homologous chromosomes separate but sister chromatids stay joined. After meiosis I each cell is haploid (n), with each chromosome still having two chromatids.
Given: 2n = 16; DNA = 2C in G1, so 4C after S phase.
Formula/Principle: chromosomes are counted by centromeres; chromatids = 2 per chromosome until the centromeres split.
Step 1: (i) Mitotic anaphase: 16 × 2 = 32 chromosomes in the cell before it divides.
Step 2: (ii) Anaphase I: the cell has not divided yet, so DNA is still 4C.
Step 3: (iii) Metaphase II: n = 8 chromosomes, each with 2 chromatids = 16 chromatids (DNA 2C).
Why: Chromatid separation doubles the chromosome count only in mitotic anaphase and anaphase II; meiosis I halves the chromosome number without splitting centromeres.
Why not the others: (A) Ignores DNA replication and the split of centromeres. (B) The DNA content does not halve until the cell divides at the end of meiosis I, and a metaphase II cell is haploid. (C) Ignores the split of centromeres in mitotic anaphase and miscounts the chromatids.
Key point: Mitotic anaphase: 4n chromosomes; anaphase I: 4C; metaphase II: n chromosomes, 2n chromatids.
