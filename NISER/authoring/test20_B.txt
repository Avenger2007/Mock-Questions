sub_topic: Reading a table of leaf features to distinguish a dorsiventral (dicot) leaf from an isobilateral (monocot) leaf and explaining the role of bulliform cells
Q: A student records features of two leaf sections.

Feature | Leaf P | Leaf Q
Mesophyll | palisade (adaxial) and spongy (abaxial) | not differentiated
Stomata | more on the abaxial (lower) epidermis | about equal on both surfaces
Venation | reticulate, veins of varying size | parallel, veins of similar size
Special epidermal cells | absent | large, empty, colourless cells on the adaxial side

Which interpretation is correct?
A) P is isobilateral (monocot) and Q dorsiventral (dicot)
B) Both are dicot leaves differing only in age
C) P is a dorsiventral dicot leaf and Q an isobilateral monocot leaf; the large colourless adaxial cells of Q are bulliform cells, which become flaccid when they lose water so that the leaf curls inwards to reduce water loss; in both leaves the vascular bundles are surrounded by a layer of thick-walled bundle sheath cells
D) The special cells in Q are guard cells that open stomata at night
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) P dorsiventral dicot, Q isobilateral monocot; bulliform cells allow leaf rolling; bundle sheath around vascular bundles.
Concept: The vertical section of a dorsiventral leaf through the lamina shows three main parts: epidermis, mesophyll and vascular system. The epidermis which covers both the upper surface (adaxial epidermis) and lower surface (abaxial epidermis) of the leaf has a conspicuous cuticle; the abaxial epidermis generally bears more stomata than the adaxial epidermis, and the latter may even lack stomata. The mesophyll, which possesses chloroplasts and carries out photosynthesis, is made up of parenchyma and has two types of cells — the palisade parenchyma (elongated cells arranged vertically and parallel to each other, adaxially placed) and the spongy parenchyma (oval or round, loosely arranged cells situated below the palisade cells and extending to the lower epidermis). Vascular bundles can be seen in the veins and the midrib; the size of the vascular bundles is dependent on the size of the veins, which vary in thickness in the reticulate venation of dicot leaves; the vascular bundles are surrounded by a layer of thick-walled bundle sheath cells. The anatomy of an isobilateral (monocot) leaf is similar to that of the dorsiventral leaf in many ways, with the following characteristic differences: the stomata are present on both surfaces of the epidermis, and the mesophyll is not differentiated into palisade and spongy parenchyma. In grasses, certain adaxial epidermal cells along the veins modify themselves into large, empty, colourless cells called bulliform cells; when the bulliform cells in the leaves have absorbed water and are turgid, the leaf surface is exposed; when they are flaccid due to water stress, they make the leaves curl inwards to minimise water loss. The parallel venation in monocot leaves is reflected in the near similar sizes of vascular bundles (except in main veins).
Given: Four features of leaves P and Q.
Step 1: Palisade/spongy differentiation and more abaxial stomata ⇒ P dorsiventral.
Step 2: Undifferentiated mesophyll, stomata on both surfaces, parallel veins ⇒ Q isobilateral.
Step 3: Large empty adaxial cells ⇒ bulliform cells.
Why: Leaf anatomy reflects light exposure and water economy: a horizontally held dicot leaf has distinct upper and lower tissues, while a vertically held grass leaf has both surfaces alike.
Why not the others: (A) Labels reversed. (B) The differences are structural, not due to age. (D) Bulliform cells are not guard cells.
Key point: Dorsiventral: palisade + spongy, more abaxial stomata; isobilateral: uniform mesophyll, bulliform cells.

Q: Which sequence and description of nuclear organisation is correct?
A) Negatively charged DNA wraps around a positively charged histone octamer to form a nucleosome (about 200 bp of DNA) → repeating nucleosomes form a 'beads-on-string' chromatin fibre → the fibre is further coiled and supercoiled into chromatin fibres, which condense into chromosomes at metaphase, a process requiring non-histone chromosomal (NHC) proteins; the nucleus is bounded by two membranes with a perinuclear space and nuclear pores, and chromosomes are metacentric, sub-metacentric, acrocentric or telocentric depending on the position of the centromere
B) Histones wrap around DNA, forming chromosomes directly, and the nuclear envelope is a single membrane
C) Nucleosomes form after chromosomes condense at metaphase
D) A telocentric chromosome has its centromere exactly in the middle
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) DNA + histone octamer → nucleosome → beads-on-string → chromatin fibres → metaphase chromosome; double nuclear envelope; four centromere types.
Concept: The distance between two consecutive base pairs is 0.34 nm, so the length of DNA in a typical mammalian cell (6.6 × 10⁹ bp) is about 2.2 metres — far greater than the dimension of a typical nucleus (approximately 10⁻⁶ m). In eukaryotes, there is a set of positively charged, basic proteins called histones, rich in the basic amino acid residues lysine and arginine, which carry positive charges in their side chains; histones are organised to form a unit of eight molecules called a histone octamer. The negatively charged DNA is wrapped around the positively charged histone octamer to form a structure called the nucleosome; a typical nucleosome contains 200 bp of DNA helix. Nucleosomes constitute the repeating unit of a structure in the nucleus called chromatin, thread-like stained (coloured) bodies seen in the nucleus; the nucleosomes in chromatin are seen as 'beads-on-string' under the electron microscope. The beads-on-string structure in chromatin is packaged to form chromatin fibres that are further coiled and condensed at the metaphase stage of cell division to form chromosomes; the packaging of chromatin at a higher level requires an additional set of proteins collectively referred to as non-histone chromosomal (NHC) proteins. The nuclear envelope, which consists of two parallel membranes with a space between (10 to 50 nm) called the perinuclear space, forms a barrier between the materials present inside the nucleus and those of the cytoplasm; the outer membrane usually remains continuous with the endoplasmic reticulum; at a number of places the nuclear envelope is interrupted by minute pores. Every chromosome essentially has a primary constriction, the centromere; based on its position, chromosomes are metacentric (middle centromere, two equal arms), sub-metacentric (centromere slightly away from the middle, one shorter and one longer arm), acrocentric (centromere situated close to its end, one extremely short and one very long arm) and telocentric (terminal centromere).
Step 1: DNA wraps the histone octamer ⇒ nucleosome.
Step 2: Nucleosomes in series ⇒ beads-on-string chromatin.
Step 3: Further coiling with NHC proteins ⇒ chromatin fibres ⇒ metaphase chromosomes.
Why: Opposite charges on DNA and histones allow the very long DNA to be packed in stages into the small nucleus.
Why not the others: (B) DNA wraps histones; the envelope is double. (C) Order reversed. (D) Telocentric means terminal centromere.
Key point: DNA → nucleosome (200 bp) → beads-on-string → chromatin fibre → chromosome.

sub_topic: Interpreting rate measurements of an enzyme at different substrate concentrations, with an inhibitor, and after exposure to low and high temperatures
Q: An enzyme's reaction rate is measured. (i) Rate rises with substrate concentration and levels off at a maximum. (ii) With substance X present, a higher substrate concentration is needed to reach half the maximum rate, but the same maximum is reached at very high substrate concentration. (iii) Enzyme kept at 4 °C is inactive, but regains activity when warmed to 37 °C. (iv) Enzyme heated to 70 °C stays inactive after cooling. Which interpretation is correct?
A) (ii) X is a non-competitive inhibitor that destroys the active site
B) (iii) and (iv) both show permanent denaturation
C) (i) shows that the rate increases indefinitely with substrate
D) (i) At high substrate concentration all active sites are occupied, so the rate approaches Vmax; (ii) X is a competitive inhibitor resembling the substrate, raising Km but not Vmax, because excess substrate outcompetes it; (iii) low temperature only temporarily inactivates the enzyme; (iv) high temperature denatures the protein and destroys its active site permanently
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) Saturation gives Vmax; X competitive (Km ↑, Vmax unchanged); cold reversibly inactivates; heat denatures.
Concept: The activity of an enzyme can be affected by a change in the conditions which can alter the tertiary structure of the protein; these include temperature, pH, change in substrate concentration or binding of specific chemicals that regulate its activity. Enzymes generally function in a narrow range of temperature and pH; each enzyme shows its highest activity at a particular temperature and pH called the optimum temperature and optimum pH; activity declines both below and above the optimum value; low temperature preserves the enzyme in a temporarily inactive state whereas high temperature destroys enzymatic activity because proteins are denatured by heat. With the increase in substrate concentration, the velocity of the enzymatic reaction rises at first; the reaction ultimately reaches a maximum velocity (Vmax) which is not exceeded by any further rise in concentration of the substrate; this is because the enzyme molecules are fewer than the substrate molecules and after saturation of these molecules, there are no free enzyme molecules to bind with the additional substrate molecules. When the inhibitor closely resembles the substrate in its molecular structure and inhibits the activity of the enzyme, it is known as a competitive inhibitor; because it competes with the substrate for the active site, its effect is overcome by raising substrate concentration, so the substrate concentration needed for half-maximal rate (Km) rises while Vmax stays the same.
Step 1: Levelling off ⇒ saturation at Vmax.
Step 2: Same Vmax, higher substrate for half Vmax ⇒ competitive inhibition.
Step 3: 4 °C reversible; 70 °C irreversible denaturation.
Why: Enzyme activity depends on substrate access to an intact active site; competition can be outweighed, but a heat-denatured site cannot recover.
Why not the others: (A) A competitive inhibitor does not lower Vmax. (B) Cold inactivation is reversible. (C) The rate saturates.
Key point: Vmax at saturation; competitive inhibitor raises Km only; cold reversible, heat irreversible.

sub_topic: The sequence of ionic events from the resting membrane potential through depolarisation and repolarisation during conduction of a nerve impulse
Q: Which sequence and description of nerve impulse generation is correct?
A) At rest the inside is positive; K⁺ enters to depolarise the membrane
B) The Na⁺/K⁺ pump moves 2 Na⁺ out and 3 K⁺ in, making the inside positive at rest
C) Repolarisation occurs because Na⁺ channels open wider
D) At rest the axonal membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺, and the sodium–potassium pump moves 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in, so the outer surface is positive and the inner negative (polarised, resting potential) → a stimulus makes the membrane freely permeable to Na⁺ at that site, Na⁺ rushes in and the polarity reverses (depolarisation, action potential) → the local current depolarises the next site, so the impulse moves along → the rise in Na⁺ permeability is short-lived and is followed by a rise in K⁺ permeability, K⁺ diffuses out and the resting potential is restored (repolarisation)
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) Resting polarisation by pump and K⁺ permeability → Na⁺ influx (depolarisation) → propagation → K⁺ efflux (repolarisation).
Concept: Neurons are excitable cells because their membranes are in a polarised state. Different types of ion channels are present on the neural membrane; these ion channels are selectively permeable to different ions; when a neuron is not conducting any impulse, i.e. resting, the axonal membrane is comparatively more permeable to potassium ions (K⁺) and nearly impermeable to sodium ions (Na⁺); similarly, the membrane is impermeable to negatively charged proteins present in the axoplasm; consequently, the axoplasm inside the axon contains a high concentration of K⁺ and negatively charged proteins and a low concentration of Na⁺; in contrast, the fluid outside the axon contains a low concentration of K⁺, a high concentration of Na⁺, and thus forms a concentration gradient. These ionic gradients across the resting membrane are maintained by the active transport of ions by the sodium-potassium pump which transports 3 Na⁺ outwards for 2 K⁺ into the cell; as a result, the outer surface of the axonal membrane possesses a positive charge while its inner surface becomes negatively charged and therefore is polarised; the electrical potential difference across the resting plasma membrane is called the resting potential. When a stimulus is applied at a site on the polarised membrane, the membrane at the site becomes freely permeable to Na⁺; this leads to a rapid influx of Na⁺ followed by the reversal of the polarity at that site, i.e. the outer surface of the membrane becomes negatively charged and the inner side becomes positively charged; the polarity of the membrane at that site is thus reversed and hence depolarised; the electrical potential difference across the plasma membrane at that site is called the action potential, which is in fact termed a nerve impulse. At sites immediately ahead, the axon membrane has a positive charge on the outer surface and a negative charge on its inner surface; as a result, a current flows on the inner surface from the depolarised site to the next site, so the action potential is generated there; thus, the impulse (action potential) generated at one site is conducted along the length of the axon. The rise in the stimulus-induced permeability to Na⁺ is extremely short-lived; it is quickly followed by a rise in permeability to K⁺; within a fraction of a second, K⁺ diffuses outside the membrane and restores the resting potential of the membrane at the site of excitation, and the fibre becomes once more responsive to further stimulation.
Step 1: Resting: inside negative (pump 3 Na⁺ out : 2 K⁺ in; K⁺ permeability).
Step 2: Stimulus: Na⁺ influx ⇒ depolarisation (action potential).
Step 3: K⁺ efflux ⇒ repolarisation; local currents propagate the impulse.
Why: Timed opening of Na⁺ and then K⁺ channels reverses and restores membrane polarity, and local currents trigger the same sequence in the next region.
Why not the others: (A) Inside is negative at rest; Na⁺ entry depolarises. (B) Pump ratio reversed. (C) Repolarisation is due to K⁺ efflux.
Key point: Rest (inside −) → Na⁺ in (depolarise) → K⁺ out (repolarise).

Stanford ecologist Paul Ehrlich used an analogy to understand the contribution of species richness: in an airplane (ecosystem) all parts are joined together using thousands of rivets (species); if every passenger travelling in it starts popping a rivet to take home (causing a species to become extinct), it may not affect flight safety (proper functioning of the ecosystem) initially, but as more and more rivets are removed, the plane becomes dangerously weak over a period of time; furthermore, which rivet is removed may also be critical; loss of rivets on the wings (key species that drive major ecosystem functions) is obviously a more serious threat to flight safety than loss of a few rivets on the seats or windows inside the plane. The reasons for conservation can be grouped into three categories: narrowly utilitarian, broadly utilitarian, and ethical; the narrowly utilitarian arguments for conserving biodiversity are obvious — humans derive countless direct economic benefits from nature: food (cereals, pulses, fruits), firewood, fibre, construction material, industrial products (tannins, lubricants, dyes, resins, perfumes) and products of medicinal importance; the broadly utilitarian argument says that biodiversity plays a major role in many ecosystem services that nature provides — the fast-dwindling Amazon forest is estimated to produce, through photosynthesis, 20 per cent of the total oxygen in the earth's atmosphere; pollination is another service; the ethical argument relates to what we owe to millions of plant, animal and microbe species with whom we share this planet.
Step 1: A few species lost ⇒ little immediate effect.
Step 2: Continued loss ⇒ progressive weakening.
Step 3: Loss of key species ⇒ disproportionate damage.
Why: Ecosystem function depends both on the number of species and on which species are lost, since some drive major processes.
Why not the others: (A) Early losses may have little effect, and species differ in importance. (B) More species gave more stable biomass. (D) Broadly utilitarian and ethical reasons also apply.
Key point: Rivet popper: gradual weakening, key species critical; diversity → stability (Tilman).

Q: (i) Two populations of frogs look almost identical but, when brought together, never interbreed. (ii) A horse and a donkey mate to produce a mule, which is sterile. Which interpretation is correct?

C) (ii) Horses and donkeys are one species because they can mate
D) Taxonomy and systematics mean exactly the same, and neither considers evolution
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) Reproductive isolation defines species; sterile hybrids show separate species; systematics adds evolutionary relationships.
Concept: Taxonomic studies consider a group of individual organisms with fundamental similarities as a species; one should be able to distinguish one species from the other closely related species based on the distinct morphological differences; biologically, all living organisms of a species are able to interbreed among themselves and produce fertile offspring, and members of different species are reproductively isolated from each other. In such cases, appearance alone may mislead: populations that look alike but do not interbreed are separate species, and organisms of different species that mate may produce sterile hybrids, such as the mule. Characterisation, identification, classification and nomenclature are the processes that are basic to taxonomy; taxonomy is not something new; human beings have always been interested in knowing more and more about the various kinds of organisms; the word systematics is derived from the Latin word 'systema' which means systematic arrangement of organisms; Linnaeus used Systema Naturae as the title of his publication; the scope of systematics was later enlarged to include identification, nomenclature and classification; systematics takes into account evolutionary relationships between organisms.
Why: Reproductive isolation, not appearance, keeps the gene pools of species separate.
Why not the others: (B) Appearance does not decide species membership. (C) Mating alone is not enough; offspring must be fertile. (D) Systematics includes evolutionary relationships.
Key point: Species = interbreeding, fertile offspring, reproductively isolated; systematics includes evolutionary relationships.
