sub_topic: What the ash left after burning dried leaf tissue contains, and what is lost on drying and burning
Q: A weighed sample of fresh leaf tissue is dried in an oven to constant mass and then burnt completely in a furnace, leaving a small amount of grey ash. Which statement about this experiment is correct?
A) The ash contains inorganic elements such as calcium, magnesium, potassium and phosphorus (as oxides, phosphates and similar salts); the carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen of the organic compounds escape as gases on burning
B) The ash contains the proteins and nucleic acids of the tissue, because these are large macromolecules
C) Most of the mass lost in the oven is carbon dioxide released from the organic compounds
D) The ash contains the same elements in the same proportions as the Earth's crust
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) The ash contains inorganic elements such as calcium, magnesium, potassium and phosphorus; the carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen of the organic compounds escape as gases on burning.
Concept: Chemical analysis of living tissue: drying removes water (the largest single component of living tissue); burning oxidises all organic compounds to gases such as CO₂, water vapour and oxides of nitrogen. What remains (the ash) is the inorganic (mineral) part of the tissue.
Why: Calcium, magnesium, potassium, sodium, phosphorus and sulphur are not converted to gases at furnace temperatures; they stay as oxides, phosphates, sulphates and carbonates. All organic molecules (sugars, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids) are destroyed.
Why not the others: (B) Proteins and nucleic acids are organic and burn away; only their mineral elements (such as P and S) remain. (C) Oven drying at moderate temperature removes water, not CO₂; organic compounds are oxidised only in the furnace. (D) Living matter differs greatly from the crust; for example, carbon and hydrogen are far more abundant in living tissue.
Key point: Fresh tissue → (dry) loses water → (burn) loses C, H, N as gases → ash = inorganic minerals.

sub_topic: Gametes formed when sister chromatids fail to separate in one cell at meiosis II
Q: In a diploid animal, meiosis I in a primary spermatocyte is normal. In meiosis II, the sister chromatids of one chromosome fail to separate in one of the two secondary spermatocytes, while the other secondary spermatocyte divides normally. What fraction of the four sperm formed from this primary spermatocyte are abnormal, and what are their chromosome numbers (n = normal haploid number)?
A) All four; two with n + 1 and two with n − 1
B) Half; both with n + 1
C) Half; one with n + 1 and one with n − 1
D) One quarter; with n + 1
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) Half; one with n + 1 and one with n − 1.
Concept: Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes; meiosis II separates sister chromatids, like mitosis. Failure of separation (non-disjunction) gives gametes with an extra or a missing chromosome. Meiosis is significant because it halves the chromosome number exactly, keeping it constant from generation to generation; errors upset this.
Why: After a normal meiosis I, each secondary spermatocyte has n chromosomes. The normal one gives two n sperm. In the faulty one, both chromatids of one chromosome go to the same pole, giving one sperm with n + 1 and one with n − 1. So two of the four sperm are abnormal.
Why not the others: (A) All four abnormal (two n + 1, two n − 1) is the result of non-disjunction in meiosis I. (B) The chromatid missing from one cell is present in the other, so the abnormal sperm cannot both be n + 1. (D) Non-disjunction always produces a pair: one cell with the extra chromosome and one lacking it.
Key point: Non-disjunction in meiosis I → 4 of 4 abnormal; in one cell at meiosis II → 2 of 4 abnormal (n + 1 and n − 1).

sub_topic: Why extra CO₂ raises photosynthesis in a C3 plant but hardly in a C4 plant
Q: In bright light at the optimum temperature, raising the CO₂ concentration of the air from about 360 to 450 µL L⁻¹ markedly increases the rate of photosynthesis of tomato (a C3 plant) but has little effect on maize (a C4 plant). What is the reason?
A) The stomata of maize close completely when CO₂ rises, so no extra CO₂ can enter
B) In maize, PEP carboxylase captures CO₂ and concentrates it in the bundle-sheath cells, so RuBisCO there is already nearly saturated at about 360 µL L⁻¹; in tomato, RuBisCO is not saturated and also reacts with O₂, so extra CO₂ increases carboxylation
C) At 450 µL L⁻¹ light becomes the limiting factor for tomato, but not for maize
D) Maize has no RuBisCO, so its rate does not depend on CO₂
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) In maize, PEP carboxylase concentrates CO₂ in the bundle-sheath cells, so RuBisCO there is already nearly saturated; in tomato, RuBisCO is unsaturated and also reacts with O₂.
Concept: Blackman's law of limiting factors: the rate is controlled by the factor in shortest supply. CO₂ is a major limiting factor in bright light. C4 plants saturate at about 360 µL L⁻¹, while C3 plants keep responding up to about 450 µL L⁻¹ or more.
Why: PEP carboxylase has a high affinity for CO₂ (as HCO₃⁻) and has no oxygenase activity. The C4 acids release CO₂ around RuBisCO in the bundle sheath, so RuBisCO works close to its maximum and photorespiration is negligible. In a C3 leaf, RuBisCO in the mesophyll is exposed to air-level CO₂, is not saturated, and loses some carbon by photorespiration; raising CO₂ favours carboxylation. This is why CO₂ enrichment is used in greenhouses for tomatoes.
Why not the others: (A) Stomata may narrow somewhat in high CO₂, but they do not close completely, and this is not why the C4 rate is unchanged. (C) If light became limiting for tomato, extra CO₂ would not raise its rate, but the observed rate does rise. (D) C4 plants have RuBisCO in their bundle-sheath cells; the Calvin cycle runs there.
Key point: C4 plants concentrate CO₂ around RuBisCO, so they saturate at lower CO₂; C3 plants respond to CO₂ enrichment.

Answer: (D) 19; it reduces acetaldehyde to ethanol, regenerating NAD⁺ so that glycolysis can continue.

sub_topic: Fate of a chromosome fragment without a centromere at the next cell division
Q: X-rays break a chromosome in a dividing cell, producing a fragment that has no centromere. What is the most likely fate of this fragment at the next division?
A) It moves to the poles normally, because spindle fibres attach to the telomeres at its ends
B) It forms a new centromere and becomes an extra complete chromosome
C) It has no kinetochore, so spindle fibres cannot attach to it; it fails to move to a pole at anaphase and is usually lost from the daughter nuclei
D) It becomes the satellite of another chromosome at the nucleolar organiser
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) It has no kinetochore, so spindle fibres cannot attach; it fails to move to a pole and is usually lost.
Concept: The centromere is the primary constriction of a chromosome. Disc-shaped kinetochores form on its sides, and spindle fibres attach to them to pull the chromatids to the poles at anaphase.
Why: Without a centromere, the fragment has no kinetochore, so it is not pulled to either pole. It lags behind, is left outside the reforming nuclei, and is lost. The daughter cells therefore carry a deletion of that segment.
Why not the others: (A) Spindle fibres attach at kinetochores on the centromere, not at the telomeres. (B) A broken fragment cannot make a new centromere; its replicated copy still has none. (D) A satellite is a small normal segment beyond the secondary constriction of certain chromosomes; it is not formed from broken fragments.
Key point: No centromere → no kinetochore → no spindle attachment → fragment lost at division.
