sub_topic: Zero-order kinetics: rate constant and dependence of half-life on initial concentration
Q: A reaction is zero order in A. The concentration of A falls from 0.80 mol L⁻¹ to 0.50 mol L⁻¹ in 20 min. What is the rate constant, and what would the half-life be if the initial concentration were 1.60 mol L⁻¹?
A) \(0.015\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\); 26.7 min, the same as for 0.80 mol L⁻¹
B) \(0.025\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\); 32.0 min
C) \(0.015\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\); 53.3 min
D) \(0.015\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\); 13.3 min
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) \(0.015\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\); 53.3 min.
Concept: For a zero-order reaction, \([A]=[A]_0-kt\) and \(t_{1/2}=\dfrac{[A]_0}{2k}\). The half-life is proportional to the initial concentration.
Given: \([A]_0=0.80\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}},\ [A]=0.50\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}},\ t=20\ \mathrm{min};\ \text{new}\ [A]_0=1.60\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)
Formula/Principle: \(k=\dfrac{[A]_0-[A]}{t};\ t_{1/2}=\dfrac{[A]_0}{2k}\)
Step 1: \(k=\dfrac{0.80-0.50}{20}=0.015\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\).
Step 2: \(t_{1/2}=\dfrac{1.60}{2\times0.015}=53.3\ \mathrm{min}\).
Why: Doubling the initial concentration doubles the zero-order half-life, from 26.7 min (for 0.80 mol L⁻¹) to 53.3 min.
Why not the others: (A) A constant half-life is the mark of a first-order reaction. (B) It uses 0.50/20 (the concentration left, not the amount used up) for k. (D) It halves the half-life instead of doubling it; for zero order \(t_{1/2}\propto[A]_0\).
Key point: Zero order: \(t_{1/2}=[A]_0/2k\), so it grows with \([A]_0\).

B) \(\ce{[Cr(H2O)5Cl]Cl2.H2O}\); it is a hydrate isomer
C) \(\ce{[Cr(H2O)4Cl2]Cl.2H2O}\); it is a hydrate isomer
D) \(\ce{[Cr(H2O)5Cl]Cl2.H2O}\); it is a linkage isomer
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) \(\ce{[Cr(H2O)5Cl]Cl2.H2O}\); it is a hydrate isomer.
Concept: Only chloride ions outside the coordination sphere are precipitated at once by silver nitrate. Hydrate (solvate) isomers differ in whether water is a ligand or is outside the coordination sphere as water of crystallisation.
Given: \(n(\text{complex})=0.010\ \mathrm{mol};\ m(\ce{AgCl})=2.87\ \mathrm g;\ M(\ce{AgCl})=143.5\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)

Step 2: Ionisable chloride per complex = 0.020/0.010 = 2, so one Cl is a ligand: \(\ce{[Cr(H2O)5Cl]Cl2.H2O}\).
Why: The complex keeps six ligands around chromium: five water molecules and one chloride. One water molecule is outside the sphere. Since it differs from \(\ce{[Cr(H2O)6]Cl3}\) only in whether a water or a chloride is the ligand, the two are hydrate isomers.
Why not the others: (A) \(\ce{[Cr(H2O)6]Cl3}\) would give 3 mol AgCl per mol, i.e. 4.31 g. (C) This complex has only one ionisable chloride and would give 1.435 g of AgCl. (D) Linkage isomers differ in the donor atom of an ambidentate ligand (such as \(\ce{NO2-}\)); neither water nor chloride is ambidentate.
Key point: Moles of AgCl precipitated at once = chloride ions outside the coordination sphere.
