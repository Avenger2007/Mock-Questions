sub_topic: Building a DNA double helix step by step from nucleotides, and the base-pairing rules that let one strand specify the other in DNA and RNA
Q: Which sequence of steps for assembling a DNA double helix, with the accompanying facts, is correct?
A) A nitrogenous base joins the 1′ carbon of a pentose by an N-glycosidic bond to form a nucleoside → a phosphate joins the 5′-OH of the sugar by a phosphoester bond to form a nucleotide → nucleotides link through 3′–5′ phosphodiester bonds into a polynucleotide chain → two antiparallel chains pair by hydrogen bonds, A with T (two bonds) and G with C (three bonds), and coil into a right-handed helix of pitch 3.4 nm with about 10 bp per turn; in RNA, uracil replaces thymine and pairs with adenine
B) Phosphodiester bonds form between bases of opposite strands, and the two strands run parallel
C) A pairs with G and T with C, so purines always face purines across the helix
D) RNA contains thymine, and its sugar lacks the 2′-OH group
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) Nucleoside → nucleotide → polynucleotide → antiparallel double helix with A=T, G≡C pairing.
Concept: A nucleotide has three components — a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar (ribose in RNA, deoxyribose in DNA) and a phosphate group. A nitrogenous base is linked to the pentose sugar through an N-glycosidic linkage to form a nucleoside, such as adenosine or deoxyadenosine; when a phosphate group is linked to the 5′-OH of a nucleoside through a phosphoester linkage, a nucleotide is formed. Two nucleotides are linked through a 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkage to form a dinucleotide, and more nucleotides join to form a polynucleotide; a polymer thus formed has at one end a free phosphate moiety at the 5′ end of the sugar and at the other end a free 3′-OH group. The backbone is formed by sugar and phosphates, and the bases project inside. Watson and Crick, using the X-ray diffraction data of Wilkins and Franklin, proposed the double helix: it is made of two polynucleotide chains with antiparallel polarity (one 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′); the bases in the two strands are paired through hydrogen bonds, adenine forming two hydrogen bonds with thymine and guanine three with cytosine, so a purine always comes opposite a pyrimidine, giving a uniform distance between the strands; the two chains are coiled in a right-handed fashion, the pitch of the helix is 3.4 nm and there are roughly 10 bp in each turn, the distance between bp being about 0.34 nm; the plane of one base pair stacks over the other in the double helix, which, in addition to hydrogen bonds, confers stability. In RNA every nucleotide has an additional –OH group at the 2′ position of ribose, and uracil is found in place of thymine (5-methyl uracil).
Step 1: Base + sugar (N-glycosidic) = nucleoside; + phosphate = nucleotide.
Step 2: 3′–5′ phosphodiester bonds give a chain with 5′ and 3′ ends.
Step 3: Two antiparallel chains pair (A=T, G≡C) and coil right-handedly.
Why: Complementary pairing of a purine with a pyrimidine keeps the helix uniform and lets each strand act as a template for the other.
Why not the others: (B) Phosphodiester bonds join nucleotides within a strand; strands are antiparallel. (C) Purines pair with pyrimidines. (D) RNA has uracil and a 2′-OH.
Key point: Antiparallel strands; A=T (2 H-bonds), G≡C (3 H-bonds); 0.34 nm per bp, ~10 bp per turn.

sub_topic: Interpreting anatomical, embryological and molecular observations as evidence of homology, analogy, divergent and convergent evolution
Q: A student records: (i) forelimbs of whale, bat, cheetah and human have the same bones but do different jobs; (ii) wings of butterflies and birds do the same job but have different structures; (iii) the eyes of octopus and of mammals look alike; (iv) thorns of Bougainvillea and tendrils of Cucurbita arise in the same position; (v) similar proteins and genes perform a given function among diverse organisms. Which interpretation is correct?
A) (i) and (iv) are analogous structures showing convergent evolution; (ii) and (iii) are homologous structures
B) All five show that the organisms have no common ancestry
C) (i) and (iv) are homologous structures — similar origin, different functions — showing divergent evolution; (ii) and (iii) are analogous structures — similar function, different origin — showing convergent evolution; (v) molecular homology, like anatomical homology, points to common ancestry
D) Only (v) is evidence of evolution, because anatomy can change during life
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) Homology (divergent) for (i), (iv); analogy (convergent) for (ii), (iii); molecular homology for (v).
Concept: Comparative anatomy and morphology show similarities and differences among organisms of today and those that existed years ago; such similarities can be interpreted to understand whether common ancestors were shared or not. Whales, bats, cheetah and human (all mammals) share similarities in the pattern of bones of forelimbs: though these forelimbs perform different functions, they have similar anatomical structure — all of them have humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges; in these animals, the same structure developed along different directions due to adaptations to different needs; this is divergent evolution and these structures are homologous; homology indicates common ancestry; other examples are the vertebrate hearts or brains, and in plants the thorn and tendrils of Bougainvillea and Cucurbita. Wings of butterfly and of birds look alike; they are not anatomically similar structures though they perform similar functions; hence analogous structures are a result of convergent evolution — different structures evolving for the same function and hence having similarity; other examples are the eye of the octopus and of mammals, or the flippers of penguins and dolphins; sweet potato (root modification) and potato (stem modification) are another example of analogy. In the same line of argument, similarities in proteins and genes performing a given function among diverse organisms give clues to common ancestry; these biochemical similarities point to the same shared ancestry as structural similarities among diverse organisms.
Why: Shared internal plan despite different use reveals inheritance from a common ancestor, whereas shared use without shared plan reveals similar selection pressures.
Why not the others: (A) The labels are reversed. (B) Homology is evidence of common ancestry. (D) Comparative anatomy is valid evidence.
Key point: Homologous = same origin, different function (divergent); analogous = same function, different origin (convergent).

Answer: (B) X stabilising, Y directional, Z disruptive.

sub_topic: Reading a table of restriction enzyme recognition sites to identify palindromes, sticky ends and fragment numbers, and the role of DNA ligase
Q: A student studies these enzymes:

Enzyme | Recognition site (5′→3′) | Cut
EcoRI | GAATTC | G^AATTC
BamHI | GGATCC | G^GATCC
SmaI | CCCGGG | CCC^GGG

A linear DNA has 3 EcoRI sites, and a circular plasmid has 2 BamHI sites. How many fragments does each give, and which statement is correct?
A) 3 and 2; SmaI produces sticky ends
B) 4 and 3; recognition sites are random sequences
C) 4 and 2; all three sites are palindromes (the same 5′→3′ on both strands); EcoRI and BamHI cut a little away from the centre, leaving single-stranded overhangs (sticky ends) that pair with any fragment cut by the same enzyme, while SmaI cuts in the middle to give blunt ends; DNA ligase then joins the sugar–phosphate backbones
D) 2 and 2; ligase cuts DNA at specific sites
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) 4 fragments from linear DNA, 2 from the plasmid; palindromes; sticky vs blunt ends; ligase joins.
Concept: In 1963, the two enzymes responsible for restricting the growth of bacteriophage in Escherichia coli were isolated; one of these added methyl groups to DNA, while the other cut DNA; the latter was called restriction endonuclease. The first letter of the name comes from the genus and the second two letters from the species of the prokaryotic cell from which they were isolated, e.g. EcoRI comes from Escherichia coli RY 13; R is derived from the name of the strain; Roman numbers following the names indicate the order in which the enzymes were isolated from that strain. Each restriction endonuclease functions by inspecting the length of a DNA sequence; once it finds its specific recognition sequence, it binds to the DNA and cuts each of the two strands of the double helix at specific points in their sugar–phosphate backbones; each restriction endonuclease recognises a specific palindromic nucleotide sequence in the DNA; the palindrome in DNA is a sequence of base pairs that reads the same on the two strands when orientation of reading is kept the same. Restriction enzymes cut the strand of DNA a little away from the centre of the palindrome sites, but between the same two bases on the opposite strands; this leaves single-stranded portions at the ends; there are overhanging stretches called sticky ends on each strand; these are named so because they form hydrogen bonds with their complementary cut counterparts; this stickiness of the ends facilitates the action of the enzyme DNA ligase. A linear DNA with n cut sites gives n + 1 fragments, whereas a circular DNA with n sites gives n fragments.
Given: Linear DNA, 3 sites; circular plasmid, 2 sites.
Formula/Principle: Linear: n + 1 fragments; circular: n fragments.
Step 1: Linear: 3 + 1 = 4.
Step 2: Circular: 2.
Step 3: G^AATTC and G^GATCC leave 5′ overhangs (AATT, GATC); CCC^GGG is blunt.
Why: The palindrome lets the enzyme cut both strands at equivalent positions, and offset cuts leave complementary overhangs.
Why not the others: (A) Linear DNA gives n + 1 fragments; SmaI gives blunt ends. (B) Circular DNA gives n fragments; sites are palindromes. (D) Ligase joins DNA; it does not cut.
Key point: Palindromic sites; offset cut → sticky ends; linear n + 1, circular n fragments.

sub_topic: The sequence of phases in logistic population growth and how it differs from exponential growth
Q: Which sequence describing logistic growth of a population introduced into a new habitat is correct?
A) Rapid exponential phase → lag phase → asymptote → decline to zero
B) Growth stays exponential forever because resources are unlimited in nature
C) The population reaches the carrying capacity first and then accelerates
D) Lag phase (few individuals adjusting) → acceleration → deceleration as resources become limiting → asymptote at the carrying capacity K, giving a sigmoid curve described by dN/dt = rN[(K − N)/K]; exponential growth, dN/dt = rN, gives a J-shaped curve and occurs only when resources are unlimited
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) Lag → acceleration → deceleration → asymptote at K (sigmoid).
Concept: If in a habitat resources are unlimited, each species has the ability to realise fully its innate potential to grow in number; the population then grows in an exponential or geometric fashion: dN/dt = rN, where r is the intrinsic rate of natural increase; this gives a J-shaped curve, and Nt = N₀e^(rt). No population of any species in nature has at its disposal unlimited resources to permit exponential growth; this leads to competition between individuals for limited resources; eventually the 'fittest' individual will survive and reproduce; a given habitat has enough resources to support a maximum possible number, beyond which no further growth is possible; let us call this limit the carrying capacity (K) for that species in that habitat. A population growing in a habitat with limited resources shows initially a lag phase, followed by phases of acceleration and deceleration and finally an asymptote, when the population density reaches the carrying capacity; a plot of N in relation to time (t) results in a sigmoid curve; this type of population growth is called Verhulst–Pearl logistic growth: dN/dt = rN[(K − N)/K]. Since resources for growth for most animal populations are finite and become limiting sooner or later, the logistic growth model is considered a more realistic one.
Step 1: Lag phase: small N, slow growth.
Step 2: Acceleration while (K − N)/K ≈ 1; deceleration as N approaches K.
Step 3: Asymptote: dN/dt = 0 when N = K.
Why: The factor (K − N)/K shrinks as the population grows, slowing growth to zero at the carrying capacity.
Why not the others: (A) The order is reversed and the curve does not fall to zero. (B) Resources are always limited in nature. (C) Growth slows near K.
Key point: Logistic: sigmoid, levels off at K; exponential: J-shaped.

sub_topic: Arranging monerans and protists in order of increasing cellular complexity, from archaebacteria to eukaryotic protozoans, with the distinguishing features of each group
Q: Which sequence and description is correct?
A) Protozoans → diatoms → eubacteria → archaebacteria, since protozoans are the simplest
B) Archaebacteria (prokaryotes with a distinct cell wall, living in extreme habitats — halophiles, thermoacidophiles, and methanogens in the guts of ruminants that produce methane) → eubacteria (prokaryotes with a rigid cell wall; includes cyanobacteria such as Nostoc and Anabaena that fix nitrogen in heterocysts) → chrysophytes (eukaryotic diatoms with silica walls forming diatomaceous earth) → dinoflagellates (red tides) and euglenoids (pellicle, photosynthetic in light) → slime moulds (saprophytic, plasmodium, spores) → protozoans (heterotrophs: amoeboid, flagellated, ciliated, sporozoans such as Plasmodium)
C) Euglenoids have a cellulose cell wall, and archaebacteria have a nucleus
D) Cyanobacteria are eukaryotic algae that form red tides
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) Archaebacteria → eubacteria → chrysophytes → dinoflagellates/euglenoids → slime moulds → protozoans.
Concept: Kingdom Monera consists of bacteria, the sole members of which are prokaryotes. Archaebacteria are special since they live in some of the most harsh habitats such as extreme salty areas (halophiles), hot springs (thermoacidophiles) and marshy areas (methanogens); archaebacteria differ from other bacteria in having a different cell wall structure, and this feature is responsible for their survival in extreme conditions; methanogens are present in the gut of several ruminant animals such as cows and buffaloes and they are responsible for the production of methane (biogas) from the dung of these animals. Eubacteria ('true bacteria') are characterised by the presence of a rigid cell wall and, if motile, a flagellum; the cyanobacteria (blue-green algae) have chlorophyll a similar to green plants and are photosynthetic autotrophs; some of these organisms, e.g. Nostoc and Anabaena, can fix atmospheric nitrogen in specialised cells called heterocysts. All single-celled eukaryotes are placed under Protista. Chrysophytes include diatoms and golden algae (desmids); in diatoms the cell walls form two thin overlapping shells embedded with silica, and they have left behind large amounts of cell wall deposits in their habitat, called 'diatomaceous earth'. Dinoflagellates are mostly marine and photosynthetic; red dinoflagellates (Gonyaulax) undergo such rapid multiplication that they make the sea appear red (red tides). Euglenoids are mostly fresh-water organisms; instead of a cell wall, they have a protein-rich layer called pellicle which makes their body flexible; though they are photosynthetic in the presence of sunlight, when deprived of sunlight they behave like heterotrophs. Slime moulds are saprophytic protists; under suitable conditions they form an aggregation called plasmodium, and during unfavourable conditions it differentiates and forms fruiting bodies bearing spores. All protozoans are heterotrophs and live as predators or parasites; they are believed to be primitive relatives of animals; there are four major groups: amoeboid, flagellated, ciliated and sporozoans (Plasmodium, which causes malaria).
Step 1: Prokaryotes first: archaebacteria, then eubacteria.
Step 2: Photosynthetic protists: chrysophytes, dinoflagellates, euglenoids.
Step 3: Saprophytic slime moulds, then heterotrophic protozoans (animal relatives).
Why: The order moves from prokaryotic cells to eukaryotic cells and from autotrophic protists towards animal-like heterotrophs.
Why not the others: (A) Prokaryotes are simpler than protists. (C) Euglenoids have a pellicle; archaebacteria lack a nucleus. (D) Cyanobacteria are prokaryotes; red tides are caused by dinoflagellates.
Key point: Monera (archaebacteria, eubacteria) → Protista (chrysophytes, dinoflagellates, euglenoids, slime moulds, protozoans).

Q: Which reasoning about incomplete dominance and co-dominance is correct?
A) In co-dominance the heterozygote shows an intermediate blend, as in pink snapdragons
B) The ABO gene has two alleles, so only three genotypes are possible
C) The gene I has three alleles — Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i; Iᴬ and Iᴮ produce slightly different forms of a sugar on the RBC surface, while i produces none → Iᴬ and Iᴮ are completely dominant over i, but when both are present each expresses its own sugar (co-dominance), giving blood group AB → with three alleles, six genotypes and four phenotypes (A, B, AB, O) are possible; in snapdragon, by contrast, Rr is pink because one R allele cannot make enough pigment, so the F₂ shows 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white (incomplete dominance)
D) An IᴬIᴮ × ii cross can give an O child
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) ABO: three alleles, co-dominance of Iᴬ and Iᴮ, 6 genotypes, 4 phenotypes; snapdragon: incomplete dominance, 1 : 2 : 1.
Concept: When the cross between two true-breeding parents gives an F₁ that does not resemble either parent but is in between the two, the phenomenon is called incomplete dominance; the inheritance of flower colour in the dog flower (snapdragon or Antirrhinum sp.) is a good example: in a cross between true-breeding red-flowered (RR) and true-breeding white-flowered plants (rr), the F₁ (Rr) was pink; when the F₁ was self-pollinated the F₂ resulted in the ratio 1 (RR) red : 2 (Rr) pink : 1 (rr) white. In co-dominance, the F₁ generation resembles both parents; a good example is the different types of red blood cells that determine ABO blood grouping in humans; ABO blood groups are controlled by the gene I; the plasma membrane of the red blood cell has sugar polymers that protrude from its surface, and the kind of sugar is controlled by the gene; the gene I has three alleles Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i; the alleles Iᴬ and Iᴮ produce a slightly different form of the sugar while allele i does not produce any sugar; because humans are diploid, each person possesses any two of the three I gene alleles; Iᴬ and Iᴮ are completely dominant over i — in other words, when Iᴬ and i are present only Iᴬ expresses (because i does not produce any sugar), and when Iᴮ and i are present Iᴮ expresses; but when Iᴬ and Iᴮ are present together they both express their own types of sugars; this is because of co-dominance; hence red blood cells have both A and B types of sugars; since there are three different alleles, there are six different combinations of these three alleles possible, and therefore a total of six different genotypes of the human ABO blood types; ABO blood grouping also provides a good example of multiple alleles.
Step 1: Alleles: Iᴬ, Iᴮ (sugar forms A, B), i (no sugar).
Step 2: Iᴬ, Iᴮ dominant over i; together co-dominant (AB).
Step 3: Genotypes = 3 + 3 = 6 (IᴬIᴬ, Iᴬi, IᴮIᴮ, Iᴮi, IᴬIᴮ, ii); phenotypes = 4.
Why: In co-dominance each allele makes its own functional product, whereas in incomplete dominance one allele's product is simply insufficient for the full phenotype.
Why not the others: (A) That describes incomplete dominance. (B) There are three alleles and six genotypes. (D) IᴬIᴮ × ii gives only Iᴬi (A) and Iᴮi (B).
Key point: Incomplete dominance → blend (1 : 2 : 1); co-dominance → both expressed (AB); ABO: 6 genotypes, 4 phenotypes.
