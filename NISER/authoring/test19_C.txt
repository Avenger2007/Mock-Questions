At very high pressure molecules are close together, and the volume occupied by molecules themselves becomes significant, so the free volume is V − nb. The deviation is measured by the compressibility factor Z = PV/nRT; for an ideal gas Z = 1 at all pressures. For gases with Z < 1, attractive forces are dominant and the gas is more compressible (negative deviation); for Z > 1 the gas is less compressible (positive deviation) because repulsive forces dominate. The temperature at which a real gas obeys the ideal gas law over an appreciable range of pressure is called the Boyle temperature or Boyle point; above it real gases show positive deviation, below it they first show negative deviation. Gases show ideal behaviour when the volume occupied is large, i.e. at low pressure and high temperature.
Why: Whether a gas is more or less compressible than ideal depends on whether attraction or molecular size has the larger effect under the given conditions.
Why not the others: (A) H₂ and He have very weak attractions. (B) CO₂ deviates strongly because of strong attractions. (D) Z depends on pressure for real gases.
Key point: Z < 1 attraction; Z > 1 molecular volume; ideal at low P, high T.
