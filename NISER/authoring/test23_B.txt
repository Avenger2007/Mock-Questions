Answer: (D) Three alleles, co-dominance of I^A and I^B, six genotypes and four phenotypes; AB × O gives A and B; PKU is pleiotropic.
Concept: ABO blood grouping provides a good example of multiple alleles. Blood groups are controlled by the gene I; the plasma membrane of the red blood cell has sugar polymers that protrude from its surface, and the kind of sugar is controlled by the gene. The gene I has three alleles, I^A, I^B and i; the alleles I^A and I^B produce a slightly different form of the sugar while allele i does not produce any sugar. Because humans are diploid, each person possesses any two of the three I gene alleles; I^A and I^B are completely dominant over i — when I^A and i are present only I^A expresses — but when I^A and I^B are present together they both express their own types of sugars, because of co-dominance; hence red blood cells have both A and B types of sugars. Since there are three different alleles, there are six different combinations and therefore six genotypes of human ABO blood types, but only four phenotypes. Multiple alleles can be found only when population studies are made, because an individual carries only two. A single gene can exhibit multiple phenotypic expression; such a gene is called a pleiotropic gene; the underlying mechanism of pleiotropy in most cases is the effect of a gene on metabolic pathways which contribute towards different phenotypes; an example is phenylketonuria, a disease caused by a mutation in the gene that codes for the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase (single gene mutation), manifested through phenotypic expression characterised by mental retardation and a reduction in hair and skin pigmentation.

Formula/Principle: Segregation of alleles; Punnett square.
Step 1: Gametes: I^A, I^B from AB; i from O.
Step 2: Offspring I^Ai (A) and I^Bi (B), 1 : 1.
Step 3: Genotypes: 3 homozygous + 3 heterozygous = 6; phenotypes A, B, AB, O = 4.
Why: Each functional allele makes its own enzyme product, so both show in the heterozygote, while the non-functional allele contributes nothing.
Why not the others: (A) One gene with three alleles; six genotypes, four phenotypes. (B) i adds no sugar. (C) That describes polygenic inheritance.

SOL:
Answer: (A) 2.2 m and about 1.6 mm; ≈ 3.3 × 10⁷ nucleosomes; histone octamer, beads-on-string, NHC proteins, euchromatin and heterochromatin.
Concept: The distance between two consecutive base pairs is 0.34 nm (0.34 × 10⁻⁹ m); if the length of DNA double helix in a typical mammalian cell is calculated (simply by multiplying the total number of bp with the distance between two consecutive bp, that is, 6.6 × 10⁹ bp × 0.34 × 10⁻⁹ m/bp), it comes out to be approximately 2.2 metres — a length far greater than the dimension of a typical nucleus (approximately 10⁻⁶ m). In prokaryotes such as E. coli, though they do not have a defined nucleus, the DNA is not scattered throughout the cell; DNA (being negatively charged) is held with some proteins (that have positive charges) in a region termed the nucleoid. Nucleosomes constitute the repeating unit of a structure in the nucleus called chromatin; the nucleosomes in chromatin are seen as 'beads-on-string' under the electron microscope; the beads-on-string structure in chromatin is packaged to form chromatin fibres that are further coiled and condensed at metaphase to form chromosomes; the packaging of chromatin at higher level requires an additional set of proteins collectively referred to as non-histone chromosomal (NHC) proteins. In a typical nucleus, some regions of chromatin are loosely packed (and stain light) and are referred to as euchromatin; the chromatin that is more densely packed and stains dark is called heterochromatin; euchromatin is said to be transcriptionally active and heterochromatin inactive.

Step 2: 4.6 × 10⁶ × 0.34 × 10⁻⁹ m = 1.56 × 10⁻³ m ≈ 1.6 mm.
Step 3: 6.6 × 10⁹/200 = 3.3 × 10⁷ nucleosomes.
Why: Opposite charges let DNA wind tightly around histones, achieving the huge compaction needed to fit metres of DNA into a micrometre-sized nucleus.
Why not the others: (B) Length off by ten; histones are positive. (C) Length and nucleosome count wrong; heterochromatin is inactive. (D) Prokaryotes lack histone-wrapped DNA in a membrane-bound nucleus.
Key point: Length = bp × 0.34 nm; histone octamer + 200 bp = nucleosome.

The most spectacular and evolutionarily fascinating examples of mutualism are found in plant–animal relationships; in many species of fig trees, there is a tight one-to-one relationship with the pollinator species of wasp; a given fig species can be pollinated only by its 'partner' wasp species and no other species; the female wasp uses the fruit not only as an oviposition (egg-laying) site but uses the developing seeds within the fruit for nourishing its larvae; the wasp pollinates the fig inflorescence while searching for suitable egg-laying sites; in return for the favour of pollination, the fig offers the wasp some of its developing seeds as food for the developing wasp larvae. Why: In mutualism each partner supplies something the other cannot get easily, and in tight mutualisms the partners coevolve.
Why not the others: (A) Lichens are mutualistic. (C) In commensalism only one partner benefits. (D) Egrets eat insects flushed out by cattle; cattle are unharmed.
Key point: Mutualism (+/+): lichen, mycorrhiza, fig–wasp, Ophrys–bee; commensalism (+/0): epiphyte, egret, barnacle.

SOL:
Answer: (B) Correct features of the four classes, including the reason Deuteromycetes are 'imperfect'.
Concept: Fungi reproduce vegetatively by fragmentation, fission and budding; asexually by spores called conidia, sporangiospores or zoospores; and sexually by oospores, ascospores and basidiospores. The sexual cycle involves three steps: (i) fusion of protoplasms between two motile or non-motile gametes, called plasmogamy; (ii) fusion of two nuclei, called karyogamy; (iii) meiosis in the zygote resulting in haploid spores. In some fungi the fusion of two haploid cells immediately results in diploid cells; in other fungi (ascomycetes and basidiomycetes), an intervening dikaryotic stage (n + n, two nuclei per cell) occurs; such a condition is called a dikaryon and the phase is called the dikaryophase. The mucosa of the stomach has gastric glands, which have three major types of cells: mucus neck cells, which secrete mucus; peptic or chief cells, which secrete the proenzyme pepsinogen; and parietal or oxyntic cells, which secrete HCl and intrinsic factor (needed for absorption of vitamin B₁₂); pepsinogen, on exposure to hydrochloric acid, gets converted into the active enzyme pepsin, the proteolytic enzyme of the stomach; pepsin converts proteins into proteoses and peptones; HCl provides the acidic pH (pH 1.8) optimal for pepsins. The bile released into the duodenum contains bile pigments (bilirubin and biliverdin), bile salts, cholesterol and phospholipids, but no enzymes; bile helps in emulsification of fats, i.e. breaking down of the fats into very small micelles; bile also activates lipases. The pancreatic juice contains inactive enzymes — trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, procarboxypeptidases, amylases, lipases and nucleases; trypsinogen is activated by an enzyme, enterokinase, secreted by the intestinal mucosa into active trypsin, which in turn activates the other enzymes in the pancreatic juice. Enzymes are proteins, so heating denatures them and extremes of pH inactivate them.
Why: Only the tube with active enzyme at a suitable pH breaks down starch, so the iodine colour disappears only there.
Why not the others: (A) Saliva contains amylase, not pepsin. (C) Bile is from the liver and acts on fats. (D) Without active enzyme, starch is not digested in this time.
Key point: Salivary amylase (pH 6.8) digests starch; enzymes are denatured by heat and inactivated by extreme pH.

sub_topic: Comparing amoeboid, ciliary and muscular movement and the structure, control and locations of skeletal, visceral and cardiac muscles
Q: Which comparison of types of movement and muscle is correct?
A) Ciliary movement moves macrophages and leucocytes, while amoeboid movement clears dust from the trachea
B) Visceral muscles are striated and voluntary, found in the limbs
C) Amoeboid movement, by streaming of protoplasm and pseudopodia with microfilaments, is seen in macrophages and leucocytes; ciliary movement occurs in internal tubular organs lined by ciliated epithelium — coordinated beats of cilia in the trachea remove dust particles and foreign substances, and in the female reproductive tract help move the ova; muscular movement moves limbs, jaws and tongue; skeletal muscles are striated and voluntary and attached to bones; visceral muscles, in the inner walls of hollow organs such as the alimentary canal and reproductive tract, are smooth (non-striated) and involuntary; cardiac muscle is striated, branched and involuntary and is found only in the heart
D) Cardiac muscle is smooth and under voluntary control
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) Amoeboid, ciliary and muscular movements correctly placed; skeletal (striated, voluntary), visceral (smooth, involuntary), cardiac (striated, involuntary).
Concept: Cells of the human body exhibit three main types of movements, namely amoeboid, ciliary and muscular. Some specialised cells in our body like macrophages and leucocytes in blood exhibit amoeboid movement; it is effected by pseudopodia formed by the streaming of protoplasm (as in Amoeba); cytoskeletal elements like microfilaments are also involved in amoeboid movement. Ciliary movement occurs in most of our internal tubular organs which are lined by ciliated epithelium; the coordinated movements of cilia in the trachea help us in removing dust particles and some of the foreign substances inhaled along with the atmospheric air; passage of ova through the female reproductive tract is also facilitated by ciliary movement. Movement of our limbs, jaws, tongue, etc., requires muscular movement; the contractile property of muscles is effectively used for locomotion and other movements by human beings and the majority of multicellular organisms. Muscles have been classified using different criteria, namely location, appearance and nature of regulation of their activities; based on their location, three types of muscles are identified: (i) skeletal, (ii) visceral and (iii) cardiac. Skeletal muscles are closely associated with the skeletal components of the body; they have a striped appearance under the microscope and hence are called striated muscles; as their activities are under the voluntary control of the nervous system, they are known as voluntary muscles too; they are primarily involved in locomotory actions and changes of body postures. Visceral muscles are located in the inner walls of hollow visceral organs of the body like the alimentary canal, reproductive tract, etc.; they do not exhibit any striation and are smooth in appearance; hence, they are called smooth muscles (non-striated muscles); their activities are not under the voluntary control of the nervous system and they are therefore known as involuntary muscles; they assist, for example, in the transportation of food through the digestive tract and gametes through the genital tract. As the name suggests, cardiac muscles are the muscles of the heart; many cardiac muscle cells assemble in a branching pattern to form a cardiac muscle; based on appearance, cardiac muscles are striated; they are involuntary in nature as the nervous system does not control their activities directly.
Why: Each type of movement and muscle is suited to its site — moving cells, moving fluid over surfaces, or moving body parts.
Why not the others: (A) The two movement types are swapped. (B) Visceral muscles are smooth and involuntary. (D) Cardiac muscle is striated and involuntary.
Key point: Skeletal: striated, voluntary; visceral: smooth, involuntary; cardiac: striated, involuntary.

Q: Three patients have localised brain injuries. Patient X walks unsteadily and cannot coordinate quick movements. Patient Y has irregular breathing and heartbeat. Patient Z has lost control of body temperature, hunger and thirst. Which interpretation is correct?
A) X has damage to the cerebellum, which integrates information from the semicircular canals of the ear and the auditory system and coordinates movement; Y has damage to the medulla, which contains centres controlling respiration, cardiovascular reflexes and gastric secretions; Z has damage to the hypothalamus, which contains centres controlling body temperature and the urge for eating and drinking and also has neurosecretory cells that release hypothalamic hormones; the cerebral hemispheres, joined by the corpus callosum, handle sensory perception, voluntary motor control, memory and communication, and the limbic system with the hypothalamus regulates sexual behaviour and emotions
B) X has damage to the medulla and Y to the cerebellum
C) Z has damage to the cerebellum, which controls hunger and thirst
D) All three injuries are in the spinal cord, since the brain does not control these functions
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) X cerebellum, Y medulla, Z hypothalamus.
Concept: The human brain can be divided into three major parts: forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain. The forebrain consists of the cerebrum, thalamus and hypothalamus; the cerebrum forms the major part of the human brain; a deep cleft divides the cerebrum longitudinally into two halves, termed the left and right cerebral hemispheres, connected by a tract of nerve fibres called the corpus callosum; the cerebral cortex contains motor areas, sensory areas and large regions that are neither clearly sensory nor motor in function, called the association areas, responsible for complex functions like intersensory associations, memory and communication. The hypothalamus lies at the base of the thalamus and contains a number of centres which control body temperature and the urge for eating and drinking; it also contains several groups of neurosecretory cells which secrete hormones called hypothalamic hormones. The inner parts of the cerebral hemispheres and a group of associated deep structures like the amygdala and hippocampus form a complex structure called the limbic lobe or limbic system; along with the hypothalamus, it is involved in the regulation of sexual behaviour, expression of emotional reactions (e.g. excitement, pleasure, rage and fear) and motivation. The hindbrain comprises the pons, cerebellum and medulla (also called the medulla oblongata); pons consists of fibre tracts that interconnect different regions of the brain; the cerebellum has a very convoluted surface in order to provide additional space for many more neurons; the medulla of the brain is connected to the spinal cord and contains centres which control respiration, cardiovascular reflexes and gastric secretions. The cerebellum integrates information received from the semicircular canals of the ear and the auditory system, which is why its damage disturbs balance and coordination.
Why: Each brain region has a specific set of centres, so damage to it produces a specific set of symptoms.
Why not the others: (B) Cerebellum and medulla functions swapped. (C) Hunger and thirst centres are in the hypothalamus. (D) These functions are controlled by brain centres.
Key point: Cerebellum — coordination and balance; medulla — breathing and heart; hypothalamus — temperature, hunger, thirst.
