sub_topic: How one generation of selection against a recessive phenotype shifts allele frequency away from Hardy–Weinberg values
Q: In a large, randomly mating population, 16 % of individuals show a recessive phenotype (aa). For one generation, all aa individuals are prevented from breeding, while AA and Aa breed at random. What is the frequency of allele a among the breeding parents, and about what percentage of the next generation will be aa?
A) 0.40; 16 %, since Hardy–Weinberg proportions are always kept
B) About 0.29; about 8 %, because selection against aa changes the allele frequency, violating a Hardy–Weinberg condition
C) 0; 0 %, because all recessive alleles are removed in one generation
D) 0.20; 4 %
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) q ≈ 0.29; about 8 % aa in the next generation.
Concept: The Hardy–Weinberg principle states that allele and genotype frequencies stay constant from generation to generation (p² + 2pq + q² = 1) if there is no mutation, no migration (gene flow), no genetic drift, random mating and no natural selection. Any of these factors disturbs the equilibrium and causes evolution.
Given: q² = 0.16, so q = 0.40 and p = 0.60; AA = 0.36, Aa = 0.48, aa = 0.16.
Formula/Principle: among breeders, q′ = (frequency of a alleles) ÷ (all alleles) = (½ × Aa) ÷ (AA + Aa).
Step 1: Breeders: AA 0.36 + Aa 0.48 = 0.84; a alleles = ½ × 0.48 = 0.24; q′ = 0.24 ÷ 0.84 ≈ 0.286 (equivalently q′ = q/(1 + q) = 0.4/1.4).
Step 2: Next generation (random mating): aa = q′² ≈ 0.082, about 8 %.
Why: The a allele survives in heterozygotes, which are not removed, so the recessive phenotype falls but does not disappear; selection against a rare recessive becomes slower each generation because most a alleles hide in carriers.
Why not the others: (A) Selection is acting, so the frequencies do change. (C) The 48 % heterozygotes still carry a. (D) Halves q instead of calculating it from the breeders.
Key point: Selection against aa: q′ = q/(1 + q); carriers keep the allele in the population.

sub_topic: Measuring asymmetric competition from the fall in population density of two species grown together
Q: Two species X and Y feed on the same resource. Grown separately, X reaches a stable density of 400 per plot and Y of 250 per plot. Grown together, X stabilises at 300 and Y at 50. What is the percentage reduction in density for each species, and what do the results show?
A) X 75 %, Y 20 %; X is harmed more
B) X 25 %, Y 80 %; this is predation, with X preying on Y
C) X 25 %, Y 80 %; both are harmed (−/−), so it is competition, which is asymmetric because Y is affected much more, and with time Y may be excluded (Gause's competitive exclusion principle)
D) X 0 %, Y 100 %; only Y is affected, so it is amensalism
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) X falls by 25 %, Y by 80 %; asymmetric competition.
Concept: In interspecific competition both species are harmed (−/−), because each reduces the resources available to the other. Competition is often asymmetric: one species suffers far more. Gause's principle states that two species competing for the same limiting resource cannot coexist indefinitely; the inferior competitor is eventually eliminated, unless the species partition the resource.
Given: X: 400 alone, 300 together; Y: 250 alone, 50 together.
Formula/Principle: percentage reduction = (alone − together) ÷ alone × 100.
Step 1: X: (400 − 300) ÷ 400 × 100 = 25 %.
Step 2: Y: (250 − 50) ÷ 250 × 100 = 80 %.
Why: Both densities fall in mixed culture, so each harms the other; the much larger fall in Y shows that X is the superior competitor.
Why not the others: (A) Reverses the two percentages. (B) Predation is +/−; nothing shows X gaining, since its density falls too. (D) X is reduced by 25 %, so it is also affected.
Key point: Competition is −/−, often asymmetric; strong competition can exclude the weaker species.

Answer: (B) X has 2400 and Y has 2600 hydrogen bonds; Y is from the hot-spring bacterium.

Step 2: Y: 400 A–T pairs and 600 G–C pairs: 2(400) + 3(600) = 2600.
Why: Organisms living at high temperatures tend to have DNA rich in G≡C pairs, which remains double-stranded at temperatures that would separate A=T-rich DNA.
Why not the others: (A) Swaps the counts: more A means fewer hydrogen bonds. (C) Ignores the different base compositions. (D) The counts are right, but A=T pairs, with two hydrogen bonds, are less stable than G≡C pairs.
Key point: A=T has 2 hydrogen bonds, G≡C has 3; more GC means more stable DNA.
