sub_topic: Predicting phenotype numbers from crosses showing incomplete dominance (snapdragon flower colour) and co-dominance (AB blood group), where genotype and phenotype ratios coincide
Q: In snapdragon, RR plants are red, Rr pink and rr white. (i) Two pink plants are crossed and 240 seedlings raised. (ii) A pink plant is crossed with a white one and 120 seedlings raised. (iii) Two persons of blood group AB (IᴬIᴮ) have children. Which set of expected results is correct?
A) (i) 180 red : 60 white; (ii) all pink; (iii) all AB
B) (i) 60 red : 120 pink : 60 white; (ii) 60 red : 60 white; (iii) A and B only, 1 : 1
C) (i) 60 red : 120 pink : 60 white; (ii) 60 pink : 60 white; (iii) A : AB : B = 1 : 2 : 1 — in both incomplete dominance and co-dominance the heterozygote has its own phenotype, so the phenotypic ratio equals the genotypic ratio
D) (i) 120 red : 120 white; (ii) 120 pink; (iii) A : B : AB : O = 1 : 1 : 1 : 1
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) 60 : 120 : 60; 60 pink : 60 white; A : AB : B = 1 : 2 : 1.
Concept: In incomplete dominance the F₁ has a phenotype that does not resemble either of the two parents and is in between the two, as in the inheritance of flower colour in the dog flower (snapdragon, Antirrhinum sp.): in a cross between true-breeding red (RR) and white (rr), the F₁ (Rr) is pink, and on self-pollination the F₂ ratio is 1 (RR) red : 2 (Rr) pink : 1 (rr) white, so the genotypic ratio is exactly like any Mendelian monohybrid cross but the phenotypic ratio has changed from 3 : 1 to 1 : 2 : 1. In co-dominance the F₁ generation resembles both parents, as in the ABO blood groups, where Iᴬ and Iᴮ are both expressed when present together, giving blood group AB.
Given: (i) Rr × Rr, 240 seedlings; (ii) Rr × rr, 120 seedlings; (iii) IᴬIᴮ × IᴬIᴮ.
Formula/Principle: Monohybrid segregation; each heterozygote distinguishable.
Step 1: (i) RR : Rr : rr = 1 : 2 : 1 ⇒ 60 red, 120 pink, 60 white.
Step 2: (ii) Rr × rr ⇒ Rr : rr = 1 : 1 ⇒ 60 pink, 60 white.
Step 3: (iii) IᴬIᴬ : IᴬIᴮ : IᴮIᴮ = 1 : 2 : 1 ⇒ A : AB : B = 1 : 2 : 1.
Why: Because neither allele masks the other, every genotype has its own phenotype, so phenotypes can be counted directly as genotypes.
Why not the others: (A) Assumes complete dominance with wrong ratios. (B) Pink × white cannot give red; AB × AB gives AB children too. (D) Ratios wrong; O needs an i allele, absent here.
Key point: Incomplete dominance and co-dominance: phenotypic ratio = genotypic ratio (1 : 2 : 1).

Q: Which sequence and description of DNA packaging in a eukaryotic nucleus is correct?
A) DNA first folds into chromosomes, which then wrap round histones to form nucleosomes; heterochromatin is lightly stained and transcriptionally active
B) Histones are negatively charged proteins rich in glutamate and aspartate, which bind the negatively charged DNA directly
C) Nucleosomes are formed by RNA wrapped round histone octamers
D) Negatively charged DNA wraps round a positively charged histone octamer (histones are rich in lysine and arginine) to form a nucleosome of about 200 bp; nucleosomes form the "beads-on-string" structure seen in chromatin, which is coiled further into chromatin fibres and, at metaphase, condensed into chromosomes, the higher-level packaging requiring non-histone chromosomal (NHC) proteins; loosely packed, lightly stained euchromatin is transcriptionally active, while densely packed, dark-stained heterochromatin is inactive
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) DNA + histone octamer → nucleosome → beads-on-string → chromatin fibre → chromosome; NHC proteins; euchromatin active, heterochromatin inactive.
Concept: In eukaryotes there is a set of positively charged, basic proteins called histones; a protein acquires charge depending upon the abundance of amino acid residues with charged side chains, and histones are rich in the basic amino acid residues lysine and arginine, both of which carry positive charges in their side chains. Histones are organised to form a unit of eight molecules called the histone octamer. The negatively charged DNA is wrapped around the positively charged histone octamer to form a structure called the nucleosome; a typical nucleosome contains 200 bp of DNA helix. Nucleosomes constitute the repeating unit of a structure in the nucleus called chromatin, thread-like stained bodies seen as "beads-on-string" under the electron microscope. The beads-on-string structure in chromatin is packaged to form chromatin fibres that are further coiled and condensed at metaphase stage of cell division to form chromosomes. The packaging of chromatin at a higher level requires an additional set of proteins collectively referred to as non-histone chromosomal (NHC) proteins. In a typical nucleus, some regions of chromatin are loosely packed and stain light (euchromatin) and are transcriptionally active; others are densely packed and stain dark (heterochromatin) and are inactive.
Step 1: Charge attraction: DNA (phosphate, −) + histone octamer (lysine, arginine, +) ⇒ nucleosome.
Step 2: Nucleosomes in series ⇒ beads-on-string chromatin ⇒ chromatin fibre.
Step 3: Further coiling with NHC proteins ⇒ metaphase chromosome.
Why: Packaging must neutralise the repulsion between phosphate groups, which positively charged histones do, before any higher folding is possible.
Why not the others: (A) Order reversed and euchromatin/heterochromatin swapped. (B) Histones are basic (positive). (C) Nucleosomes contain DNA.
Key point: Nucleosome (≈200 bp DNA + histone octamer) → chromatin → chromosome; euchromatin active.

B) After one generation all DNA forms a single band of intermediate (hybrid) density, and after two generations equal amounts of hybrid and light DNA appear — exactly as predicted if each daughter molecule keeps one parental strand (semiconservative replication); at the fork DNA polymerase synthesises new strands only in the 5′→3′ direction, so one strand is made continuously and the other discontinuously as Okazaki fragments joined by DNA ligase, beginning at a defined origin of replication

D) The result shows conservative replication, since heavy DNA disappears

SOL:
Answer: (B) Generation 1: all hybrid; generation 2: half hybrid, half light; polymerase works 5′→3′, lagging strand in Okazaki fragments joined by ligase.
Concept: Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl grew E. coli in a medium containing ¹⁵NH₄Cl (¹⁵N being the heavy isotope of nitrogen) as the only nitrogen source for many generations, so ¹⁵N was incorporated into newly synthesised DNA. The heavy DNA can be distinguished from normal DNA by centrifugation in a caesium chloride density gradient. They then transferred the cells into a medium with normal ¹⁴NH₄Cl and took samples at definite time intervals as the cells multiplied. The DNA extracted from the culture one generation after the transfer (i.e. after 20 minutes; E. coli divides in 20 minutes) had a hybrid or intermediate density; DNA extracted after another generation (after 40 minutes) was composed of equal amounts of this hybrid DNA and of light DNA. The main enzyme of replication is DNA-dependent DNA polymerase, which catalyses polymerisation only in one direction, 5′→3′; consequently, on one strand (template with polarity 3′→5′) replication is continuous, while on the other it is discontinuous, and the discontinuously synthesised fragments are later joined by the enzyme DNA ligase. Replication originates at a definite region of E. coli DNA called the origin of replication.
Given: ¹⁵N/¹⁵N parental DNA; growth in ¹⁴N.
Step 1: Generation 1: each molecule = one ¹⁵N strand + one ¹⁴N strand ⇒ single hybrid band.
Step 2: Generation 2: hybrid molecules give 1 hybrid + 1 light each ⇒ 50 % hybrid, 50 % light.
Step 3: Conservative replication would have given heavy + light bands after generation 1, which was not seen.
Why: Each parental strand serves as a template and stays paired with its new complementary strand.
Why not the others: (A) That is the conservative prediction. (C) Light DNA appears in the second generation. (D) The hybrid band rules out conservative replication.
Key point: Meselson–Stahl: hybrid in generation 1, hybrid + light in generation 2 ⇒ semiconservative.

In most ecosystems all the pyramids are upright; however, there are exceptions: if you count the number of insects feeding on a big tree, the pyramid is inverted, and the pyramid of biomass in the sea is also generally inverted because the biomass of fishes far exceeds that of phytoplankton. Why not the others: (A) The energy pyramid cannot be inverted. (B) One tree supports many insects, inverting the number pyramid. (C) Saprophytes are given no place.
Key point: Energy pyramid always upright; number and biomass pyramids can be inverted.
