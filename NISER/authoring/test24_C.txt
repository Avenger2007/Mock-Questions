Key point: lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp; lone pairs equatorial in tbp, trans in octahedral.

A) Phenol + bromine water gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol, while Br₂ in CS₂ at low temperature gives mainly mono-bromophenols (o- and p-); dilute HNO₃ at 298 K gives o- and p-nitrophenol (separable by steam distillation, the ortho isomer being steam volatile due to intramolecular H-bonding), while concentrated HNO₃ gives 2,4,6-trinitrophenol; sodium phenoxide + CO₂ under pressure followed by acid gives salicylic acid (Kolbe's reaction); phenol + CHCl₃ + aqueous NaOH followed by acid gives salicylaldehyde (Reimer–Tiemann reaction); –OH activates the ring and is ortho/para directing
B) Phenol + bromine water gives only m-bromophenol; –OH is meta directing
C) Kolbe's reaction gives benzaldehyde; Reimer–Tiemann gives benzoic acid
D) Dilute HNO₃ gives only picric acid; p-nitrophenol is steam volatile
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) Bromination, nitration, Kolbe and Reimer–Tiemann products correctly given; –OH is o/p directing and activating.
Concept: In phenols, the reactions that take place on the aromatic ring are electrophilic substitution reactions; the –OH group attached to the benzene ring activates it towards electrophilic substitution, and it also directs the incoming group to ortho and para positions in the ring as these positions become electron rich due to the resonance effect caused by the –OH group. With dilute nitric acid at low temperature (298 K), phenol yields a mixture of ortho and para nitrophenols; the ortho and para isomers can be separated by steam distillation — o-nitrophenol is steam volatile due to intramolecular hydrogen bonding while p-nitrophenol is less volatile due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding, which causes the association of molecules. With concentrated nitric acid, phenol is converted to 2,4,6-trinitrophenol, commonly known as picric acid; the yield of the reaction product is poor. On treating phenol with bromine, different reaction products are formed under different experimental conditions: when the reaction is carried out in solvents of low polarity such as CHCl₃ or CS₂ and at low temperature, monobromophenols are formed; the usual halogenation of benzene takes place in the presence of a Lewis acid, such as FeBr₃, which polarises the halogen molecule, but in the case of phenol the polarisation of the bromine molecule takes place even in the absence of a Lewis acid due to the highly activating effect of the –OH group attached to the benzene ring. When phenol is treated with bromine water, 2,4,6-tribromophenol is formed as a white precipitate. Kolbe's reaction: the phenoxide ion generated by treating phenol with sodium hydroxide is even more reactive than phenol towards electrophilic aromatic substitution; hence it undergoes electrophilic substitution with carbon dioxide, a weak electrophile, and ortho-hydroxybenzoic acid (salicylic acid) is formed as the main product. Reimer–Tiemann reaction: on treating phenol with chloroform in the presence of sodium hydroxide, a –CHO group is introduced at the ortho position of the benzene ring; the intermediate substituted benzal chloride is hydrolysed in the presence of alkali to produce salicylaldehyde.
Why: The –OH group strongly donates electron density by resonance to ortho and para positions, making these sites very reactive.
Why not the others: (B) Bromine water gives the tribromo product; –OH is o/p directing. (C) Kolbe gives salicylic acid; Reimer–Tiemann gives salicylaldehyde. (D) Dilute HNO₃ gives mononitrophenols; the ortho isomer is steam volatile.
Key point: Bromine water → 2,4,6-tribromophenol; Kolbe → salicylic acid; Reimer–Tiemann → salicylaldehyde.

Q: Three unlabelled liquids are ethanal, propanone and benzaldehyde. Which set of test results is correct?
A) All three give silver mirror with Tollens' reagent and red precipitate with Fehling's
B) Propanone gives silver mirror; ethanal does not react with Fehling's; benzaldehyde gives iodoform
C) Only propanone reduces Fehling's solution
D) Ethanal: Tollens' positive (silver mirror), Fehling's positive (red-brown Cu₂O), iodoform positive (has CH₃CO–); propanone: Tollens' and Fehling's negative, iodoform positive (yellow CHI₃); benzaldehyde: Tollens' positive but Fehling's negative (aromatic aldehydes do not respond), iodoform negative; Clemmensen (Zn–Hg/HCl) or Wolff–Kishner (NH₂NH₂ then KOH in ethylene glycol) reduces C=O to CH₂, and NaBH₄ reduces aldehydes to primary and ketones to secondary alcohols
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) Ethanal: Tollens' +, Fehling's +, iodoform +; propanone: iodoform only; benzaldehyde: Tollens' only.
Concept: Aldehydes differ from ketones in their oxidation reactions; aldehydes are easily oxidised to carboxylic acids on treatment with common oxidising agents like nitric acid, potassium permanganate, potassium dichromate, etc.; even mild oxidising agents, mainly Tollens' reagent and Fehling's reagent, also oxidise aldehydes. Ketones are generally oxidised under vigorous conditions, i.e., strong oxidising agents and at elevated temperatures. Tollens' test: on warming an aldehyde with freshly prepared ammoniacal silver nitrate solution (Tollens' reagent), a bright silver mirror is produced due to the formation of silver metal; the aldehydes are oxidised to the corresponding carboxylate anion. Fehling's test: Fehling reagent comprises two solutions, Fehling solution A (aqueous copper sulphate) and Fehling solution B (alkaline sodium potassium tartrate); these two solutions are mixed in equal amounts before the test; on heating an aldehyde with Fehling's reagent, a reddish-brown precipitate is obtained; aldehydes are oxidised to the corresponding carboxylate anion, and aromatic aldehydes do not respond to this test. Oxidation of methyl ketones by the haloform reaction: aldehydes and ketones having at least one methyl group linked to the carbonyl carbon atom (methyl ketones) are oxidised by sodium hypohalite to sodium salts of corresponding carboxylic acids having one carbon atom less than that of the carbonyl compound; the methyl group is converted to haloform, and the iodoform reaction with sodium hypoiodite is also used for detection of the CH₃CO group or CH₃CH(OH) group which produces CH₃CO group on oxidation. Aldehydes and ketones are reduced to primary and secondary alcohols respectively by sodium borohydride (NaBH₄) or lithium aluminium hydride (LiAlH₄) as well as by catalytic hydrogenation. The carbonyl group of aldehydes and ketones is reduced to a CH₂ group on treatment with zinc amalgam and concentrated hydrochloric acid (Clemmensen reduction) or with hydrazine followed by heating with sodium or potassium hydroxide in a high boiling solvent such as ethylene glycol (Wolff–Kishner reduction).
Why: Aldehydes are easily oxidised; Fehling's needs an aliphatic aldehyde; the iodoform test needs a CH₃CO– group.
Why not the others: (A) Propanone fails Tollens' and Fehling's; benzaldehyde fails Fehling's. (B) Propanone fails Tollens'; ethanal reduces Fehling's; benzaldehyde lacks CH₃CO–. (C) Propanone does not reduce Fehling's.
Key point: Tollens' all aldehydes; Fehling's aliphatic aldehydes only; iodoform needs CH₃CO–.

sub_topic: Ranking acid strength of substituted carboxylic acids using inductive effects, number and position of electron-withdrawing groups, and comparing benzoic acids with aliphatic acids
Q: Which order of acid strength is correct?
A) 4-chlorobutanoic acid > 3-chlorobutanoic acid > 2-chlorobutanoic acid
B) CH₃COOH > HCOOH > ClCH₂COOH > CF₃COOH
C) CF₃COOH > CCl₃COOH > CHCl₂COOH > ClCH₂COOH > HCOOH > CH₃COOH; 2-chlorobutanoic acid > 3-chlorobutanoic acid > 4-chlorobutanoic acid; p-nitrobenzoic acid > benzoic acid > p-methoxybenzoic acid; electron-withdrawing groups stabilise the carboxylate anion by dispersing its negative charge (–I effect, strongest when close to –COOH and increasing with number and electronegativity of groups), while electron-donating groups destabilise it and decrease acidity
D) p-Methoxybenzoic acid > benzoic acid > p-nitrobenzoic acid
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) All three orders correctly ranked by inductive and resonance effects.
Concept: Carboxylic acids are weaker than mineral acids but stronger than alcohols and many simple phenols; the higher acidity of carboxylic acids compared with phenols can be attributed to the conjugate base, carboxylate ion, being stabilised by two equivalent resonance structures in which the negative charge is at the more electronegative oxygen atom. Electron-withdrawing groups (EWG) increase the acidity of carboxylic acids by stabilising the conjugate base through delocalisation of the negative charge by inductive and/or resonance effects; conversely, electron-donating groups decrease the acidity by destabilising the conjugate base. The effect of the following groups in increasing acidity order is Ph < I < Br < Cl < F < CN < NO₂ < CF₃; thus, the following acids are arranged in order of increasing acidity (based on pKa values): CF₃COOH > CCl₃COOH > CCl₂HCOOH > NO₂CH₂COOH > NC–CH₂COOH > FCH₂COOH > ClCH₂COOH > BrCH₂COOH > HCOOH > ClCH₂CH₂COOH > C₆H₅COOH > C₆H₅CH₂COOH > CH₃COOH > CH₃CH₂COOH. Direct attachment of groups such as phenyl or vinyl to the carboxylic acid increases its acidity, contrary to the decrease expected due to the resonance effect, because of the greater electronegativity of the sp² hybridised carbon to which the carboxyl carbon is attached. The inductive effect decreases rapidly with distance, so a chlorine at the α-position increases acidity more than one at β or γ. The presence of an electron-withdrawing group on the phenyl of aromatic carboxylic acid increases their acidity while electron-donating groups decrease their acidity.
Why: Anything that spreads out the negative charge of the carboxylate ion favours ionisation.
Why not the others: (A) The –I effect weakens with distance. (B) Order reversed. (D) –NO₂ increases and –OCH₃ decreases acidity.
Key point: More, stronger and closer EWGs ⇒ stronger acid; EDGs weaken.
