Q: Three point charges of \(+2.0\ \mu\mathrm C\) each are placed at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side 0.30 m in air. What is the magnitude of the net force on any one of them? \(\left(\dfrac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}=9.0\times10^9\ \mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}\right)\)
A) 0.40 N
B) 0.80 N
C) 0.69 N
D) 0.35 N
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) 0.69 N.
Given: \(q=2.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm C,\quad a=0.30\ \mathrm m,\quad k=9.0\times10^9\ \mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}\)
Formula/Principle: \(F=\dfrac{kq^2}{a^2};\quad F_{\text{net}}=\sqrt{F^2+F^2+2F^2\cos60^\circ}=\sqrt3F\)
Step 1: \(F=\dfrac{9.0\times10^9\times(2.0\times10^{-6})^2}{(0.30)^2}=\dfrac{0.036}{0.090}=0.40\ \mathrm N\)
Step 2: \(\text{the two forces are repulsive, each along a side, with}\ 60^\circ\ \text{between them}\)
Worked derivation: \[F_{\text{net}}=\sqrt3\times0.40=0.69\ \mathrm N\]
Check: \[\text{components along the bisector:}\ 2F\cos30^\circ=2\times0.40\times0.866=0.69\ \mathrm N;\ \text{perpendicular components cancel}\]
Why: \(\text{forces add as vectors (principle of superposition)}\)
Why not the others: (A) \(0.40\ \mathrm N\ \text{is the force due to one charge only}\); (B) \(0.80\ \mathrm N=2F\ \text{adds the magnitudes as if the forces were parallel}\); (D) \(0.35\ \mathrm N=F\cos30^\circ\ \text{is one component of one force}\).
Key point: \(\text{two equal forces at}\ 60^\circ\ \text{give}\ \sqrt3F\)

sub_topic: Field and potential at the midpoint between two equal like charges
Q: Two point charges, each \(+q\), are fixed a distance \(d\) apart in air. With \(k=\dfrac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\) and the potential taken as zero at infinity, what are the electric field and the potential at the midpoint between them?
A) Field zero; potential \(\dfrac{4kq}d\)
B) Field zero; potential zero
C) Field \(\dfrac{8kq}{d^2}\); potential zero
D) Field zero; potential \(\dfrac{2kq}d\)
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) Field zero; potential \(\dfrac{4kq}d\).
Given: \(q_1=q_2=+q,\quad r_1=r_2=\dfrac d2\)
Formula/Principle: \(\vec E=\sum\dfrac{kq_i}{r_i^2}\hat r_i\ \text{(vector sum)};\quad V=\sum\dfrac{kq_i}{r_i}\ \text{(scalar sum)}\)
Step 1: \(E_1=E_2=\dfrac{kq}{(d/2)^2}=\dfrac{4kq}{d^2},\ \text{in opposite directions}\Rightarrow E=0\)
Step 2: \(V_1=V_2=\dfrac{kq}{d/2}=\dfrac{2kq}d\)
Worked derivation: \[E=0,\qquad V=\frac{2kq}d+\frac{2kq}d=\frac{4kq}d\]
Check: \[\text{work to bring}\ q_0\ \text{from infinity to the midpoint}=q_0V=\frac{4kqq_0}d\ne0,\ \text{although the force there is zero}\]
Why: \(\text{fields are vectors and cancel; potentials are scalars of the same sign and add}\)
Why not the others: (B) \(V=0\ \text{at the midpoint is for charges}\ +q\ \text{and}\ -q\); (C) \(\tfrac{8kq}{d^2}\ \text{and}\ V=0\ \text{are for charges}\ +q\ \text{and}\ -q\); (D) \(\tfrac{2kq}d\ \text{is the potential of one charge only}\).
Key point: \(E=0\ \text{does not mean}\ V=0\)

sub_topic: Drift speed against electric field at two temperatures
Q: The drift speed \(v_d\) of electrons in a copper wire is plotted against the applied electric field \(E\), for small fields, at 300 K and at 400 K. Which description of the two graphs is correct?
A) Both are straight lines through the origin; the 400 K line is steeper, because the electrons move faster when hot
B) Both are curves that flatten out, because the drift speed cannot exceed the thermal speed
C) Both are the same straight line, because the number density of electrons does not change with temperature
D) Both are straight lines through the origin; the 400 K line is less steep, because the relaxation time and so the mobility are smaller
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) Both are straight lines through the origin; the 400 K line is less steep, because the relaxation time and so the mobility are smaller.
Given: \(v_d\ \text{against}\ E\ \text{for copper at}\ 300\ \mathrm K\ \text{and}\ 400\ \mathrm K\)
Formula/Principle: \(v_d=\dfrac{e\tau}mE=\mu E,\quad\mu=\dfrac{e\tau}m\)
Step 1: \(v_d\propto E\Rightarrow\text{straight line through the origin, slope}=\mu\)
Step 2: \(T\ \text{higher}\Rightarrow\text{more frequent collisions with the vibrating ions}\Rightarrow\tau\ \text{smaller}\)
Step 3: \(\mu(400\ \mathrm K)<\mu(300\ \mathrm K)\)
Worked derivation: \[\text{slope}=\mu=\frac{e\tau}m:\qquad\text{slope}_{400\ \mathrm K}<\text{slope}_{300\ \mathrm K}\]
Check: \[\rho=\frac m{ne^2\tau}:\ \tau\ \text{smaller}\Rightarrow\rho\ \text{larger, as observed for metals on heating}\]
Why: \(\text{for a metal}\ n\ \text{is fixed; the temperature acts through}\ \tau\)
Why not the others: (A) \(\text{thermal speed is random and gives no drift;}\ \tau\ \text{falls, so the slope falls}\); (B) \(\text{Ohm's law holds for a metal at small fields:}\ v_d\propto E\); (C) \(n\ \text{is unchanged, but}\ \tau\ \text{changes, so the slope changes}\).
Key point: \(\text{mobility}\ \mu=\dfrac{v_d}E=\dfrac{e\tau}m\)

sub_topic: Field at the centre of a coil when the same wire is rewound into two turns
Q: A wire bent into a single circular loop carries a current \(I\) and produces a magnetic field \(B\) at its centre. The same wire is rewound into a circular coil of two turns and carries the same current. What is the field at the centre now?
A) \(2B\)
B) \(4B\)
C) \(\dfrac B2\)
D) \(8B\)
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) \(4B\).
Given: \(\text{length of wire}\ L=2\pi R;\quad N'=2\)
Formula/Principle: \(B=\dfrac{\mu_0NI}{2R}\ \text{(centre of a circular coil, from the Biot–Savart law)}\)
Step 1: \(2\times2\pi R'=2\pi R\Rightarrow R'=\dfrac R2\)
Step 2: \(B'=\dfrac{\mu_0(2)I}{2(R/2)}=\dfrac{2\mu_0I}R\)
Worked derivation: \[\frac{B'}B=\frac{2\mu_0I/R}{\mu_0I/2R}=4\]
Check: \[N\ \text{turns from the same wire:}\ R'=\frac RN\Rightarrow B'=N^2B=2^2B\]
Why: \(\text{the number of turns doubles and the radius is halved; each change doubles the field}\)
Why not the others: (A) \(2B\ \text{counts the turns but not the smaller radius}\); (C) \(\tfrac B2\ \text{inverts the dependence on radius and ignores the turns}\); (D) \(8B=N^3B\).
Key point: \(\text{same wire, same current:}\ B\propto N^2\)

sub_topic: Displacement current in a capacitor being charged
Q: A parallel plate capacitor of capacitance 2.0 nF is being charged so that the potential difference between its plates rises at \(5.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}\). The field between the plates is uniform. What are the displacement current through the whole area between the plates and through a central area equal to half the plate area?
A) 10 mA and 10 mA
B) Zero and zero, because no charge crosses the gap
C) 10 mA and 5.0 mA
D) 20 mA and 10 mA
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) 10 mA and 5.0 mA.
Given: \(C=2.0\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm F,\quad\dfrac{dV}{dt}=5.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}\)
Formula/Principle: \(I_d=\varepsilon_0\dfrac{d\Phi_E}{dt};\quad\Phi_E=EA=\dfrac q{\varepsilon_0}\Rightarrow I_d=\dfrac{dq}{dt}=C\dfrac{dV}{dt}\)
Step 1: \(I_d=2.0\times10^{-9}\times5.0\times10^6=1.0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm A=10\ \mathrm{mA}\)
Step 2: \(\text{uniform field}:\ \Phi_E\propto\text{area}\Rightarrow I_d\left(\tfrac A2\right)=\tfrac12I_d\)
Worked derivation: \[I_d(A)=10\ \mathrm{mA},\qquad I_d\left(\tfrac A2\right)=5.0\ \mathrm{mA}\]
Check: \[\text{conduction current in the wires}=\frac{dq}{dt}=C\frac{dV}{dt}=10\ \mathrm{mA}=I_d\ \ (\text{continuity of total current})\]
Why: \(\text{a changing electric flux acts as a current even where no charge flows}\)
Why not the others: (A) \(\text{half the area encloses half the flux}\); (B) \(I_d\ \text{is due to changing flux, not to moving charge}\); (D) \(C\tfrac{dV}{dt}=10\ \mathrm{mA},\ \text{not}\ 20\ \mathrm{mA}\).
Key point: \(I_d=\varepsilon_0\dfrac{d\Phi_E}{dt}\ \text{equals the charging current}\)

sub_topic: Motion of the real image in a concave mirror as the object approaches the focus
Q: A small object moves along the principal axis of a concave mirror at constant speed, from a point far beyond the centre of curvature C towards the focus F. Which statement about its real image is correct?
A) The image moves towards the mirror and is always slower than the object
B) The image moves away from the mirror at the same speed as the object throughout
C) The image moves away from the mirror; it is faster than the object while the object is beyond C and slower when the object is between C and F
D) The image moves away from the mirror; it is slower than the object while the object is beyond C, has the same speed when the object is at C, and is faster when the object is between C and F
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) The image moves away from the mirror; it is slower than the object while the object is beyond C, has the same speed when the object is at C, and is faster when the object is between C and F.
Given: \(\text{object on the axis, speed}\ v_o,\ \text{moving from beyond}\ C\ \text{to}\ F\)
Formula/Principle: \(\dfrac1v+\dfrac1u=\dfrac1f\Rightarrow-\dfrac1{v^2}\dfrac{dv}{dt}-\dfrac1{u^2}\dfrac{du}{dt}=0\)
Step 1: \(\dfrac{dv}{dt}=-\dfrac{v^2}{u^2}\dfrac{du}{dt}=-m^2\dfrac{du}{dt}\)
Step 2: \(\text{object and image move in opposite directions along the axis:}\ \text{object towards the mirror}\Rightarrow\text{image away from it}\)
Step 3: \(|u|>2f:\ |m|<1;\quad|u|=2f:\ |m|=1;\quad f<|u|<2f:\ |m|>1\)
Worked derivation: \[v_{\text{image}}=m^2v_o:\quad<v_o\ \text{beyond}\ C,\quad=v_o\ \text{at}\ C,\quad>v_o\ \text{between}\ C\ \text{and}\ F\]
Check: \[f=10\ \mathrm{cm}:\ |u|=30\ \mathrm{cm}\Rightarrow|v|=15\ \mathrm{cm},\ m^2=\tfrac14;\quad|u|=15\ \mathrm{cm}\Rightarrow|v|=30\ \mathrm{cm},\ m^2=4\]
Why: \(\text{the image travels from}\ F\ \text{to}\ C\ \text{while the object comes in from infinity to}\ C,\ \text{and from}\ C\ \text{to infinity while the object goes from}\ C\ \text{to}\ F\)
Why not the others: (A) \(\text{a real image moves opposite to the object, so away from the mirror}\); (B) \(m^2=1\ \text{only when the object is at}\ C\); (C) \(\text{the two regions are interchanged}\).
Key point: \(\text{axial speed of image}=m^2\times\text{speed of object}\)

sub_topic: What large-angle scattering of alpha particles shows, and its dependence on Z
Q: In a Geiger–Marsden type experiment with a thin gold foil (Z = 79), most alpha particles pass almost undeflected, and about 1 in 8000 is deflected by more than 90°. The gold foil is replaced by a silver foil (Z = 47) with the same number of atoms per unit area, and the same beam is used. Which conclusion from the gold result, with the prediction for silver, is correct?
A) The positive charge is spread over the whole atom; more alpha particles are deflected by more than 90° with silver
B) The positive charge and most of the mass are in a very small nucleus; fewer alpha particles are deflected by more than 90° with silver
C) The alpha particles are turned back by collisions with electrons; the number deflected by more than 90° does not change
D) The positive charge and most of the mass are in a very small nucleus; more alpha particles are deflected by more than 90° with silver
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) The positive charge and most of the mass are in a very small nucleus; fewer alpha particles are deflected by more than 90° with silver.
Given: \(\text{gold}:\ Z=79,\ \text{about}\ 1\ \text{in}\ 8000\ \text{deflected by more than}\ 90^\circ;\quad\text{silver}:\ Z=47,\ \text{same atoms per unit area}\)
Formula/Principle: \(F=\dfrac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{(2e)(Ze)}{r^2};\quad\text{head-on closest approach}\ d=\dfrac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0K}\)
Step 1: \(\text{most pass undeflected}\Rightarrow\text{most of the atom is empty space}\)
Step 2: \(\text{a few turn back}\Rightarrow\text{a large force at very small}\ r\Rightarrow\text{charge and mass concentrated in a tiny nucleus}\)
Step 3: \(\text{a deflection of more than}\ 90^\circ\ \text{needs an approach to within about}\ d\ \text{of the nucleus; target area per nucleus}\propto d^2\propto Z^2\)
Worked derivation: \[\frac{N_{\text{Ag}}}{N_{\text{Au}}}\approx\left(\frac{47}{79}\right)^2\approx0.35<1\]
Check: \[\frac{d_{\text{Ag}}}{d_{\text{Au}}}=\frac{47}{79}\approx0.59;\qquad0.59^2\approx0.35\]
Why: \(\text{a smaller nuclear charge repels less, so the alpha particle must pass closer to be turned back, and fewer do}\)
Why not the others: (A) \(\text{a spread-out charge gives only small deflections, so}\ 1\ \text{in}\ 8000\ \text{turning back could not occur}\); (C) \(m_e\approx\tfrac{m_\alpha}{7300}:\ \text{an electron cannot turn an alpha particle back}\); (D) \(\text{the force is proportional to}\ Z,\ \text{so a smaller}\ Z\ \text{gives fewer large deflections, not more}\).
Key point: \(\text{large-angle scattering}\Rightarrow\text{nucleus; its amount increases with}\ Z\)

sub_topic: Radius and density of two nuclei of different mass number
Q: The nuclei \(^{27}\mathrm{Al}\) and \(^{125}\mathrm{Te}\) are compared. What are the ratio of their radii and the ratio of their nuclear densities?
A) 3 : 5 and 1 : 1
B) 27 : 125 and 1 : 1
C) 3 : 5 and 27 : 125
D) 9 : 25 and 5 : 3
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) 3 : 5 and 1 : 1.
Given: \(A_1=27,\quad A_2=125\)
Formula/Principle: \(R=R_0A^{1/3};\quad\rho=\dfrac{Am_N}{\tfrac43\pi R^3}\)
Step 1: \(\dfrac{R_1}{R_2}=\left(\dfrac{27}{125}\right)^{1/3}=\dfrac35\)
Step 2: \(\rho=\dfrac{Am_N}{\tfrac43\pi R_0^3A}=\dfrac{3m_N}{4\pi R_0^3}\ \ (\text{independent of}\ A)\)
Worked derivation: \[R_1:R_2=3:5,\qquad\rho_1:\rho_2=1:1\]
Check: \[\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\left(\frac35\right)^3=\frac{27}{125}=\frac{A_1}{A_2}\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}}\ \text{is the same}\]
Why: \(\text{the volume of a nucleus is proportional to the number of nucleons, because the nuclear force is short-ranged and saturates}\)
Why not the others: (B) \(27:125\ \text{is the ratio of volumes, not of radii}\); (C) \(\text{density does not depend on}\ A\); (D) \(9:25\ \text{is the ratio of surface areas}\).
Key point: \(R=R_0A^{1/3}\Rightarrow\text{nuclear density is the same for all nuclei}\)
