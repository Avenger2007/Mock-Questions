sub_topic: Counting ATP used, K+ taken in and net charge moved by the sodium–potassium pump, and the polarity of the resting and depolarised membrane
Q: In a resting neuron, the sodium–potassium pump moves 900 Na⁺ ions out of the cell in a short interval. How many ATP molecules are used, how many K⁺ ions are brought in, and what is the net number of positive charges moved out? Which statement about membrane polarity is also correct?
A) 900 ATP; 900 K⁺; no net charge; the inside of a resting membrane is positive
B) 450 ATP; 300 K⁺; 600 charges; during an impulse, K⁺ rushes in first
C) 300 ATP; 600 K⁺; 300 positive charges out; at rest the outer surface is positive and the inner surface negative (polarised), and at the site of an impulse a rapid influx of Na⁺ makes the inner surface positive (depolarised)
D) 300 ATP; 200 K⁺; 700 charges; the resting membrane has no potential difference
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) 300 ATP, 600 K⁺ in, 300 net positive charges out; polarised at rest, depolarised by Na⁺ influx.
Concept: The axonal membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺ at rest, and the sodium–potassium pump transports 3 Na⁺ outwards for every 2 K⁺ into the cell, using one ATP per cycle. As a result, the outer surface carries a positive charge and the inner surface becomes negative, so the membrane is polarised; the potential difference across it is the resting potential. When a stimulus is applied, the membrane becomes freely permeable to Na⁺, which rushes in, reversing the polarity (positive inside), and this is the action potential or nerve impulse.
Given: Na⁺ pumped out = 900.
Formula/Principle: Each pump cycle: 1 ATP, 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, so net 1 positive charge out.
Step 1: Cycles = 900 ÷ 3 = 300, so 300 ATP are used.
Step 2: K⁺ in = 2 × 300 = 600; net charge out = 900 − 600 = 300 positive charges.
Why: The unequal exchange (3 out, 2 in) moves positive charge outwards, helping to keep the outside positive. During an impulse, Na⁺ enters through channels down its gradient, and the pump later restores the gradients.
Why not the others: (A) Assumes a 1 : 1 exchange; the resting inside is negative. (B) 450 cycles would move 1350 Na⁺; during an impulse, Na⁺ enters first. (D) 200 K⁺ is the count for 100 cycles, and the resting membrane does have a potential difference.
Key point: Pump: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in per ATP; rest = outside positive; impulse = Na⁺ influx, inside positive.

Q: In dihybrid crosses with Drosophila, Morgan found that genes for yellow body and white eye produced only 1.3 % recombinant offspring, while genes for white eye and miniature wing produced 37.2 % recombinants. All three genes lie on the X chromosome. Which explanation is correct?
A) The genes are linked on one chromosome, so they tend to be inherited together; crossing over between homologous chromosomes is less likely between genes that are close together, so tightly linked genes (yellow body and white eye) show few recombinants and loosely linked genes show more; Sturtevant used the frequency of recombination as a measure of the distance between genes to map their positions
B) The yellow-body and white-eye genes are on different chromosomes and assort independently, which reduces recombination
C) Recombination frequency increases as genes come closer together
D) Recombinant offspring arise from mutation, not crossing over, so their frequency cannot be used to map genes
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) Close genes recombine rarely; recombination frequency measures genetic distance.
Concept: The chromosomal theory of inheritance (Sutton and Boveri) holds that genes are located on chromosomes, which pair and segregate during meiosis in the same way as Mendel's factors. Morgan coined the term linkage for the physical association of genes on a chromosome and recombination for the generation of non-parental gene combinations. Linked genes deviate from the 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio expected for independent assortment.
Why: Crossing over can happen anywhere along the chromosome, so the farther apart two genes are, the more likely a crossover falls between them and separates their alleles. A low frequency (1.3 %) therefore indicates tight linkage, and a high one (37.2 %) loose linkage. Because the frequencies reflect distances, they can be used to place genes in order along a chromosome.
Why not the others: (B) Genes on different chromosomes give 50 % recombinants by independent assortment; these genes are all on the X chromosome. (C) It is the reverse: closer genes give fewer recombinants. (D) Recombinants arise by crossing over in meiosis, which is why their frequency can be used for mapping.
Key point: Linkage reduces recombination; lower recombination frequency means genes are closer together.

sub_topic: What insertions of one, two and three nucleotides show about the triplet, non-overlapping and comma-less nature of the genetic code
Q: In a bacterial gene, the following changes are made near the start of the coding sequence: inserting one nucleotide, or two nucleotides, gives a completely non-functional protein; inserting three nucleotides gives a functional protein with one extra amino acid; inserting one nucleotide and deleting one nucleotide a few bases later gives a protein that differs only in the few amino acids between the two changes. What do these observations show?
A) Each amino acid is coded by a single nucleotide, and insertions only change one amino acid
B) The code is read in triplets from a fixed starting point without any punctuation between codons, so inserting or deleting one or two nucleotides shifts the reading frame (frameshift) and changes every codon downstream, while inserting three nucleotides adds one codon and leaves the reading frame intact
C) The code is overlapping, so each nucleotide belongs to three codons
D) Insertions affect proteins only if they occur inside the stop codon
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) The triplet, comma-less code is read from a fixed start, so 1- or 2-base changes cause frameshifts while 3-base insertions add one amino acid.
Concept: The genetic code is a triplet code (each codon has three nucleotides), it is read in a contiguous fashion without punctuation, and it is non-overlapping. Insertion or deletion of one or two bases changes the reading frame from the point of change; these are called frameshift insertion or deletion mutations. Inserting or deleting three or multiples of three bases inserts or deletes one or multiple codons, so one or more amino acids are added or lost, and the reading frame is unaltered from that point onwards.
Why: Think of the sentence "RAM HAS RED CAP" read three letters at a time. Inserting one letter after RAM gives "RAM HAS BRE DCA P…", in which every later word is wrong. Inserting three letters adds one new word, and the rest of the sentence reads correctly. An insertion followed by a nearby deletion restores the frame after the deletion, so only the codons in between are altered.
Why not the others: (A) A one-nucleotide code could specify only four amino acids, and a single insertion changes all downstream codons. (C) An overlapping code would make single changes affect neighbouring codons in a different pattern; it is non-overlapping. (D) The changes were made near the start of the coding sequence, not in the stop codon.
Key point: Triplet, non-overlapping, comma-less code: ±1 or ±2 bases = frameshift; ±3 bases = one codon added or lost.
