sub_topic: Explaining why meiosis halves the chromosome number and generates variation while mitosis keeps it constant, by comparing synapsis, crossing over and the separation of homologues and chromatids
Q: A student compares mitosis in onion root tip cells with meiosis in anther cells. Which explanation of the key mechanistic differences is correct?
A) In mitosis each chromosome's sister chromatids separate at anaphase, giving two daughter cells genetically identical to the parent with the same chromosome number; in meiosis I homologous chromosomes pair (synapsis, forming bivalents at zygotene), exchange segments by crossing over at pachytene (recombinase enzyme, chiasmata visible at diplotene), and the homologues — not the chromatids — separate at anaphase I, halving the chromosome number; meiosis II then separates sister chromatids, like mitosis, giving four haploid cells; the reduction division conserves the chromosome number across generations in sexually reproducing organisms, and crossing over plus independent assortment of bivalents increase genetic variability
B) Meiosis is a single division producing two diploid cells; crossing over occurs during mitotic prophase
C) In anaphase I sister chromatids separate, so meiosis I is equational
D) Mitosis halves the chromosome number; meiosis keeps it constant and produces identical cells
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) Mitosis separates sister chromatids (equational); meiosis I separates homologues after synapsis and crossing over (reductional); meiosis II is equational.
Concept: Mitosis is the most dramatic period of the cell cycle; since the number of chromosomes in the parent and progeny cells is the same, it is also called equational division. In anaphase of mitosis, each chromosome arranged at the metaphase plate is split simultaneously and the two daughter chromatids, now referred to as chromosomes of the future daughter nuclei, begin their migration towards the two opposite poles. Mitosis usually results in the production of diploid daughter cells with identical genetic complement. The production of offspring by sexual reproduction includes the fusion of two gametes, each with a complete haploid set of chromosomes; gametes are formed from specialised diploid cells, and this specialised kind of cell division that reduces the chromosome number by half results in the production of haploid daughter cells; this kind of division is called meiosis. Meiosis involves two sequential cycles of nuclear and cell division called meiosis I and meiosis II but only a single cycle of DNA replication; meiosis I is initiated after the parental chromosomes have replicated to produce identical sister chromatids at the S phase. Meiosis involves pairing of homologous chromosomes and recombination between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes; four haploid cells are formed at the end of meiosis II. During zygotene of prophase I chromosomes start pairing together, a process called synapsis, accompanied by the formation of the synaptonemal complex; the complex formed by a pair of synapsed homologous chromosomes is called a bivalent or a tetrad. Pachytene is characterised by the appearance of recombination nodules, the sites at which crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids of the homologous chromosomes; crossing over is the exchange of genetic material between two homologous chromosomes and is an enzyme-mediated process, the enzyme involved being called recombinase. In diplotene the X-shaped chiasmata become visible. In anaphase I, the homologous chromosomes separate, while sister chromatids remain associated at their centromeres. Meiosis II resembles a normal mitosis; at anaphase II the centromere of each chromosome splits, allowing the sister chromatids to move toward opposite poles. Meiosis is the mechanism by which conservation of specific chromosome number of each species is achieved across generations in sexually reproducing organisms, even though the process, per se, paradoxically, results in reduction of chromosome number by half; it also increases the genetic variability in the population of organisms from one generation to the next.
Why: Separating homologues rather than chromatids is what halves the number, and pairing with exchange is what creates new combinations.
Why not the others: (B) Meiosis has two divisions producing four haploid cells; crossing over is in meiotic prophase I. (C) Homologues separate in anaphase I, so meiosis I is reductional. (D) The roles are reversed.
Key point: Mitosis: chromatids separate (2n → 2n); meiosis I: homologues separate (2n → n); meiosis II: chromatids separate.

A chromatographic separation of the leaf pigments shows that the colour that we see in leaves is not due to a single pigment but due to four pigments: chlorophyll a (bright or blue green in the chromatograph), chlorophyll b (yellow green), xanthophylls (yellow) and carotenoids (yellow to yellow-orange). Pigments are substances that have an ability to absorb light at specific wavelengths; chlorophyll a is the chief pigment associated with photosynthesis, and the wavelengths at which there is maximum absorption by chlorophyll a, i.e., in the blue and the red regions, also show a higher rate of photosynthesis. Though chlorophyll is the major pigment responsible for trapping light, other thylakoid pigments like chlorophyll b, xanthophylls and carotenoids, which are called accessory pigments, also absorb light and transfer the energy to chlorophyll a; indeed, they not only enable a wider range of wavelength of incoming light to be utilised for photosynthesis but also protect chlorophyll a from photo-oxidation. The pigments are organised into two discrete photochemical light harvesting complexes (LHC) within Photosystem I and Photosystem II; each photosystem has all the pigments (except one molecule of chlorophyll a) forming a light harvesting system also called antennae, and the reaction centre is formed by a single chlorophyll a molecule. In the chloroplast, the membrane system is responsible for trapping the light energy and also for the synthesis of ATP and NADPH; in the stroma, enzymatic reactions synthesise sugar, which in turn forms starch. The former set of reactions, since they are directly light driven, are called light reactions (photochemical reactions); the latter are not directly light driven but are dependent on the products of light reactions (ATP and NADPH), hence to distinguish them they are called, by convention, dark reactions (carbon reactions).
Why: Energy flows from many antenna pigments to one reaction-centre chlorophyll a, and products of the membrane reactions feed the stromal reactions.

sub_topic: Calculating respiratory quotients for carbohydrate, fat and organic acid substrates from balanced equations and interpreting RQ data, and explaining why respiration is called an amphibolic pathway
Q: Germinating seeds of three species gave these readings: seed X released 6 mL CO₂ for 6 mL O₂ used; seed Y released 102 mL CO₂ for 145 mL O₂; seed Z released 4 mL CO₂ for 3 mL O₂. Which interpretation is correct?
A) All three respire only carbohydrate, since RQ is always 1
B) RQ of X = 1.0 (carbohydrate, e.g. C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O), Y ≈ 0.7 (fat, e.g. tripalmitin 2C₅₁H₉₈O₆ + 145O₂ → 102CO₂ + 98H₂O), Z ≈ 1.33 (organic acid such as malic acid); respiration is amphibolic because it is involved both in breakdown (catabolism) and synthesis (anabolism) — fats enter as glycerol (via PGAL) and fatty acids (via acetyl CoA), proteins as amino acids at various points in the Krebs cycle, and when fatty acids or other compounds are synthesised the same intermediates (e.g. acetyl CoA) are withdrawn from the pathway
C) RQ of Y is 1.42, showing protein; respiration is purely catabolic
D) RQ of Z is 0.75; Z is respiring fat
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) RQ: X = 1.0 (carbohydrate), Y ≈ 0.70 (fat), Z ≈ 1.33 (organic acid); respiration is amphibolic.
Concept: During aerobic respiration, O₂ is consumed and CO₂ is released; the ratio of the volume of CO₂ evolved to the volume of O₂ consumed in respiration is called the respiratory quotient (RQ) or respiratory ratio: RQ = volume of CO₂ evolved/volume of O₂ consumed. The respiratory quotient depends upon the type of respiratory substrate used during respiration. When carbohydrates are used as substrate and are completely oxidised, the RQ will be 1, because equal amounts of CO₂ and O₂ are evolved and consumed, respectively: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy, RQ = 6CO₂/6O₂ = 1.0. When fats are used in respiration the RQ is less than 1: for the fatty acid tripalmitin, 2(C₅₁H₉₈O₆) + 145O₂ → 102CO₂ + 98H₂O + energy, RQ = 102CO₂/145O₂ = 0.7. When proteins are respiratory substrates the ratio would be about 0.9. What is important to recognise is that in living organisms respiratory substrates are often more than one; pure proteins or fats are never used as respiratory substrates. Glucose is the favoured substrate for respiration, and all carbohydrates are usually first converted into glucose before they are used for respiration; other substrates can also be respired, but then they do not enter the respiratory pathway at the first step. Fats would need to be broken down into glycerol and fatty acids first; if fatty acids were to be respired they would first be degraded to acetyl CoA and enter the pathway; glycerol would enter the pathway after being converted to PGAL; the proteins would be degraded by proteases and the individual amino acids (after deamination), depending on their structure, would enter the pathway at some stage within the Krebs cycle or even as pyruvate or acetyl CoA. Since respiration involves breakdown of substrate, the respiratory process has traditionally been considered a catabolic process and the respiratory pathway a catabolic pathway; but when the organism needs to synthesise fatty acids, acetyl CoA would be withdrawn from the respiratory pathway for it; hence the respiratory pathway comes into the picture both during breakdown and synthesis of fatty acids, and similarly during breakdown and synthesis of protein too; because the respiratory pathway is involved in both anabolism and catabolism, it would hence be better to consider it as an amphibolic pathway rather than a catabolic one. Organic acids such as malic acid (C₄H₆O₅ + 3O₂ → 4CO₂ + 3H₂O) are oxygen-rich and give RQ greater than 1.
Given: X: 6/6; Y: 102/145; Z: 4/3.
Formula/Principle: RQ = volume CO₂ evolved/volume O₂ consumed.
Step 1: RQ(X) = 6/6 = 1.0 ⇒ carbohydrate.
Step 2: RQ(Y) = 102/145 = 0.70 ⇒ fat.
Step 3: RQ(Z) = 4/3 = 1.33 ⇒ organic acid (oxygen-rich substrate).
Why: Oxygen-poor substrates (fats) need more O₂ per CO₂, while oxygen-rich ones (organic acids) need less.
Why not the others: (A) RQ depends on substrate. (C) 102/145 ≈ 0.70, typical of fat; respiration is amphibolic. (D) 4/3 > 1, not a fat.
Key point: RQ: carbohydrate 1, fat ≈ 0.7, protein ≈ 0.9, organic acids > 1; respiration is amphibolic.

Q: In Drosophila, a dihybrid female heterozygous for two linked genes (parental arrangement AB/ab) is test-crossed with ab/ab. The progeny are: AB 450, ab 450, Ab 50, aB 50. Which interpretation is correct?
A) The genes assort independently, since all four classes appear
B) Recombination frequency = 50%; the genes are on different chromosomes
C) Recombination frequency = 100/1000 × 100 = 10%; the genes are 10 map units (centimorgans) apart; Morgan found that genes on the X chromosome did not segregate independently and that parental combinations were much more frequent than non-parental ones — for example, about 1.3% recombination between white eye and yellow body but 37.2% between white eye and miniature wing, so tightly linked genes show low recombination; Sturtevant used recombination frequency as a measure of distance between genes to map their positions on the chromosome, supporting the chromosome theory of inheritance proposed by Sutton and Boveri
D) Recombination frequency = 90%; the genes are very far apart
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) RF = 10%, map distance 10 cM; Morgan's linkage data and Sturtevant's mapping correctly described.
Concept: Sutton and Boveri argued that the pairing and separation of a pair of chromosomes would lead to the segregation of a pair of factors they carried; Sutton united the knowledge of chromosomal segregation with Mendelian principles and called it the chromosomal theory of inheritance. Experimental verification of the chromosomal theory of inheritance by Thomas Hunt Morgan and his colleagues led to discovering the basis for the variation that sexual reproduction produced; Morgan worked with tiny fruit flies, Drosophila melanogaster, which were found very suitable for such studies — they could be grown on simple synthetic medium in the laboratory, complete their life cycle in about two weeks, a single mating could produce a large number of progeny flies, there was a clear differentiation of the sexes, and they have many types of hereditary variations that can be seen with low power microscopes. Morgan carried out several dihybrid crosses in Drosophila to study genes that were sex-linked; the crosses were similar to the dihybrid crosses carried out by Mendel in peas — for example, Morgan hybridised yellow-bodied, white-eyed females to brown-bodied, red-eyed males and intercrossed their F1 progeny; he observed that the two genes did not segregate independently of each other and the F2 ratio deviated very significantly from the 9:3:3:1 ratio. Morgan and his group knew that the genes were located on the X chromosome and saw quickly that when the two genes in a dihybrid cross were situated on the same chromosome, the proportion of parental gene combinations was much higher than the non-parental type; Morgan attributed this to the physical association or linkage of the two genes and coined the term linkage to describe this physical association of genes on a chromosome and the term recombination to describe the generation of non-parental gene combinations. Morgan and his group also found that even when genes were grouped on the same chromosome, some genes were very tightly linked (showed very low recombination) while others were loosely linked (showed higher recombination); for example, he found that the genes white and yellow were very tightly linked and showed only 1.3 per cent recombination while white and miniature wing showed 37.2 per cent recombination. His student Alfred Sturtevant used the frequency of recombination between gene pairs on the same chromosome as a measure of the distance between genes and 'mapped' their position on the chromosome.
Given: Parental 450 + 450 = 900; recombinant 50 + 50 = 100; total 1000.
Formula/Principle: RF (%) = recombinants/total × 100; 1% RF = 1 map unit (cM).
Step 1: Recombinants = 100.
Step 2: RF = 100/1000 × 100 = 10%.
Step 3: Map distance = 10 cM.
Why: Recombinants arise only by crossing over between the two loci, which is more likely the farther apart they are.
Why not the others: (A) Independent assortment gives a 1:1:1:1 ratio, not 9:9:1:1. (B) 50% is the maximum for unlinked genes; here only 10%. (D) 90% are parental, not recombinant.
Key point: RF = recombinants/total; low RF ⇒ tight linkage; RF used as map distance.

KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) Substitution changes one codon; 1–2 base insertions/deletions cause frameshifts; 3 bases change one amino acid; features of the code correct.
Concept: George Gamow argued that since there are only 4 bases and if they have to code for 20 amino acids, the code should constitute a combination of bases; he suggested that in order to code for all the 20 amino acids, the code should be made up of three nucleotides; this was a very bold proposition, because a permutation combination of 4³ (4 × 4 × 4) would generate 64 codons, generating many more codons than required. The salient features of the genetic code are as follows: (i) the codon is triplet — 61 codons code for amino acids and 3 codons do not code for any amino acids, hence they function as stop codons; (ii) some amino acids are coded by more than one codon, hence the code is degenerate; (iii) the codon is read in mRNA in a contiguous fashion — there are no punctuations; (iv) the code is nearly universal — for example, from bacteria to human UUU would code for phenylalanine (phe), with some exceptions in mitochondrial codons and in some protozoans; (v) one codon codes for only one amino acid, hence it is unambiguous and specific; (vi) AUG has dual functions — it codes for methionine (met), and it also acts as the initiator codon. The relationship between genes and DNA is best understood by mutation studies; effects of large deletions and rearrangements in a segment of DNA are easy to comprehend, as it may result in loss or gain of a gene and so a function. The effect of point mutations will be explained here; a classical example of point mutation is a change of a single base pair in the gene for beta globin chain that results in the change of amino acid residue glutamate to valine, resulting in a diseased condition called sickle cell anaemia. Effect of point mutations that inserts or deletes a base in the structural gene can be better understood by the following simple example: consider a statement RAM HAS RED CAP; if we insert a letter B in between HAS and RED and rearrange the statement, it would read RAM HAS BRE DCA P; similarly, if we now insert two letters at the same place, say BI, it would read RAM HAS BIR EDC AP; now we insert three letters together, say BIG, the statement would read RAM HAS BIG RED CAP; the same exercise can be repeated by deleting the letters R, E and D one by one and rearranging the statement to make a triplet word: RAM HAS EDC AP, RAM HAS DCA P, RAM HAS CAP. The conclusion from the above exercise is very obvious: insertion or deletion of one or two bases changes the reading frame from the point of insertion or deletion, while insertion or deletion of three or its multiple bases inserts or deletes one or multiple codons, hence one or multiple amino acids, and the reading frame remains unaltered from that point onwards; such mutations are referred to as frameshift insertion or deletion mutations.
Why: The code is read in fixed triplets without punctuation, so the effect depends on whether the change keeps the triplet frame.
Why not the others: (A) Effects reversed. (B) 3 of the 64 codons are stop codons. (C) Three-base insertions keep the frame.
Key point: Substitution → one codon changed; ±1 or ±2 bases → frameshift; ±3 → one amino acid added or lost.
