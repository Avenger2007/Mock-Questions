sub_topic: Reading a Hydrilla bubble-count experiment with Blackman's law of limiting factors (light, CO2 and temperature)
Q: A twig of Hydrilla in tap water at 20 °C is lit by a lamp of increasing intensity, and the oxygen bubbles released per minute are counted. Results: at intensities 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 (arbitrary units) the counts are 10, 20, 30, 31 and 31. At intensity 5, adding a pinch of sodium bicarbonate raises the count to 52. At intensity 1, warming the water to 30 °C leaves the count at 10. Which interpretation is correct?
A) At low intensities light is the limiting factor, so the rate rises in proportion to light; above intensity 3 the rate levels off because another factor, CO₂, has become limiting, which is why bicarbonate (a CO₂ source) raises it; warming has no effect at intensity 1 because light, not temperature, is the factor in shortest supply there — as Blackman's law states, the rate is set by the factor nearest its minimal value
B) Light becomes harmful above intensity 3, and bicarbonate protects the chloroplasts from damage
C) Temperature is the main limiting factor at all light intensities, so warming should have raised the count at intensity 1
D) The levelling off shows that all the chlorophyll has been destroyed, and bicarbonate makes new chlorophyll
KEY: A
SOL:
Answer: (A) Light limits at low intensity, CO₂ limits at high intensity, and temperature cannot act while light is limiting.
Concept: Photosynthesis is affected by several external factors (light, CO₂ concentration, temperature, water) and internal ones. Blackman's (1905) law of limiting factors states that if a chemical process is affected by more than one factor, its rate is determined by the factor nearest its minimal value; it is the factor that directly affects the process if its quantity is changed. Light saturation occurs at about 10 % of full sunlight, and above that other factors become limiting. CO₂ is the major limiting factor in nature because its concentration in air is very low (about 0.03–0.04 %).
Why: Between intensities 1 and 3 each step of light raises the count by the same amount, so light is limiting. From 3 to 5 the count barely changes: light is no longer limiting, and supplying extra CO₂ (bicarbonate) raises the rate sharply, showing that CO₂ was now the limiting factor. The dark (biosynthetic) reactions are enzyme-controlled and temperature-sensitive, but at intensity 1 the supply of ATP and NADPH from the light reaction is too low, so warming cannot speed things up.
Why not the others: (B) The rate did not fall, so there is no sign of damage, and bicarbonate is a carbon source, not a protectant. (C) The unchanged count at 30 °C shows temperature was not limiting at low light. (D) Destroyed chlorophyll could not respond to bicarbonate by increasing the rate.
Key point: Only the factor in shortest supply controls the rate; raising any other factor has no effect until that one is relieved.

Q: In one week, leaf P grows from 4 cm² to 8 cm² and leaf Q from 40 cm² to 44 cm². Which statement about their growth, and about the types of growth in plants, is correct?
A) Both have the same relative growth rate because both added 4 cm²
B) Q has the higher relative growth rate because it is larger; arithmetic growth is described by \(W_1=W_0e^{rt}\)
C) Both show the same absolute growth (4 cm² per week), but the relative growth rate of P is 100 % per week and that of Q only 10 % per week; in arithmetic growth only one daughter cell keeps dividing and length rises linearly (\(L_t=L_0+rt\)), whereas in geometric growth both progeny cells keep dividing, giving exponential growth (\(W_1=W_0e^{rt}\)) that slows into a sigmoid curve as nutrients become limited
D) P has a higher absolute growth rate; geometric growth gives a straight line when size is plotted against time
KEY: C
SOL:
Answer: (C) Equal absolute growth, relative growth rate 100 % (P) and 10 % (Q); linear arithmetic and exponential geometric growth.
Concept: Growth can be measured as absolute growth rate (growth per unit time) or relative growth rate (growth per unit time per unit initial size). In arithmetic growth, after mitotic division only one daughter cell continues to divide while the other differentiates and matures, as in an elongating root at constant rate; a plot of length against time is a straight line. In geometric growth, both progeny cells retain the ability to divide; growth is slow at first (lag phase), then rapid and exponential (log phase), and finally slows to a stationary phase as nutrients become limited, giving a sigmoid curve.
Given: P: 4 → 8 cm²; Q: 40 → 44 cm² in one week.
Formula/Principle: Absolute growth = final size − initial size; relative growth rate = absolute growth ÷ initial size.
Step 1: Absolute growth: P = 8 − 4 = 4 cm²; Q = 44 − 40 = 4 cm².
Step 2: Relative growth rate: P = 4/4 = 1 = 100 % per week; Q = 4/40 = 0.1 = 10 % per week.
Why: The two leaves add the same area, but P adds an area equal to its own original size, so its efficiency of growth is ten times that of Q. Relative growth rate is the better measure for comparing systems of different sizes.
Why not the others: (A) Equal absolute growth does not mean equal relative growth. (B) Q's relative rate is lower, and \(W_1=W_0e^{rt}\) describes geometric (exponential) growth. (D) The absolute growths are equal, and the straight line belongs to arithmetic growth.
Key point: Relative growth rate = absolute growth ÷ initial size; arithmetic growth is linear, geometric growth is exponential and then sigmoid.

Key point: Muscles contract → chest volume up → lung volume up → pressure down → air in; relaxation reverses it.

Q: When a new antibiotic is used widely, after some years most infections are caused by resistant bacteria. Which explanation follows Darwin's theory of natural selection?
A) The antibiotic forced each bacterium to change its body during its lifetime, and these acquired changes were inherited, as with the long neck of the giraffe
B) The antibiotic directly caused the needed resistance mutations in bacteria that were in contact with it
C) Large single-step mutations created a new species of bacterium overnight, which is the main idea of Darwin's theory
D) Heritable variation already existed: a few bacteria were resistant before the antibiotic was used; in its presence they survived and reproduced more, leaving more offspring (greater reproductive fitness), so over generations resistant forms came to dominate, just as dark moths increased in industrial England — evolution by natural selection acting on pre-existing variation
KEY: D
SOL:
Answer: (D) Selection favours resistant variants that already existed, which then leave more offspring.
Concept: Darwin, based on observations during the voyage of HMS Beagle, concluded that existing living forms share similarities with one another and with forms that existed millions of years ago. Two key concepts of his theory are branching descent and natural selection. Populations have built-in variation in characteristics; those characteristics that enable some to survive better in natural conditions will outbreed others. Fitness, according to Darwin, ultimately refers to reproductive fitness: those who are better fit in an environment leave more progeny. He was also influenced by Malthus's ideas on populations: populations could grow exponentially, yet their size remains fairly constant, so there must be a struggle for existence.
Why: In nature the antibiotic acts as a selecting agent, killing sensitive bacteria. Resistant variants, which arose earlier by chance, leave more descendants, and the frequency of resistance rises. Similarly, excessive use of herbicides, pesticides and drugs selects resistant varieties in a much shorter time scale; these are examples of evolution by anthropogenic action.
Why not the others: (A) This is Lamarck's theory of use and disuse with inheritance of acquired characters. (B) The environment selects; it does not create the needed mutations. (C) Saltation (single-step large mutation) was Hugo de Vries' idea, not Darwin's gradual selection.
Key point: Natural selection acts on existing heritable variation; fitness means leaving more offspring.

SOL:

Q: A circular plasmid and a linear DNA molecule each contain two sites for EcoRI. After complete digestion, the fragments are separated by agarose gel electrophoresis. How many fragments does each give, and which statement about the gel is correct?
A) 3 fragments from the plasmid and 2 from the linear DNA; DNA moves towards the cathode because it is positively charged
B) 2 fragments from the plasmid and 3 from the linear DNA; DNA moves towards the anode because it is negatively charged; smaller fragments move farther through the agarose matrix (a natural polymer from sea weeds), and the bands are seen as bright orange under UV light after staining with ethidium bromide, then cut out and eluted for use

D) 4 fragments from the plasmid and 3 from the linear DNA; bands are seen in ordinary light without any stain
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) 2 and 3 fragments; DNA moves to the anode, small fragments farthest, seen orange under UV with ethidium bromide.
Concept: The cutting of DNA by restriction endonucleases results in fragments of DNA, which are separated by gel electrophoresis. Since DNA fragments are negatively charged molecules, they can be separated by forcing them to move towards the anode under an electric field through a medium/matrix; the commonly used matrix is agarose, a natural polymer extracted from sea weeds. The fragments separate according to their size through the sieving effect of the agarose gel: the smaller the fragment, the farther it moves. Separated DNA fragments can be visualised only after staining with ethidium bromide followed by exposure to UV radiation, as bright orange bands; the bands are cut out from the gel and extracted (elution).
Given: two EcoRI sites in each molecule.
Formula/Principle: Number of fragments: circular DNA = number of cuts; linear DNA = number of cuts + 1.
Step 1: Plasmid: 2 cuts in a closed ring → 2 fragments.
Step 2: Linear DNA: 2 cuts in a line → 3 fragments.
Why: Cutting a ring once only opens it; each further cut adds one piece. A linear molecule already has two ends, so the first cut already produces two pieces.
Why not the others: (A) Counts are reversed and DNA is negatively charged. (C) Linear DNA gives 3 pieces, and small fragments move faster. (D) Overcounts, and DNA bands are invisible without staining.
Key point: Circular DNA: fragments = cuts; linear: cuts + 1; DNA runs to the anode, small fragments farthest.

sub_topic: Calculating logistic growth rates at a quarter, half and three-quarters of the carrying capacity and finding where growth is fastest
Q: A population follows logistic growth with intrinsic rate of natural increase r = 0.4 per year and carrying capacity K = 1000. What is the growth rate dN/dt (individuals per year) when N = 250, 500 and 750?
A) 100, 200, 300 — growth keeps increasing with N
B) 75, 100, 75 — growth is fastest at N = K/2 and is equal at N = K/4 and N = 3K/4
C) 75, 50, 25 — growth is fastest at the smallest population
D) 0, 100, 0 — growth occurs only at N = K/2
KEY: B
SOL:
Answer: (B) 75, 100 and 75 individuals per year; growth is fastest at K/2.
Concept: No population can grow exponentially for ever, since resources become limited. A population growing in a habitat with limited resources shows an initial lag phase, followed by acceleration and deceleration, and finally an asymptote, when the population density reaches the carrying capacity (K), the maximum number the habitat can support. The plot of N against time is a sigmoid curve, called Verhulst–Pearl logistic growth, described by dN/dt = rN(K − N)/K.
Given: r = 0.4 yr⁻¹; K = 1000; N = 250, 500, 750.
Formula/Principle: dN/dt = rN(K − N)/K.
Step 1: N = 250: 0.4 × 250 × 750/1000 = 0.4 × 250 × 0.75 = 75.
Step 2: N = 500: 0.4 × 500 × 0.5 = 100.
Step 3: N = 750: 0.4 × 750 × 0.25 = 75.
Why: The term N rises as the population grows, while the factor (K − N)/K, the environmental resistance, falls; their product is greatest when N = K/2 and symmetric about it. Since resources are finite, the logistic model is considered more realistic than exponential growth.
Why not the others: (A) Ignores the (K − N)/K factor. (C) At small N there are few breeders, so growth is not fastest there. (D) Growth is positive at all N between 0 and K.
Key point: Logistic growth: dN/dt = rN(K − N)/K, maximum at N = K/2 and zero at N = K.
